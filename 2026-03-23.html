The Wisconsin Recount That Didn't Happen: How Gary Gates' 2004 Lawsuit Almost Changed American Elections

Epistemic status: High confidence on the documented facts of the case. Medium confidence on the counterfactual implications, which inherently involve speculation about alternative histories.

You probably know that recounts happened in Florida 2000 and that various lawsuits challenged aspects of the 2020 election. But there's a lesser-known case from 2004 that came remarkably close to establishing a precedent that could have transformed how American presidential elections are contested.

Let me tell you about Gary Gates and the recount that Wisconsin law said should have happened—but didn't.

The Basic Facts

In the 2004 presidential election, George W. Bush defeated John Kerry in Wisconsin by 11,384 votes out of nearly 3 million cast. That's a margin of 0.38%.

Here's what most people don't know: Wisconsin law at the time required an automatic recount for any statewide race decided by less than 0.5%. According to Wisconsin Statute § 9.01, when the margin falls below this threshold, the state is required to conduct a recount at no cost to the candidates.

Kerry conceded the day after the election. Bush won the Electoral College 286-251. Everyone moved on.

Except Gary Gates didn't move on.

Who Was Gary Gates?

Gates was a Green Bay attorney and Democratic activist who had served as John Kerry's Wisconsin campaign attorney during the 2004 election. As reported by the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, Gates filed a lawsuit on November 18, 2004—two weeks after the election—arguing that Wisconsin's State Elections Board had violated state law by failing to order the mandatory recount.

His argument was straightforward: The statute said "shall conduct a recount" when the margin is under 0.5%. The margin was 0.38%. Therefore, a recount must happen regardless of whether any candidate requested it.

The Wisconsin Elections Board's position was equally clear: No candidate had requested a recount, so no recount would occur.

Why This Mattered More Than It Seemed

At first glance, this looks like a technical dispute over statutory interpretation. But consider what was actually at stake.

If Gates had won, it would have established that certain close elections trigger mandatory recounts regardless of candidate wishes or concessions. The recount would have happened even though Kerry had conceded and even though no one believed it would change the outcome.

This would have created a completely different legal landscape for future elections. Instead of recounts being optional procedures that candidates could waive through concession, they would become automatic institutional safeguards triggered by objective mathematical thresholds.

Let me walk through why this matters.

The Concession Problem

American elections rely heavily on an informal norm: the losing candidate concedes. As I've mentioned in previous newsletters, this "consent of the loser" norm is crucial for democratic legitimacy.

But here's the tension: What happens when that norm breaks down?

In 2020, Trump didn't concede. In 2024, if we imagine a scenario where an election denier loses a close race for secretary of state, they might not concede. The norm assumes good faith actors.

Gates' lawsuit inadvertently highlighted an alternative approach: Make recounts automatic in close races so they don't depend on any candidate's decision.

Under this system, it wouldn't matter whether Trump conceded in 2020 or whether a future candidate concedes in 2028. If the margin hits a certain threshold, the recount happens. No one has the power to waive it through concession.

What the Court Decided

The Wisconsin Court of Appeals ruled against Gates in January 2005. According to the court's decision in Gates v. State Elections Board, the court held that the statute should be read in context with other provisions that gave candidates the right to request recounts.

The court reasoned that the mandatory recount provision was intended to ensure candidates in close races could get recounts without having to pay for them, not to mandate recounts when no candidate wanted one.

As the court wrote: "We conclude that the board's interpretation of the statute as requiring a recount only when a candidate petitions for one is reasonable."

Gates appealed to the Wisconsin Supreme Court, which declined to hear the case in April 2005.

And that was that. The legal question was settled in Wisconsin, and no mandatory recount happened.

The Counterfactual: What If Gates Had Won?

Let's think through what might have been different if Gates had won his case.

First, the immediate 2004 impact: Wisconsin would have conducted a full recount of the presidential election despite Kerry's concession. This would have cost the state an estimated $500,000-$1 million in 2004 dollars, according to contemporary reporting.

The recount almost certainly wouldn't have changed the outcome—Bush's 11,384-vote margin was large enough to survive a recount. But it would have established the precedent that close statewide races must be recounted regardless of candidate preference.

Second, the precedential impact: Other states would have looked at Wisconsin's mandatory recount system as a model. If Wisconsin could require automatic recounts, why couldn't other states?

Consider how this might have changed 2020. If swing states had mandatory recount thresholds:

  • Arizona: Biden won by 10,457 votes (0.3% margin) - would have triggered automatic recount
  • Georgia: Biden won by 11,779 votes (0.24% margin) - would have triggered automatic recount
  • Wisconsin: Biden won by 20,682 votes (0.63% margin) - might not have triggered recount depending on threshold

    • With automatic recounts, no one could claim the process was politically motivated. The recounts would happen because the law required them, not because Trump demanded them. This removes some of the political theatre while maintaining verification of close results.

    Third, the norm-independence advantage: Perhaps most importantly, automatic recounts would have decoupled result verification from candidate behavior. Whether Trump conceded or not, whether he made wild fraud claims or not, the recount would happen anyway.

    This is what I mean by institutional safeguards versus personal courage. We got lucky in 2020 that enough election officials resisted pressure. But as I discussed in my March 16 newsletter about presidential removal powers, relying on individual courage creates statistical vulnerabilities.

    Automatic recounts replace "Will this candidate do the right thing?" with "Does the math say we recount?" That's a much more robust foundation.

    Why Wisconsin Originally Had This Law

    Wisconsin didn't arrive at mandatory recounts by accident. The state has a long progressive tradition of election integrity reforms dating back to the early 1900s.

    According to the Wisconsin Legislative Reference Bureau, the state enacted automatic recount provisions in the 1970s following concerns about close elections in the 1960s. The idea was simple: in truly close races, the margin of error in vote counting might be larger than the victory margin. Better to recount automatically than to rely on candidates to request one.

    The law reflected a worldview where recounts are quality control mechanisms, not political weapons. They're like backup systems on an airplane—you don't wait for someone to request them, you build them into the process.

    What Changed After Gates Lost

    Wisconsin amended its recount law in 2011 and 2016. The current version, Wisconsin Statute § 9.01, still allows recounts but makes them fully candidate-initiated. There's no automatic recount threshold anymore.

    This means Wisconsin went in the opposite direction from Gates' vision. Instead of making recounts more automatic, the state made them more discretionary.

    Other states followed similar paths. As the National Conference of State Legislatures documents, most states now have recount laws that either:

  • Require a candidate to request and sometimes pay for recounts
  • Have automatic recount thresholds that can be waived by the losing candidate
  • Leave recounts entirely to candidate discretion

    • Almost no states have truly automatic, non-waivable recounts.

    The Modern Relevance

    Why does a failed 2004 lawsuit matter in 2026?

    Because we're likely to see extremely close elections in 2028. And the question of whether recounts should be automatic versus optional is going to come up again.

    Consider the different scenarios:

    Scenario A (Current System): A MAGA candidate loses a 2028 secretary of state race in Nevada by 0.3%. They refuse to concede and demand a recount, claiming fraud. The recount becomes a political circus, with each side questioning the other's motives.

    Scenario B (Gates System): Same race, same margin. But Nevada has an automatic recount law with a 0.5% threshold. The recount happens not because anyone demanded it, but because the law requires it. It's procedural, not political.

    Scenario B doesn't eliminate all the problems—election deniers can still make false fraud claims during an automatic recount. But it does eliminate the optics problem of "only candidates who claim fraud get recounts."

    The broader principle: As I've discussed in previous newsletters about certification procedures and election official removal, American election law assumes good faith actors. We rely on norms, individual courage, and informal guardrails.

    Automatic recounts represent a shift toward norm-independent institutional safeguards. They don't assume anyone will do the right thing. They just trigger based on math.

    The Cost-Benefit Question

    The obvious objection to automatic recounts is cost. Wisconsin estimated their 2004 presidential recount would cost $500,000-$1 million. Scale that to multiple states and multiple races, and you're talking tens of millions of dollars per election cycle.

    But compare that cost to the cost of legitimacy crises. According to a Brookings Institution analysis, the January 6 attack caused over $30 million in immediate damages and security costs, not counting the broader economic and political costs of the legitimacy crisis.

    Automatic recounts in 2020's closest races might have cost $20-30 million total across all swing states. That's a rounding error compared to the cost of the crisis that actually happened.

    More importantly, the institutional cost of operating without clear verification procedures is harder to quantify but potentially enormous. If 30-40% of Americans doubt election results (as various polls have shown since 2020), what's the economic cost of that distrust? What policies don't get implemented because governing legitimacy is questioned?

    Research by political scientists suggests that visible, independent verification procedures increase public confidence in election results. Automatic recounts would provide that verification not through partisan fighting but through mathematical triggers.

    Why No One Picked This Up

    Given these advantages, why didn't automatic recounts become standard after 2004?

    A few reasons:

    First, the Gates case was barely noticed. It happened during the post-election transition period when everyone was focused on other things. Kerry had conceded, Bush was preparing to take office for his second term, and a state court case about statutory interpretation in Wisconsin didn't make national news.

    Second, neither party had a clear incentive to push for automatic recounts. Republicans controlled most statehouses in the 2000s and 2010s and saw recounts as favoring Democrats (who generally do better in heavily-populated urban areas where counting irregularities are more common). Democrats were focused on voting access issues, not recount mechanisms.

    Third, the cost objection was politically powerful. In an era of tight state budgets, spending millions on recounts of elections that wouldn't change outcomes was an easy target for criticism.

    Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, we hadn't yet experienced an election where the losing candidate refused to concede and mounted a sustained attack on election legitimacy. In 2004, the risk of a legitimacy crisis seemed theoretical. By 2020, it was reality.

    The Opening for 2026-2028

    Here's why this matters right now: Multiple swing states are considering election law changes before 2028.

    According to Voting Rights Lab's tracking, at least 8 swing states have active legislative discussions about election procedures heading into the 2026 legislative sessions. Some of this is coming from election denial advocates trying to add barriers. But there's also a counter-movement pushing for stronger verification procedures.

    Automatic recounts could be part of that conversation. A state legislator could introduce a bill establishing automatic recounts for statewide races within 0.5% or 1.0%. The bill could include:

  • Clear mathematical triggers that can't be waived
  • Standardized recount procedures to ensure consistency
  • State funding to cover all costs
  • Expedited timelines to meet federal deadlines
  • Transparency requirements (public observation, livestreaming, etc.)

    • This wouldn't be a partisan proposal. Both parties benefit from having clear, pre-established verification procedures that don't depend on candidate decisions.

    The legislative window is narrow—most state legislatures will complete their 2027 sessions by summer 2027, giving about 15 months to pass laws that would govern the 2028 election.

    2. State-Specific Advocacy: Pick 2-3 swing states where automatic recounts could realistically pass in 2027 and build a coalition to push for them. Arizona and Nevada might be especially promising given their recent election controversies and relatively professionalized legislatures.

    4. Cost-Benefit Analysis: Develop a rigorous economic analysis of automatic recount costs versus legitimacy crisis costs. Make the case that spending $20-30 million per cycle on verification is far cheaper than the alternative.

    5. Public Education Campaign: Most voters have no idea that recounts are optional and can be waived through concession. A campaign explaining how automatic recounts would work could build public support that pushes legislators to act.

    The key insight from Gary Gates' failed lawsuit is that American elections don't have to rely entirely on norms and individual integrity. We can build institutional safeguards that trigger automatically, regardless of whether candidates act in good faith.

    We missed that opportunity in 2004. We don't have to miss it again in 2028.

    The people who will administer and certify the 2028 election are being selected right now. And the laws that will govern recounts in 2028 are being written—or not written—in state legislatures over the next 15 months.

    If you want to work on democracy protection and you're looking for a specific, tractable project with bipartisan appeal and clear implementation paths, automatic recounts deserve serious consideration.
