The Presidential Removal Power: How Easy Is It to Fire Democracy's Defenders?

Epistemic status: High confidence on legal doctrine and historical precedent. Medium confidence on how this plays out in 2029+ scenarios given limited modern precedent for mass removal attempts.

Here's a question that should keep democracy defenders up at night: What happens if a president elected in 2028 decides to fire every federal official who might resist election subversion in 2032?

I don't mean hypothetically. I mean legally—what actually stops this from happening?

The answer is more unsettling than you might think.

The Basic Problem

The U.S. Constitution gives the President broad authority to remove executive branch officials. Article II, Section 2 says the President "shall nominate, and by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, shall appoint" officers—but it says nothing explicit about removal.

This silence has created 200+ years of constitutional ambiguity. And that ambiguity matters enormously for election security.

Why? Because many of the federal officials who could either protect or undermine elections in 2028-2032 serve at the president's pleasure. If a president wants to replace them with loyalists, the legal barriers are surprisingly thin.

The Supreme Court Just Made This Worse

In May 2024, the Supreme Court decided Jarkesy v. SEC, a case that significantly expanded presidential removal power. The court ruled that administrative law judges at the Securities and Exchange Commission had too much protection from presidential removal.

Justice Roberts wrote for the 6-3 majority that such protections violated separation of powers. The president must have sufficient control over executive officers to fulfill his constitutional duties, the court reasoned.

But here's what makes this genuinely scary for election security: The same logic could apply to other "independent" positions across the federal government.

As constitutional law scholars at Yale noted in their analysis, Jarkesy represents "the most significant expansion of presidential removal power in decades, with implications extending far beyond the SEC."

The Precedent: You Can Fire People Investigating You

We actually have a case study for how removal power works in practice during constitutional crises: Watergate.

On October 20, 1973, President Nixon ordered Attorney General Elliot Richardson to fire Special Prosecutor Archibald Cox, who was investigating Nixon. Richardson refused and resigned. Nixon then ordered Deputy Attorney General William Ruckelshaus to fire Cox. Ruckelshaus also refused and resigned.

Nixon finally found someone willing to do it: Solicitor General Robert Bork, who fired Cox in what became known as the Saturday Night Massacre.

Here's the critical point: Nixon had the legal authority to do all of this. The public outcry was enormous and contributed to his eventual resignation, but the removals themselves were constitutional. As the Justice Department's Office of Legal Counsel concluded in a 1973 memo, "The President has plenary authority to remove any officer in the executive branch."

The only thing that stopped Nixon was political pressure, not legal barriers.

Who Could Be Fired in an Election Crisis?

Let's get concrete about which federal officials matter for election security in 2028-2032, and how vulnerable they are to removal:

The Attorney General: Completely serves at the president's pleasure. No statutory protection. Could be fired for refusing to bring politically motivated voter fraud cases or for investigating actual election crimes by presidential allies.

U.S. Attorneys (93 positions): These are the federal prosecutors in each judicial district. They're presidentially appointed and serve at the president's pleasure. A president could fire any U.S. Attorney investigating election crimes by allies or refusing to prosecute political opponents.

This isn't hypothetical. In 2017, the Trump administration requested resignation letters from all 46 remaining Obama-appointed U.S. Attorneys, as reported by NPR. While such mass replacements are normal between administrations, they demonstrate how easy removal is.

The FBI Director: Here's where it gets interesting. Congress tried to insulate this position after Watergate. The FBI Director serves a 10-year term specifically to span multiple presidencies.

But the Supreme Court has ruled this doesn't prevent removal. In Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935), the court held that Congress can limit presidential removal power for some positions—but primarily for independent regulatory agencies, not law enforcement.

The FBI Director can be fired. Trump fired FBI Director James Comey in 2017, and while it was politically explosive, it was legal.

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) Director: CISA is the agency responsible for federal election security assistance. Its director serves at the president's pleasure with no special statutory protections.

Trump fired CISA Director Chris Krebs in November 2020 via tweet, after Krebs called the 2020 election "the most secure in American history." The firing was completely legal.

The One Big Exception: Independent Regulatory Commissions

There is one category of federal officials with stronger removal protections: commissioners of independent regulatory agencies.

The Federal Election Commission, for example, has six commissioners who serve six-year terms and can only be removed "for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office" under 52 U.S.C. § 30106(a)(3).

This "for cause" protection is constitutional, the Supreme Court ruled in Humphrey's Executor. A president can't fire an FEC commissioner just for political disagreement.

But here's the problem: The FEC has limited jurisdiction over state election administration and voter registration. It regulates federal campaign finance, not the mechanics of vote counting or certification that we've discussed in previous newsletters.

The officials who actually run federal election security assistance? Much more vulnerable.

Why This Matters for 2028-2032

Imagine this scenario:

It's early 2029. A new president takes office after a close 2028 election. Throughout 2029-2031, this president systematically replaces U.S. Attorneys in swing states with loyalists. The Attorney General is already a close ally.

By summer 2032, three months before the presidential election, federal prosecutors in Pennsylvania, Arizona, Michigan, Wisconsin, Georgia, Nevada, and North Carolina all report to people who owe their jobs to the incumbent.

State election officials who resist pressure to manipulate voter rolls or certification have nowhere to turn for federal protection. U.S. Attorneys decline to investigate credible reports of intimidation against county election workers. The FBI Director, appointed in 2029, deprioritizes election crime investigations.

When a close election comes down to one state in November 2032, and county officials face the kind of pressure we saw in 2020, there's no federal cavalry coming.

This isn't paranoia. This is just the straightforward application of existing presidential removal power.

What the Research Says

Political scientists who study democratic backsliding have identified politicization of law enforcement as a critical warning sign.

A 2019 paper in Perspectives on Politics by Harvard's Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt documents how erosion of judicial and prosecutorial independence precedes democratic collapse in case after case.

Their key finding: "Democratic backsliding today begins at the ballot box." Elected leaders use their legal authority to systematically replace independent officials with loyalists. Each replacement is individually defensible. The pattern is devastating.

Turkey's Erdoğan replaced thousands of judges and prosecutors after 2016, all legally. Hungary's Orbán reshaped the judiciary through constitutional changes that gave his party appointment power, all within existing rules. Venezuela's Chávez packed the Supreme Court in 2004 with loyalists using legal procedures, as documented by Human Rights Watch.

The American presidency has far more unilateral removal power than most democratic executives. We've just been lucky that most presidents haven't fully exploited it.

The Constitutional Theory Behind This

The Supreme Court's removal power doctrine rests on the "unitary executive theory"—the idea that Article II vests all executive power in the president, who must therefore have control over all executive officers.

This theory has gained significant ground in recent Supreme Court decisions. In Seila Law v. CFPB (2020), the court struck down the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's single-director structure with for-cause removal protection, ruling 5-4 that it violated separation of powers.

Chief Justice Roberts wrote: "The President must be able to remove not just officers who disobey him, but also those who... execute the laws in a manner that is inconsistent with the President's policies."

Now apply that logic to election security. If a president's "policy" is to aggressively investigate alleged (but non-existent) voter fraud, can they fire officials who won't pursue those investigations? Under Seila Law's reasoning, probably yes.

Legal scholars are divided on this. A 2023 Harvard Law Review article by Jed Shugerman argues the unitary executive theory "lacks historical foundation and creates severe risks for democratic accountability."

But the current Supreme Court majority has embraced it. And that majority is likely to remain for years.

What About Civil Service Protections?

You might be thinking: "What about all those career federal employees who can't be fired easily?"

This is an important point. Most federal employees below the political appointee level have significant civil service protections under 5 U.S.C. § 7513. They can only be removed for cause after extensive procedural protections.

But in October 2020, Trump issued Executive Order 13957, creating a new "Schedule F" employment category. This would have reclassified tens of thousands of federal policy positions to remove civil service protections, making them at-will employees.

Biden revoked this order on his first day in office. But Trump has promised to reinstate it if elected in 2024, and other Republican candidates have endorsed similar plans.

The legal authority to do this is unclear—it would almost certainly be litigated. But the Office of Personnel Management under Trump argued the president has this authority under the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978.

If Schedule F or something similar were reinstated in 2025 or 2029, civil service protections for many election-security-relevant positions could disappear.

So What Actually Stops Mass Firings?

Given all this, what actually prevents a president from systematically removing officials who might resist election subversion?

The honest answer: Mostly political cost, not legal barriers.

The Saturday Night Massacre sparked a constitutional crisis because public outcry was intense. Congress began impeachment proceedings. Nixon's approval ratings collapsed. He resigned nine months later.

But notice what didn't stop Nixon: legal prohibitions on removal. Those didn't exist in any meaningful way.

The same is true today. A president could fire the FBI Director, CISA Director, and multiple U.S. Attorneys in a single week. It would be legal. The consequences would be political.

This matters because political consequences depend on public awareness and media coverage. If removals happen gradually, or are justified with plausible-sounding reasoning, public outcry might not materialize.

And if removals happen after winning an election in 2028, a president has four years before facing voters again. That's potentially long enough for the storm to pass.

What This Means for Democracy Defense

This analysis points to several uncomfortable conclusions:

First, legal protections for election-security-related federal officials are weaker than most people realize. The system depends heavily on presidential restraint.

Second, strengthening these protections through legislation would require overcoming significant constitutional hurdles. After Seila Law and Jarkesy, it's unclear what removal protections Congress could create that the current Supreme Court would uphold.

Third, defending against this vulnerability requires building state-level capacity that doesn't depend on federal law enforcement. State attorneys general, state investigators, state courts—these become even more critical if federal officials can be purged.

Fourth, early warning systems matter enormously. If a president starts systematically replacing U.S. Attorneys or other relevant officials in 2029-2030, that's a flashing warning sign for 2032. Pro-democracy organizations need to be tracking these appointments closely.

The Bottom Line

Presidential removal power is much broader than most people realize, especially after recent Supreme Court decisions. Federal officials who might protect election integrity in 2028-2032—including U.S. Attorneys, the FBI Director, and election security agency heads—can largely be fired at will.

Legal barriers to a president installing loyalists in these positions are minimal. The main deterrent is political cost, which depends on public awareness.

For anyone building democracy protection projects: Don't assume federal law enforcement will be a reliable backstop in future election crises. Build redundant state-level capacity. Monitor federal appointments in swing states closely. And create public accountability mechanisms that raise the political cost of politically motivated removals.

The Saturday Night Massacre happened 50 years ago. We remember it as a cautionary tale about presidential overreach. But the legal authority Nixon used that night? Still fully intact.
