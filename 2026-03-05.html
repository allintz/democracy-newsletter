The Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network: Inside the Opposition's Ground Game

Epistemic status: High confidence on documented facts about organizational structure and activities, moderate confidence on strategic assessments based on public reporting.

In previous newsletters, I've covered what democracy defenders are building and what vulnerabilities they're trying to patch. Today I want to look at the other side of the board: specifically, the Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network, which has quietly become one of the most sophisticated efforts to challenge election procedures and results at the local level.

This matters for 2028 because while we've been tracking high-profile election deniers running for secretary of state, Heritage has been building something more subtle and potentially more durable: a trained volunteer network in swing state counties, equipped with specific legal strategies and connected to coordinated legal support.

What the Election Integrity Network Actually Is

The Election Integrity Network (EIN) launched in late 2021 as a project of the Heritage Foundation, the influential conservative think tank. But it's not primarily a think tank project - it's an organizing operation.

According to ProPublica's investigation published in May 2024, the network has recruited and trained over 5,000 volunteers across eight swing states: Arizona, Georgia, Wisconsin, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Nevada, North Carolina, and Florida. These aren't random activists - they go through multi-week training programs on election law, observation procedures, and challenge processes.

The network is run by Cleta Mitchell, a longtime Republican election lawyer who gained prominence for participating in the January 6, 2021 call where Trump pressed Georgia officials to "find 11,780 votes." (I covered that call in an earlier newsletter, so I won't rehash it here.) Mitchell left her law firm after that controversy but found a new home at Heritage.

Here's what makes EIN structurally different from previous voter challenge efforts: it's not primarily focused on Election Day itself. Instead, the network concentrates on three earlier intervention points.

The Three-Stage Strategy

Stage 1: Voter roll challenges (12-18 months before Election Day)

EIN trains volunteers to file mass challenges to voter registrations, arguing that specific voters should be removed from rolls. According to Reuters reporting from August 2024, the network has filed or coordinated challenges affecting over 850,000 voter registrations since 2022.

The challenges typically target voters flagged by data matching - people who appear to have moved based on National Change of Address (NCOA) data, or whose names appear similar to names on jury duty exemption lists or death records. As I explained in a previous newsletter on voter roll purges, these matching processes have high error rates because they rely on fuzzy name matching and address data that's often outdated.

What's new is the scale and coordination. In Georgia alone, according to the Atlanta Journal-Constitution's reporting in March 2024, EIN-coordinated volunteers filed challenges to over 100,000 registrations across 23 counties in a single month.

Stage 2: Election procedure challenges (6-12 months before Election Day)

The second focus is challenging specific election procedures through state and local administrative processes. This includes things like drop box locations, early voting hours, and absentee ballot processing rules.

Here's an example of how this works in practice. In Fulton County, Georgia, EIN volunteers submitted detailed objections to the county's plan for drop box placement in January 2024, according to Fulton County Board of Elections meeting minutes. The objections cited security concerns and requested boxes be relocated away from certain precincts. The county ultimately moved three boxes.

That might sound minor - and moving three drop boxes probably didn't affect many voters. But multiply that by hundreds of similar challenges across dozens of counties, and you create several effects: you consume election administrator time and resources, you create uncertainty about procedures, and you establish a record of "irregularities" that can be cited later if results are contested.

Stage 3: Observer deployment and real-time challenges (Election Day and counting period)

The third component is deploying trained observers to polls and counting centers, equipped with specific things to watch for and direct lines to lawyers who can file immediate challenges.

According to Democracy Docket's tracking of the 2022 midterms, EIN-trained observers were present at over 200 counting centers across six states. The network provides observers with detailed checklists and encourages them to document and immediately report potential irregularities to coordinated legal teams.

This isn't technically illegal - election observation is a normal and healthy part of democratic processes. But the scale and coordination matter. When you have trained observers in every key counting location, connected to coordinated legal response, you can quickly generate formal challenges that require official responses.

The Funding and Infrastructure

Where does the money come from? Heritage doesn't break out EIN's budget separately, but The Guardian reported in September 2024 that the organization received a $15 million increase in donor-restricted funding in 2023, with donor communications explicitly mentioning "election integrity priorities."

The network has also built technical infrastructure. According to Vox's investigation published in January 2025, EIN operates a proprietary database system called "IV3" (apparently standing for "Intelligent Voter Verification and Validation") that aggregates publicly available voter data, NCOA records, and other datasets to flag potential registration issues.

The system then generates challenge forms pre-filled with the required information for each state's procedures. This dramatically lowers the barrier to filing challenges - volunteers don't need to understand the technical requirements, they just need to sign and submit the forms the system generates.

How Effective Has This Been?

This is harder to assess definitively, but we have some data points.

In terms of voter removals, EIN's own materials (obtained by ProPublica) claim credit for approximately 150,000 voters removed from rolls in 2022-2024. That number is hard to independently verify, because it's not always clear which removals resulted from EIN challenges versus routine list maintenance.

However, we can look at what happened in specific places. In Fulton County, Georgia, according to reporting by GPB News in November 2024, approximately 2,100 voters were removed following challenges coordinated through EIN in 2023-2024. Of those, the county later determined that approximately 800 were removed in error and needed to be reinstated after voters showed up to vote and discovered they'd been purged.

That's an error rate of roughly 38% - meaning about two out of every five challenged voters were actually eligible and legally registered. This aligns with error rates I discussed in the voter roll purge newsletter - the data matching process simply isn't accurate enough to reliably identify ineligible voters.

More importantly, several hundred of those erroneously purged voters only discovered the problem when they tried to vote. Some cast provisional ballots that were later counted after verification, but according to research by the Carter Center, provisional ballot rejection rates in Georgia were around 15% in 2024, meaning some voters were likely disenfranchised.

What Makes This Different From Past Voter Challenge Programs

The Republican National Committee ran voter challenge programs for decades (until they were banned by a consent decree that I've mentioned in previous newsletters, which expired in 2018). True the Vote, which I've also covered before, has organized challenge campaigns.

What makes EIN structurally different is the integration of several components:

  • Sustained year-round operation, not just ramping up before elections
  • Technical infrastructure that systematizes and scales the challenge process
  • Legal coordination providing real-time support to volunteers
  • Legitimate institutional backing from Heritage, which provides credibility
  • Training programs that create a persistent volunteer base rather than one-off recruitment

    • The training component is particularly significant. According to materials ProPublica obtained, the training covers election law, observation techniques, challenge procedures, and also includes messaging guidance - how to frame concerns in terms of "election integrity" rather than partisan advantage, how to engage with media, how to present evidence of irregularities.

    This is basically creating an institutionalized opposition research and legal challenge infrastructure that will persist across multiple election cycles.

    The Counter-Response and Current Legal Status

    Several states have strengthened protections against mass challenges. Georgia passed SB 189 in 2024, which limits individuals to challenging 10 voters per county per calendar month unless they have specific personal knowledge of ineligibility.

    Pennsylvania's Secretary of State issued guidance in February 2025 clarifying that challenges must be based on personal knowledge, not just data matching, and that counties can reject facially insufficient challenges without hearings.

    The ACLU and several other organizations have filed lawsuits challenging mass removals in multiple states. A federal court in Wisconsin blocked approximately 20,000 removals in October 2024, finding that the challenges relied on unreliable data and targeted minority voters disproportionately.

    But these defenses are reactive and vary by state. There's no uniform federal standard, and some states (like Arizona and North Carolina) have minimal protections against mass challenges.

    Why This Matters for 2028

    The 2028 election is still more than two years away, but the voter roll challenges for that election are happening right now. Under most states' laws, voters can be challenged starting around 12-18 months before an election (with the 90-day quiet period I've discussed before applying only to systematic purges by election officials, not individual challenges).

    That means the challenge season for 2028 has already begun in some jurisdictions. And the infrastructure to coordinate those challenges at scale is already built and operating.

    The second thing that matters is the observer and legal response network. In 2020, the Trump campaign and Republican Party had to hastily assemble legal teams and observers when they decided to contest results. In 2028, there will be a standing, trained, coordinated network already in place.

    The third thing is the documentation and narrative building. Every procedural objection, every challenge filed, every irregularity documented creates a paper trail that can support later claims that an election was flawed. Even if individual challenges fail, the cumulative record of "concerns" can be cited to justify skepticism of results.

    I want to be clear: documenting problems and challenging potentially ineligible registrations is not inherently antidemocratic. Election observation and verification processes are important safeguards. The concern is specifically about coordinated mass challenges using unreliable data, which research consistently shows disproportionately affects eligible voters from minority communities and creates barriers to voting.

    The Broader Context: Institution Building

    Taking a step back, what Heritage has built with EIN is an example of opposition institution building. It's not flashy or headline-grabbing like election deniers refusing to certify results. It's sustained, systematic, and integrated into normal democratic processes.

    This matches patterns I've discussed in previous newsletters about democratic backsliding - the most effective attacks on democracy aren't coups, they're gradual institutional changes that make it harder for certain people to vote or easier to challenge results.

    The effectiveness of this strategy doesn't depend on bad faith from election officials or dramatic constitutional crises. It works by making election administration harder, by consuming administrator resources with challenges and objections, by creating uncertainty about who's on the voter rolls, and by building infrastructure that can be activated if someone wants to contest close results.

    Bottom Line

    The Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network represents a significant investment in sustained, systematic opposition infrastructure for influencing election procedures and outcomes. With 5,000+ trained volunteers, proprietary technology, coordinated legal support, and operations in all key swing states, it's substantially more sophisticated and durable than previous voter challenge efforts.

    For someone thinking about democracy protection projects, this suggests several gaps:

    Immediate defense needs: There's apparently no equivalent coordinated rapid-response system for helping purged voters get reinstated or for assisting election officials in evaluating and responding to mass challenges. Organizations like the Brennan Center provide legal expertise, but there's no systematic volunteer network to match EIN's ground game.

    Data and monitoring: We need better real-time tracking of voter registration challenges - where they're being filed, what data they're based on, what the resolution rates are. This information exists in county records but isn't systematically aggregated or analyzed.

    Voter notification systems: When voters are challenged and potentially removed, notification is often inadequate. Automated systems that alert voters they've been challenged and help them respond could significantly reduce disenfranchisement.

    Election official support: Small counties facing dozens or hundreds of coordinated challenges often lack the legal and administrative resources to properly evaluate them. Regional legal support networks specifically for election officials handling challenge surges would address a concrete need.

    The broader takeaway is that defending democracy requires building institutions and infrastructure, not just winning individual legal battles or elections. The opposition has figured this out. The defense side is still largely operating on a crisis-response model rather than sustained institution building.

    That's probably the most important thing to understand about EIN: it's not fundamentally about the 2024 or even 2028 election. It's about building persistent infrastructure that will shape election administration for the next decade.
