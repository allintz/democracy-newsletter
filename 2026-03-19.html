How Costa Rica Built the World's Most Trusted Election System (And What the US Can Learn)

Epistemic status: High confidence on Costa Rica's institutional design and documented outcomes; medium confidence on direct applicability to US context given different constitutional structures.

Let me tell you about a democracy that solved a problem the United States is still struggling with.

In Costa Rica, election results are announced by a body that literally cannot be captured by the president or ruling party. When that body declares a winner, approximately 98% of Costa Ricans trust the result, according to Vanderbilt University's AmericasBarometer surveys. For context, trust in US election administration varies wildly by party and has dropped below 40% among Republicans since 2020.

Costa Rica didn't achieve this through better technology or bigger budgets. They did it through institutional design—creating an election authority that's genuinely independent of both the government and political parties. And the way they built those safeguards offers specific lessons for US democracy reformers thinking about 2026-2028.

The Supreme Electoral Tribunal: Independence by Design

Costa Rica runs elections through the Supreme Electoral Tribunal (TSE), established in their 1949 constitution after a brief civil war. Here's what makes it different from US election administration:

The TSE has constitutional status equal to the executive, legislative, and judicial branches. Not subordinate to them—equal to them. According to Costa Rica's Political Constitution Article 9, the government explicitly consists of three supreme powers plus the Supreme Electoral Tribunal.

What does constitutional parity actually mean in practice? Three things that matter:

First, the TSE controls its own budget. The legislature cannot defund it. Article 176 of the constitution guarantees the TSE at least 0.5% of the national budget, allocated automatically without legislative approval. During election years, they get more.

Second, no other branch can override TSE decisions on electoral matters. When the TSE declares someone the winner, that's final. Article 102 of the Electoral Code makes TSE rulings binding on all state institutions, including courts. There's no appeal to the Supreme Court, no emergency injunction process, no legislative intervention possible.

Third, the TSE doesn't just count votes—it has exclusive authority over voter registration, party registration, campaign finance, and certifying elected officials. According to International IDEA's analysis, this concentration of electoral functions in one constitutionally protected body is unusual even among independent electoral management bodies.

How Personnel Selection Prevents Capture

But constitutional status doesn't matter if the wrong people control the institution. The selection mechanism is what makes this work.

The TSE has three magistrates (plus two alternates). They serve staggered six-year terms. Here's the key part: they're selected by the Supreme Court—specifically, by a two-thirds supermajority vote of all 22 Supreme Court justices.

Why does this matter? Because Supreme Court justices themselves are selected by the legislature with a two-thirds supermajority requirement. So TSE magistrates need support from justices who themselves required cross-party legislative consensus.

This creates a double-supermajority filter. You cannot pack the TSE with loyalists unless you first achieve sustained two-thirds legislative control, then use that to pack the Supreme Court over multiple years, then use that to eventually influence TSE appointments.

Fabrice Lehoucq, a political scientist at the University of North Carolina who studies Costa Rican institutions, wrote in 2002 that this selection mechanism "makes it nearly impossible for any single party to control the Tribunal." The outcome data backs this up: across 75+ years, the TSE has never been credibly accused of favoring a presidential administration.

What This Actually Looks Like in Practice

Here's a concrete example of how structural independence translates to real authority.

In Costa Rica's 2014 presidential election, early results showed National Liberation Party candidate Johnny Araya losing to center-left challenger Luis Guillermo Solís. Araya quit campaigning a month before the April runoff, effectively conceding. But legally, his name had to stay on the ballot.

The TSE simply proceeded with the runoff as scheduled. They counted the votes (Solís won 78% to 22%). They certified Solís as president-elect. The entire process took three weeks from runoff to certification. The New York Times reported zero controversy, zero litigation, zero disputed results.

Compare that to the institutional stress test in the US 2020 election, where the losing side attempted certification obstruction in multiple states despite much larger margins.

The difference isn't that Costa Ricans are more virtuous or less partisan. According to Vanderbilt's 2019 survey data, Costa Rica has normal levels of political polarization—about 65% of citizens identify with a political party, similar to US rates.

The difference is that partisan actors in Costa Rica face an institution they genuinely cannot capture on timescales relevant to any single election.

The Staff Professional Model

The TSE doesn't just have independent leadership—it has a professionalized permanent staff insulated from political pressure.

Costa Rica's Electoral Code establishes career civil service positions throughout the TSE hierarchy. You cannot be hired for most TSE positions if you've held partisan political office in the previous four years. Staff are hired through competitive examination, not political appointment.

At the local level, TSE district offices are staffed by permanent employees who serve across multiple elections and administrations. According to International IDEA's comparative study, this creates "institutional memory and technical expertise independent of electoral cycles."

Here's what that looks like on the ground: When Costa Rica implemented biometric voter registration in 2011, the project was managed by career TSE technical staff who had worked on voter registration for 10-15 years. When leadership changed after the 2014 election, those staff remained. The technical knowledge stayed in the institution.

Contrast that with US county election offices, where turnover has hit 30-40% since 2020, often taking decades of institutional knowledge with it. Many critical swing state positions are now held by people hired within the last two years.

What the US Could Actually Adopt

I want to be clear about what's transferable and what isn't. The US cannot copy-paste Costa Rica's constitution. We have federalism. We have 50 states running their own elections under state law. We have constitutional constraints on what Congress can mandate for state election administration.

But three specific elements could work within US constitutional structure:

State-level constitutional independent election authorities. Nothing prevents a state constitution from establishing an independent election board with secured funding and supermajority appointment requirements. Michigan's 2022 constitutional amendment created an independent redistricting commission through direct voter initiative. The same mechanism could create independent election authorities.

Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin could all pursue this through constitutional amendment. Some already have partial versions—Georgia has an independent Secretary of State, though the State Election Board lacks Costa Rica-style protections.

Supermajority appointment requirements. Even without full constitutional independence, states could require legislative supermajorities to appoint secretaries of state or election board members. This already exists in some states for judicial appointments and public utility commissioners.

A 2/3 requirement for election administration positions would force cross-party consensus and make capture much harder. It wouldn't prevent all bad appointments, but it would prevent unilateral party control.

Protected multi-year budgets. State legislatures could guarantee minimum election administration funding as a percentage of state budget, similar to Costa Rica's 0.5% floor. Several US states already have constitutional dedications for education spending or transportation infrastructure.

According to research by Charles Stewart III at MIT, US election administration is dramatically underfunded compared to other democratic countries, with spending at roughly $3-5 per voter versus $10-15+ in countries with professionalized systems. Guaranteed funding could support permanent professional staff instead of the current volunteer-and-temporary-worker model in most counties.

Why This Matters for 2026-2028

The US currently runs elections through a fragmented system of elected partisans (secretaries of state), appointed boards (often with party balance requirements), and thousands of county officials with wildly varying qualifications and resources.

This structure made sense when elections were genuinely local affairs and partisan balance seemed sufficient to ensure fairness. It doesn't make sense when presidential campaigns are systematically targeting county certification chokepoints and swing state election administration positions.

We documented in recent newsletters how this played out: defeated officials refusing to certify their own losses in New Mexico, county board deadlocks in Arizona and Michigan, secretaries of state facing presidential pressure campaigns, and systematic efforts to replace independent-minded election officials with partisan loyalists.

Costa Rica's model shows there's an alternative. You can have genuine institutional independence through careful structural design. You can insulate election administration from partisan capture without removing democratic accountability—TSE magistrates can be removed for misconduct, just not for partisan reasons.

The key insight is that appointment mechanisms matter more than formal independence declarations. Lots of countries have "independent" election commissions that get packed with ruling party loyalists because the appointment process allows it. Costa Rica's double-supermajority filter actually works because the math prevents single-party control.

Bottom Line for Democracy Builders

If you're thinking about starting projects in the democracy space, state-level constitutional reform for election administration is dramatically under-resourced compared to its potential impact.

Here's the opportunity: Several swing states have citizen initiative processes that allow constitutional amendments through petition signatures. These states include Arizona, Michigan, and Nevada—three of the most critical for 2028 presidential election integrity.

A well-designed campaign could put independent election authority amendments on state ballots in 2026, two years before the next presidential election. The political conditions might actually be favorable: voters tend to support "good government" election administration reforms when they're framed as removing partisan control rather than favoring one party.

Existing organization doing related work: RepresentUs has successfully passed anti-corruption and election reform amendments at the state level, including ranked choice voting in Alaska and independent redistricting in multiple states. They have campaign infrastructure and experience with state constitutional reform that could potentially extend to election administration.

Project idea: A "State Election Independence Initiative" could draft model constitutional amendments for independent election authorities, identify winnable states, and run coordinated ballot initiative campaigns for 2026. Key elements would include: supermajority appointment requirements, protected funding, professional civil service staff protections, and clear authority over certification.

The window is short—you need about 18-24 months to organize a successful state constitutional amendment campaign. That means work needs to start in 2026 for any structural changes to be in place before 2028 preparations begin in earnest.

Costa Rica proved this can work. The question is whether US democracy defenders will build it before we need it.
