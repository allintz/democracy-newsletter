Today's Topic: Why Counties Matter More Than You Think—The Surprisingly Local Nature of American Election Administration

Reading time: 5 minutes

Epistemic status: High confidence on structural facts about U.S. election administration; medium confidence on specific vulnerability assessments, which depend on local implementation details that vary significantly.

---

When most people think about election security, they picture the Secretary of State's office or maybe the state legislature. But here's something that surprised me when I first learned it: the actual machinery of American elections—the stuff that determines whether your vote gets counted correctly—happens at a radically local level that most voters never think about.

Today I want to walk you through why this matters for democracy protection, using specific examples of how this decentralized structure has both saved elections and created dangerous vulnerabilities.

The Weird Fact About American Elections

Here's the core insight: America doesn't really have 50 election systems. It has roughly 10,500 election jurisdictions, most of them at the county level.

According to the U.S. Election Assistance Commission, these local jurisdictions make critical operational decisions about:

  • Which voting machines to purchase and how to configure them
  • How to design ballots (which is harder than it sounds—remember the 2000 "butterfly ballot" in Palm Beach County that confused thousands of voters?)
  • Where to place polling locations and how many to have
  • How to process mail-in ballots
  • How to conduct recounts when they're needed

    • The federal government provides some baseline rules. State governments add another layer. But then each county implements those rules with enormous discretion about the details.

    When Decentralization Saved Democracy: Maricopa County 2020

    Let me show you how this structure protected democracy in 2020, using Arizona as a case study.

    After the 2020 election, Trump and his allies focused enormous pressure on Arizona, which Biden won by just 10,457 votes. They needed to flip the state, and Maricopa County—which includes Phoenix and casts about 60% of Arizona's votes—was the key target.

    Here's what happened: The Arizona state legislature, controlled by Republicans, held hearings with Rudy Giuliani pushing election fraud claims. According to testimony documented by the January 6th Committee, Trump personally called Arizona state legislators and the Republican governor, pressuring them to overturn the results.

    But the election administration itself happened at the county level. Maricopa County's Board of Supervisors—four Republicans and one Democrat—certified their results on November 20, 2020. The county's Recorder, Stephen Richer (a Republican), and the elections staff maintained their professional standards throughout.

    As documented by the Arizona Republic, when pressured, the Republican Board Chairman Clint Hickman said simply: "We do elections well here. It's time to dial back the rhetoric."

    The critical point: Even if state-level officials had been willing to participate in overturning the election, they couldn't simply command county officials to change the results. Counties operated their own systems, maintained their own documentation, and made their own certification decisions.

    This structural separation of authority—not just individual courage—created a resilience that protected the outcome.

    When Decentralization Creates Danger: The Coffee County Breach

    Now let me show you the flip side: how this same decentralized structure creates vulnerabilities.

    Coffee County, Georgia is a rural county with about 43,000 people. In January 2021—after the presidential election but before the Georgia Senate runoffs—Trump-aligned operatives gained access to the county's election systems.

    According to court filings in the Curling v. Raffensperger case, these operatives copied virtually all the data from the county's election servers, including software and ballot images. The Washington Post's investigation found that local officials appear to have invited them in.

    Here's why the county-level structure mattered: Coffee County's election supervisor could grant this access without state approval. There was no requirement for state-level oversight of who accessed the county's systems. The breach wasn't discovered for months, and only became public through litigation.

    Small counties often lack the resources for sophisticated security. According to research by the Brennan Center for Justice, counties with populations under 50,000 frequently have election departments with just 1-3 full-time staff members and minimal IT security infrastructure.

    This means the security of American elections is only as strong as the least-protected county in a swing state.

    The Math Gets Scary When You Think About Swing States

    Let me walk you through why this structure creates systematic vulnerabilities in close elections.

    In 2020, Biden's victory depended on several states with very narrow margins. According to certified election results compiled by the Cook Political Report, flipping just three states would have changed the outcome:

  • Arizona: 10,457 votes (across 15 counties)
  • Georgia: 11,779 votes (across 159 counties)
  • Wisconsin: 20,682 votes (across 72 counties)

    • Now think about what an election subversion attempt needs: You don't need to compromise every county in a state. You just need to create enough chaos, delays, or challenges in a few strategic counties to prevent or delay state certification.

    Here's a concrete example of how vulnerable this gets: Otero County, New Mexico (population 67,000) made national news in June 2022 when its county commission initially refused to certify primary election results, citing distrust in voting machines. Only a state Supreme Court order forced certification.

    Imagine this happening simultaneously in 4-5 counties in a swing state after a close presidential election, with state officials sympathetic to the effort. The county-level discretion that protected democracy in Maricopa County becomes a vulnerability when multiple counties coordinate obstruction.

    What Makes a County More Vulnerable?

    Research helps us identify which counties face higher risks. The Bipartisan Policy Center's 2021 report on election administration identifies several risk factors:

    Small jurisdiction size: Counties under 50,000 population typically have:

  • 1-2 person election offices
  • Limited technical expertise
  • Budgets that can't afford security consultants
  • Officials who often have other primary job responsibilities

    • Partisan selection methods: In many states, county election officials are:

  • Elected on partisan ballots (creating incentives to please party bases)
  • Appointed by county commissioners (who may prioritize party loyalty)
  • Subject to recall by partisan activists

    • High turnover: The Election Assistance Commission's 2024 survey found that approximately 35% of local election officials left their positions between 2022-2024, creating experience gaps.

    Lack of legal support: Small counties often can't afford to retain election law expertise, making officials vulnerable to pressure or manipulation through legal-sounding but incorrect claims.

    Why This Matters for 2028

    Here's the key strategic insight: Election subversion attempts will likely focus on county-level obstruction in 2028, not just state-level pressure.

    The January 6th Committee's investigation revealed extensive 2020 coordination between Trump campaign officials and local activists in multiple states. According to the Committee's final report, this included direct outreach to county officials to delay certification or refuse to certify results.

    That effort failed in 2020 largely because it was improvised in the weeks after the election. But we should expect more sophisticated, pre-planned county-level strategies in future close elections:

  • Pre-positioning sympathetic officials: Running candidates for county clerk, county commission, or election board positions who have already signaled willingness to challenge results
  • Legal preparation: Having pre-written legal challenges ready to file simultaneously in multiple counties
  • Coordinated delays: Creating bottlenecks at the county level that cascade into missing state certification deadlines
  • Information warfare: Generating local "controversies" that give county officials political cover to delay

    • What Can Actually Be Done?

    Given this county-level vulnerability, what interventions make sense?

    State-level structural reforms:

  • Require state-level cybersecurity standards for all county systems
  • Mandate risk-limiting audits that don't depend on county discretion (like Colorado's system)
  • Create state-level rapid response teams to support counties facing pressure or crises
  • Remove county discretion from key decisions like whether to certify (make it ministerial)

    • County-level capacity building:

  • Organizations like the Center for Tech and Civic Life provide grants to improve county election infrastructure
  • The Elections Group trains election officials in professionalization
  • State associations of county clerks can share best practices and provide peer support

    • Legal preparation:

  • Pre-drafted emergency litigation tools for when counties refuse to certify
  • Clear precedents establishing that certification is ministerial, not discretionary
  • Backup certification mechanisms that don't depend on county action

    • Attention and monitoring:

  • Systematic tracking of county election office turnover in swing states
  • Early warning systems for counties implementing unusual procedural changes
  • Local media coverage of county election board meetings (which currently get almost no attention)

    • The Bottom Line

    American elections are only as secure as the least-protected county in a swing state. The same decentralized structure that helped save democracy in 2020—by preventing state-level officials from simply overriding local results—also creates systematic vulnerabilities that can be exploited through coordinated county-level obstruction.

    For someone building democracy protection projects, here's the key insight: The 2026 county-level elections for clerks, recorders, and commissioners in swing states may be more important than most statewide races. These positions rarely receive meaningful funding, media attention, or candidate recruitment—but they'll control the machinery of the 2028 election.

    A project idea worth considering: Create a "swing county election official tracker" that monitors turnover, recruitment, and elections for these positions in the ~50-100 counties that could actually matter in a close presidential election. This doesn't exist in any systematic way right now, but it's where the action will be in 2028.

    The good news: County-level vulnerabilities can be addressed through state legislation, better resourcing, and professional norms. The bad news: We have very limited time to implement these protections before the officials who will administer the 2028 election are in place.
