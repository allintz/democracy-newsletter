The Ballot-Rejection Playbook: How Technical Voter Challenges Can Disenfranchise Thousands

Epistemic status: High confidence on the basic mechanics and historical examples; moderate confidence on likelihood of future large-scale deployment.

Today we're diving into a less-visible but powerful mechanism for election subversion: mass challenges to voter registrations and cast ballots. This isn't about fake electors or refusing to certify—it's about using legitimate procedural tools at scale to remove voters or reject ballots, often targeting specific communities.

Here's why this matters: unlike certification refusal or legislative interference, voter challenges operate at the retail level of democracy. They're harder to detect, easier to justify as "election integrity," and can be devastatingly effective in close races.

How Voter Challenges Actually Work

Most states allow citizens or political parties to challenge whether specific voters are eligible or whether specific ballots should be counted. The basic idea is reasonable: there should be a mechanism to catch genuine errors like someone registered in two states or voting at the wrong address.

But here's the vulnerability: these laws rarely specify limits on how many challenges one person can file, what evidence is required, or how quickly they must be resolved.

According to research by the Brennan Center for Justice, a single challenger can submit lists with thousands of names, often generated from database matching that produces high error rates. Election officials then face an impossible choice: spend limited resources investigating each challenge (pulling staff away from actual election administration), or risk accusations of ignoring potential fraud.

Georgia 2020: A Test Run

The 2020 election in Georgia provides a clear example of this tactic in action.

In the months before the 2020 runoff elections, conservative groups challenged approximately 364,000 voter registrations—about 4% of Georgia's registered voters. As documented by NPR's investigation, these challenges were based largely on change-of-address data from the US Postal Service, which is notoriously unreliable (many people file temporary address changes, college students maintain registrations at home, etc.).

Local election boards had to schedule hearings and review each challenge. In Muscogee County alone, over 4,000 challenges were filed in the weeks before the January 2021 runoff—creating an administrative nightmare for county officials already managing early voting and election preparation.

Most challenges were ultimately rejected. But that wasn't really the point.

The Real Goal: Chaos and Deterrence

Mass voter challenges serve multiple strategic purposes even when they fail:

1. Resource exhaustion. Election officials must divert staff from voter education, poll worker training, and equipment preparation to process challenges. In Georgia's 2020 runoff, some counties held marathon weekend hearing sessions to work through challenge lists.

2. Voter intimidation. Challenged voters receive notices that their eligibility is questioned. Many don't understand the process and simply don't vote rather than risk showing up and being turned away. Research from the Bipartisan Policy Center found that receipt of a challenge notice significantly reduces turnout among targeted voters, even when the challenge is ultimately denied.

3. Plausible deniability. Unlike refusing to certify or submitting fake electors, filing voter challenges uses an established legal process. Challengers can claim they're just concerned citizens protecting election integrity.

4. Creating grounds for broader challenges. Even unsuccessful challenges generate documentation that can be cited later as "evidence" of problems with voter rolls or election administration—ammunition for post-election litigation.

The North Carolina Precedent

To understand how devastating this tactic can be, look at North Carolina in 2016.

As documented in federal court proceedings, over 6,700 voters in three counties were challenged in the weeks before the 2016 election, based on returned mail and database matching. Local election boards—some controlled by Republicans who'd only recently been appointed—upheld many of these challenges despite weak evidence.

A federal judge eventually blocked the removals, finding they violated federal law's 90-day quiet period before elections. But the judge's order specifically noted that without court intervention, thousands of eligible voters would have been disenfranchised.

Here's the key detail: the challenges disproportionately targeted Black voters. In Beaufort County, African Americans made up 26% of registered voters but 42% of those challenged. The targeting wasn't random—it was based on data analysis designed to identify likely Democratic voters.

Why This Works (And Is Hard to Stop)

Several factors make mass voter challenges an attractive tactic for election subversion:

Timing vulnerabilities. Most states don't have the 90-day quiet period that federal law requires for systematic purges. Individual challenges can be filed right up to Election Day. This means insufficient time for thorough investigation or court review.

Burden of proof problems. In many states, the burden falls on the challenged voter to prove their eligibility, not on the challenger to prove ineligibility. According to analysis by election law experts at UCLA, this reverses the normal presumption and can effectively block eligible voters who lack easy access to documentation.

No cost for bad-faith challenges. In most jurisdictions, there's no penalty for filing frivolous challenges. A challenger who files 1,000 challenges and gets 995 rejected faces no consequences—but may have succeeded in removing 5 eligible voters or intimidating dozens more into not voting.

Decentralization makes defense difficult. With over 10,000 election jurisdictions, defenders can't monitor every county election board. By the time voting rights groups learn about mass challenges in a particular county, hearings may already be scheduled or completed.

The 2024 Expansion (And Looking Ahead)

The 2024 election saw a significant expansion of this tactic, though not at the scale that had been feared.

According to reporting by ProPublica and the Texas Tribune, the group True the Vote developed technology to enable mass challenges and recruited thousands of volunteers. Their stated goal was to file millions of challenges nationwide based on database matching and canvassing.

In practice, most of these efforts were blocked by courts or rejected by election officials. But the infrastructure now exists: the databases, the software, the trained volunteers, the legal templates.

For 2026 and 2028, we should expect:

More sophisticated targeting. Early attempts used crude database matching. Future efforts will likely use commercially available consumer data and more refined algorithms to identify voters more likely to have moved or otherwise be vulnerable to challenges.

Earlier timing. Filing challenges months before Election Day (outside the 90-day federal quiet period) makes court intervention less likely and gives more time for hearings.

Strategic geography. In a close race, mass challenges in a few swing counties could be decisive. You don't need to challenge voters statewide—just in places where the margin is tight and where you control the local election board.

What Makes Someone Vulnerable to Challenge?

Understanding who's most at risk helps illustrate why this tactic is particularly concerning:

  • Renters who move frequently (but may still be legally registered at their previous address if they haven't moved precincts)
  • College students registered at school addresses
  • People with common names (database matching produces false positives)
  • Voters who receive mail at PO boxes or have mail forwarded
  • Military members and their families who may maintain registration in their home state
  • Married people whose names appear differently in different databases

    • Notice a pattern? These aren't categories of likely fraudulent voters. They're categories where database matching produces errors—and where challenges can remove eligible voters while claiming to protect election integrity.

    The Legal Landscape

    The federal National Voter Registration Act (NVRA) provides some protection. It requires that systematic purges of voter rolls stop 90 days before a federal election, as documented in this DOJ guidance.

    But this "quiet period" has important limitations:

    It applies to systematic purges, not individual challenges. Courts have split on whether filing hundreds or thousands of challenges at once counts as systematic.

    It only covers federal elections. State and local elections may have different rules.

    Enforcement is reactive. The DOJ or private plaintiffs must sue—which takes time and resources.

    Several states have strengthened protections. Michigan's 2023 reforms now require that mass challenges be filed at least 180 days before an election and impose penalties for frivolous challenges. Nevada similarly tightened its rules in 2023.

    But many swing states still have weak protections, and new legislatures could weaken existing safeguards.

    Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Defenders

    Mass voter challenges represent a retail-level threat that's qualitatively different from the wholesale election subversion attempts we've covered in previous newsletters.

    Instead of trying to flip entire states through certification refusal or fake electors, this tactic works by removing voters one by one—but at scale. It's death by a thousand cuts rather than a single dramatic blow.

    This makes it simultaneously more defensible (it uses established legal processes) and harder to counter (it's distributed across thousands of local jurisdictions). And unlike certification refusal, which requires capturing key institutional positions, anyone can file voter challenges in most states.

    For those interested in democracy protection work, this suggests several high-value interventions:

    State-level policy reform. Push for laws requiring clear evidence for challenges, penalties for frivolous challenges, earlier deadlines, and burden of proof on challengers. Michigan's 2023 reforms provide a good template.

    Rapid-response legal infrastructure. Organizations like the Election Protection Coalition need resources to detect and respond to mass challenges quickly—ideally before hearings are held. This means monitoring county election board agendas and having legal teams ready to file emergency injunctions.

    Voter education. Many challenged voters don't know they can contest the challenge or how to prove eligibility. Targeted outreach to populations likely to be challenged (renters, students, people with common names) could reduce intimidation effects.

    Technology for defenders. Just as True the Vote built technology to enable mass challenges, defenders need technology to detect unusual patterns in challenge filings and quickly verify voter eligibility.

    Supporting election officials. County election boards need resources and training to handle mass challenges appropriately—including understanding their legal obligation to reject challenges not supported by evidence.

    The organizations doing the most important work on this include the Brennan Center for Justice (policy and litigation), Common Cause (state-level advocacy), and the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights (rapid-response legal defense).

    If you're looking for a concrete project: very few states maintain public, easily accessible databases of voter challenges filed and their outcomes. Building such a database—even for a single swing state—would provide crucial transparency and early warning of mass challenge campaigns. It's the kind of unglamorous infrastructure work that could matter enormously in a close election.

    The 2024 election saw mass challenges mostly fail. But the playbook is now developed, the infrastructure exists, and the legal landscape remains permissive in many states. We should expect more sophisticated attempts in future close elections—making this an area where prevention today could prevent crisis tomorrow.
