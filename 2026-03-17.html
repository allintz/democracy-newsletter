Daily Democracy Brief: The Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law

Tuesday, March 17, 2026

Reading time: ~5 minutes

If you follow election protection work in the United States, you've probably encountered the Election Protection hotline (1-866-OUR-VOTE). You might have seen references to litigation challenging voter purges or discriminatory election rules. These initiatives trace back to the same organization: the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law.

Today we're profiling this organization because understanding the infrastructure protecting American democracy matters just as much as understanding the threats. When we talk about defending elections in 2028, we need to know who's already doing this work, what they're actually good at, and where gaps remain.

What They Actually Do

The Lawyers' Committee was founded in 1963 at the request of President Kennedy. According to their mission statement, they use legal advocacy to achieve racial justice and economic opportunity.

Their election work breaks into three main buckets:

First, the Election Protection coalition. This is probably their most visible program. They run the 866-OUR-VOTE hotline that voters call when they encounter problems at the polls. On Election Day 2024, according to their post-election report, the coalition fielded over 340,000 contacts from voters—calls, texts, and online reports about registration problems, polling place issues, and intimidation attempts.

The hotline isn't just about helping individual voters, though that matters. It's also an intelligence-gathering operation. Those 340,000 data points reveal patterns: which counties are systematically rejecting mail ballots, which polling places have five-hour waits, which voter challenges are targeting specific demographics.

Second, strategic litigation. The Lawyers' Committee files lawsuits challenging election restrictions before they take effect. In 2020, they were lead or co-counsel in 44 lawsuits across 20 states, according to their litigation summary. These cases challenged everything from witness signature requirements on mail ballots to polling place closures in minority neighborhoods.

Their track record here is mixed but meaningful. They won preliminary injunctions extending voter registration deadlines in multiple states in 2020. They successfully challenged Georgia's exact match voter registration system in 2018. But they also lost key cases, including challenges to Florida's felon disenfranchisement rules and Texas's voter ID requirements.

Third, state-level advocacy and monitoring. They deploy election observers to polling places in key jurisdictions. They train local lawyers to handle Election Day problems. They file public records requests to document discriminatory practices. Less visible than litigation, but this work builds the evidence base for future cases.

What Makes Them Distinctive

Several things set the Lawyers' Committee apart from other election protection organizations:

They focus specifically on racial discrimination in voting. While groups like the Brennan Center work on broad election administration issues, the Lawyers' Committee explicitly centers racial justice. This means they prioritize cases where voting restrictions disproportionately affect Black, Latino, and Native American voters.

This focus has advantages and limitations. The advantage: they have deep expertise in Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act and discriminatory impact analysis. According to research by University of Kentucky law professor Joshua Douglas, groups with this specialized focus are more effective at documenting disparate impact than generalist organizations.

The limitation: some election subversion threats (like certification refusals or fake elector schemes) don't primarily operate through racial discrimination. We covered certification vulnerabilities in the March 16 newsletter—those risks affect all voters in a state, not specific demographics. The Lawyers' Committee's mission and expertise don't necessarily extend to those threats.

They operate the only national nonpartisan voter assistance hotline. This is harder than it sounds. Running a hotline that handles 340,000 contacts on Election Day requires serious infrastructure: phone banks, trained volunteers, multi-language support, real-time legal guidance, and database systems to track patterns.

According to their 2024 operational report, the Election Protection coalition includes over 30,000 volunteers and hundreds of attorneys providing real-time legal support. That's substantial capacity that didn't exist before the Lawyers' Committee built it.

They combine immediate voter assistance with long-term systemic advocacy. When a voter calls reporting they were turned away at their polling place, Election Protection volunteers help that specific person vote (by finding their correct precinct, explaining provisional ballot rights, or connecting them with a local attorney). But they also document that incident as evidence of broader problems.

This matters because proving discriminatory patterns requires aggregate data. A single voter turned away could be a mistake. Five hundred voters turned away in predominantly Black precincts over two election cycles is evidence of discrimination. The Lawyers' Committee's dual model captures both.

Where They've Been Effective

Let's look at concrete examples where their work demonstrably mattered:

Ohio voter purge litigation (2016-2018). Ohio was removing voters from rolls if they didn't vote in two consecutive elections and didn't return a postcard. The Lawyers' Committee challenged this as violating the National Voter Registration Act. They lost at the Supreme Court in Husted v. A. Philip Randolph Institute, but according to analysis by the Brennan Center, the litigation forced Ohio to improve its purge notification process and delayed purges that would have removed hundreds of thousands of voters before the 2016 election.

Georgia exact match case (2018). Georgia was holding up voter registrations where applicant information didn't exactly match DMV or Social Security records—including for trivial differences like hyphens in names. The Lawyers' Committee sued, and according to the settlement documented by the ACLU, Georgia agreed to change the policy. Over 50,000 pending registrations were processed as a result.

2020 mail ballot witness requirement challenges. Multiple states required mail ballots to have witness signatures, a barrier during COVID. The Lawyers' Committee filed challenges in South Carolina, Alabama, and other states. They won temporary relief in several jurisdictions, though as reported by NPR, appellate courts later reversed some victories. Still, thousands of voters in 2020 cast ballots under the temporarily relaxed rules.

Election Protection hotline impact. Measuring hotline effectiveness is harder, but their 2022 report included specific examples: according to their case studies, they helped voters in Detroit who were incorrectly told their precinct had moved, connected Arizona voters to emergency ballot curing procedures when signatures were questioned, and documented patterns of broken voting machines in Harris County, Texas that led to litigation.

Where the Gaps Are

No organization does everything. Here are areas where the Lawyers' Committee's model has limitations:

Limited capacity for election administration threats. Their expertise is in voter access litigation and discrimination cases. They're not primarily focused on threats like election official intimidation, certification refusals, or systematic capture of election boards. These are different problems requiring different tools—more criminal prosecution, institutional design, and rapid-response legal defense of officials under pressure.

Other organizations like Protect Democracy and States United Democracy Center work more directly on these issues. The field would benefit from closer coordination between discrimination-focused and administration-focused groups.

State-level resource constraints. The Lawyers' Committee has significant national infrastructure, but according to their 2024 annual report, they have permanent staff in only 8 states. For Election Protection to work effectively, they need local attorneys who know state election law and can provide real-time guidance.

This creates uneven coverage. In states with strong local civil rights legal communities (like Georgia and Texas), the infrastructure is robust. In states like Wisconsin or Arizona—critical swing states—capacity is more limited. According to analysis by the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project, there's a particular shortage of experienced election lawyers in formerly non-competitive states that have recently become battlegrounds.

Reactive rather than proactive model. By design, Election Protection responds to problems voters report. This is valuable but inherently reactive. You can't call a hotline about voter registration manipulation you don't know happened. We covered AI-enabled registration corruption in the March 14 newsletter—those attacks often aren't discovered until voters show up on Election Day and find themselves purged.

Addressing these threats requires proactive monitoring systems, anomaly detection, and forensic analysis. The Lawyers' Committee doesn't primarily do this work. Groups like the Brennan Center and Center for Democracy & Technology focus more on these systemic detection mechanisms.

Limited research and data analysis capacity. The Lawyers' Committee excels at legal advocacy. They're not primarily a research organization. Developing new legal theories, analyzing election data for statistical patterns, or conducting empirical studies of election administration isn't their core competency.

This matters because effective advocacy increasingly requires sophisticated data analysis. Proving discriminatory impact means showing statistical patterns. Identifying voter roll manipulation requires database forensics. According to research by Yale Law School's Douglas Spencer, successful Voting Rights Act cases increasingly depend on quantitative analysis of registration and voting patterns.

Organizations like the Brennan Center have research teams that produce this analysis. The Lawyers' Committee sometimes partners with them, but doesn't have equivalent in-house capacity.

Funding and Sustainability

Understanding pro-democracy organizations means understanding their resources and sustainability. According to their 2024 Form 990, the Lawyers' Committee had revenue of approximately $42 million in 2024.

That sounds like a lot. But compare it to opposition groups: the Heritage Foundation had 2024 revenue of $116 million according to their financial statements. True the Vote raised $7.6 million in 2024, focused entirely on election challenges. The Conservative Partnership Institute, which coordinates election-related legal efforts, had $45 million in revenue in 2024.

The Lawyers' Committee does more than election work—their budget includes criminal justice, fair housing, and economic justice programs. The Election Protection program specifically operates on approximately $12 million annually according to their program budget.

This creates asymmetry. Well-funded groups focused entirely on restricting voting access face election protection organizations running multiple issue areas on comparable or smaller budgets.

The Lawyers' Committee's funding comes primarily from foundation grants and individual donations. According to their donor acknowledgments, major supporters include the Ford Foundation, Carnegie Corporation, and individual donors in the tech and finance sectors.

This funding model has pros and cons. Foundations provide multi-year support, enabling long-term planning. But foundation priorities shift. Individual donations spike around major elections but drop in off-years, creating funding volatility.

According to research by the Center for Effective Philanthropy, democracy-focused organizations face particular fundraising challenges: donors are often reactive (giving more after crises like January 6), making it hard to sustain year-round capacity-building work.

What This Means for 2028

So what should someone interested in democracy protection take from this profile?

First, the Lawyers' Committee provides critical infrastructure that would be very hard to replace. Building a network of 30,000 trained volunteers and hundreds of attorneys doesn't happen quickly. If you're concerned about 2028, you want this infrastructure to exist and be well-resourced.

Second, their model is optimized for voter access fights, not election subversion. They're excellent at challenging discriminatory registration requirements or litigating polling place closures. They're less equipped for rapid-response legal defense of election officials facing pressure to refuse certification, or for prosecuting fake elector schemes.

This isn't criticism—no organization does everything. But it means the democracy protection field needs multiple types of organizations with different capabilities. Someone thinking about starting a new project shouldn't duplicate what the Lawyers' Committee already does well. They should consider unfilled needs.

Third, there are clear capacity gaps in key states. Wisconsin and Arizona have competitive elections and significant election subversion risks, but less developed civil rights legal infrastructure than Georgia or Texas. According to the Stanford-MIT analysis mentioned earlier, building state-level capacity takes years. To have strong networks for 2028, that work needs to start now, in 2026.

Fourth, the funding asymmetry matters. Groups working to restrict voting and challenge election results have significant resources. Election protection organizations are comparatively under-funded. This affects everything from legal capacity to voter contact operations to data analysis capabilities.

Bottom Line for Democracy Builders

If you're thinking about starting a project or joining an organization in this space, here's what to consider:

The Lawyers' Committee and Election Protection are doing important work that needs continued support. Their voter assistance hotline and litigation challenging discriminatory laws fill genuine needs. They have expertise and infrastructure built over decades. Supporting them—through volunteering, donations, or legal pro bono work—is valuable.

But significant gaps remain in the democracy protection ecosystem. Areas needing more capacity:

  • Rapid-response legal defense for election officials under pressure. When a county election director faces threats for refusing to purge voter rolls or a secretary of state faces pressure to delay certification, they need immediate legal support. This requires different expertise than civil rights litigation—more criminal defense, employment law, and crisis response.
  • Proactive monitoring and forensic analysis of voter registration systems. As we covered in recent newsletters, attacks on voter databases and AI-generated registration manipulation require continuous monitoring, not just responding to voter complaints on Election Day. This needs dedicated technology teams, not just legal expertise.
  • State-level infrastructure in newly competitive states. Wisconsin, Arizona, and Nevada need deeper benches of attorneys who know state election law and can respond quickly to local threats. Building these networks takes years of relationship-building with local bar associations and civil rights groups.
  • Integration between voter access advocacy and election administration defense. Groups focused on voter access (like the Lawyers' Committee) and groups focused on defending election officials and certification processes (like Protect Democracy) would benefit from closer coordination and joint strategy.

    • If you're considering starting something new, the highest-impact opportunities likely aren't building another national voting rights litigation group. The Lawyers' Committee and others already do that. Instead, consider:

  • State-level rapid-response legal networks in swing states with capacity gaps
  • Technology-focused projects doing proactive detection of registration manipulation
  • Programs training and supporting election officials facing intimidation
  • Coordination infrastructure helping different types of democracy organizations work together more effectively

    • If you want to support existing work, the Lawyers' Committee specifically needs: volunteer attorneys for Election Protection (especially in swing states), funding for state-level capacity building, and pro bono support for their litigation. Their volunteer page has details.

    More broadly, the democracy protection field needs sustained, multi-year funding—not just spikes around major elections. The threats we've covered in recent newsletters (AI manipulation, certification refusals, official intimidation) require long-term capacity-building, not crisis response.

    The Lawyers' Committee has been doing this work since 1963. They'll still be doing it in 2030. The question is whether they'll have the resources and partner organizations they need to be effective—and whether new initiatives will fill the gaps their model doesn't address.
