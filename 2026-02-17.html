The "Independent State Legislature Theory" Wasn't Really About State Legislatures

Epistemic status: High confidence on legal facts and court rulings (primary sources); medium confidence on strategic implications for future elections.

You've probably heard the phrase "independent state legislature theory" thrown around, especially after the Supreme Court rejected it in Moore v. Harper (2023). But here's what I didn't understand until I dug into it: the theory wasn't actually about giving state legislatures more independence. It was about making them less subject to oversight.

Let me explain what was really at stake—and why this seemingly abstract constitutional debate matters enormously for 2026 and 2028.

What the Theory Actually Claimed

The independent state legislature theory (ISLT) rested on a specific reading of the U.S. Constitution's Elections Clause, which says: "The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof."

Proponents argued this meant state legislatures specifically—not the state as a whole—have exclusive power over federal election rules. According to legal scholars analyzing the theory, this would mean:

  • State courts couldn't review election laws for violations of state constitutions
  • State governors couldn't veto election legislation
  • Citizen ballot initiatives couldn't create election rules
  • Independent redistricting commissions would be unconstitutional

    • Think about what this would mean in practice. Let's say a state legislature passes a law saying "the legislature can reject county vote certifications if it suspects fraud" (similar to what Pennsylvania's legislature considered in 2020). Normally, the state supreme court could strike this down as violating the state constitution. Under ISLT, they couldn't touch it.

    The 2020 Connection

    This wasn't just abstract legal theory. The ISLT became central to actual attempts to subvert the 2020 election.

    After Trump lost Pennsylvania by about 80,000 votes, Republican state legislators asked Congress to reject Pennsylvania's electoral votes. Their legal argument relied heavily on ISLT. According to NPR's analysis of the legal briefs, they argued that because Pennsylvania's Supreme Court had extended the mail ballot deadline (due to COVID-19 postal delays), the state court had unconstitutionally interfered with the legislature's exclusive authority.

    Trump's legal team made similar arguments in multiple states. The basic pattern: state courts or election officials made decisions that legislatures disagreed with, therefore the entire election was invalid, therefore the legislature should be allowed to appoint Trump electors instead.

    None of these arguments succeeded in 2020. But here's the scary part: four Supreme Court justices had signaled openness to some version of ISLT in earlier cases.

    How Moore v. Harper Changed Things

    The case that finally resolved this came from North Carolina. The state legislature had drawn extremely gerrymandered congressional maps. The North Carolina Supreme Court struck them down under the state constitution's guarantee of "free elections."

    The legislature argued: state courts can't review congressional maps because of ISLT. The case went to the U.S. Supreme Court.

    In June 2023, the Supreme Court rejected the independent state legislature theory 6-3. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that state courts absolutely can review federal election laws under state constitutions. The Elections Clause doesn't give legislatures "exclusive and independent authority."

    This was huge. It meant state courts remain a meaningful check on legislative overreach in elections.

    But Here's What Still Worries Me

    The Court's decision wasn't as protective as it might sound. Three key concerns:

    First, the Court left a major opening. Roberts wrote that while state courts can review election laws, they can't "transgress the ordinary bounds of judicial review" or engage in "significant departures from the state legislature's statutory language."

    What does that mean in practice? We don't really know. It's vague enough that in a future case, the Supreme Court could second-guess a state court's decision and say "that interpretation went too far."

    Second, state court composition can change quickly. North Carolina is the perfect example. In 2022, the state Supreme Court had a Democratic majority and struck down the gerrymandered maps. In 2023, after Republican justices won election, the same court reversed itself and reinstated the maps using almost identical reasoning the U.S. Supreme Court had just rejected.

    This means that even with Moore v. Harper protecting state court oversight, the protection is only as strong as the judges on those courts.

    Third, the decision doesn't address legislature vs. governor conflicts. The Court focused on state courts, but didn't clearly resolve whether governors' vetoes or citizen initiatives can be overridden by ISLT-style arguments. According to election law expert Rick Hasen's analysis, these questions remain somewhat open.

    Why This Matters for 2026 and 2028

    Here's my practical takeaway for people working on democracy protection:

    State supreme court races in 2026 are critically important. Even though Moore v. Harper preserved state court oversight, that oversight depends entirely on who sits on those courts. Wisconsin, Ohio, Michigan, North Carolina, and Montana all have supreme court seats up in 2024-2026 cycles.

    If you're looking for high-leverage intervention points, these races are it. They're often low-spending, low-attention races where relatively modest resources can make a real difference. And they directly determine whether state courts will serve as meaningful checks on legislative election manipulation.

    Watch for new legislation testing the boundaries. We should expect some state legislatures to pass election laws that push the limits of what Moore v. Harper allows. They might not explicitly claim ISLT, but they'll try to insulate themselves from court review through careful statutory language.

    Governor vetoes matter more than before. Since Moore v. Harper didn't fully resolve the governor question, having governors willing to veto problematic election legislation provides an important safeguard. This makes gubernatorial races in swing states especially important in 2026.

    A Concrete Example

    Let me make this real with a scenario that could plausibly happen in 2028:

    Imagine a swing state legislature passes a law saying "county boards must certify election results within 3 days, but if the board believes fraud may have occurred, they can request the legislature investigate before certification."

    A county board in a Democratic-leaning area refuses to certify, citing "concerns." The state supreme court orders them to certify. The legislature argues the court can't second-guess the legislature's statutory scheme—this isn't the kind of constitutional violation Roberts was talking about.

    Does this violate Moore v. Harper? Maybe not—the legislature might argue it's a procedural detail, not the court "transgressing ordinary bounds." Whether this works depends entirely on how the state supreme court interprets both the state constitution and the boundaries set by Moore v. Harper.

    This is why the composition of those courts matters so much.

    Bottom Line for Democracy Defenders

    Moore v. Harper was a real victory—it prevented the most extreme version of ISLT from becoming law. But it didn't solve the underlying problem, which is that state legislatures can still do a lot of damage to elections if other institutional checks (courts, governors, election administrators) aren't strong.

    If you're thinking about where to focus energy in 2026:

  • State supreme court races should be top priority in competitive states
  • Gubernatorial races in states with aggressive legislatures matter enormously
  • Track new election legislation for attempts to limit court review through clever drafting
  • Support organizations working on these issues—the Brennan Center for Justice does excellent work tracking election law developments, and Bolster focuses specifically on state-level democracy infrastructure

    • The key insight is this: Moore v. Harper protected the structure of checks and balances, but it didn't protect us from those structures being captured by partisan actors. We still have to do the work of ensuring the right people are in those roles.

    And we have about 20 months to do it before the 2028 presidential election cycle begins in earnest.
