Who Actually Counts the Votes? The Hidden World of County Canvassing Boards

Reading time: 5 minutes

Epistemic status: High confidence on basic mechanics and documented incidents; medium confidence on how vulnerabilities might play out in 2026-2028

Here's something I didn't fully understand until recently: when you hear about "election certification," that's not just one thing that happens at the state level. It's actually a multi-stage process that starts with small groups of local officials you've probably never heard of.

These groups are called county canvassing boards (or county boards of canvass, depending on the state). And they're one of the most important—and vulnerable—chokepoints in American democracy.

Let me explain how this works, why it matters, and what could go wrong.

The First Step in Making Your Vote Official

After polls close on Election Day, your county doesn't just immediately announce results. Instead, there's a formal certification process.

According to research from the MIT Election Data and Science Lab, county canvassing boards—typically composed of 2-4 local officials—meet in the days following the election to review results, reconcile discrepancies, and officially certify the vote count. Only after they certify can the results move up to the state level.

This matters because if a county board refuses to certify, those votes simply don't get counted in the state total. The process stops right there.

Who Serves on These Boards?

This is where it gets interesting. The composition varies wildly by state.

In Michigan, according to state election law, county canvassing boards consist of two Democrats and two Republicans appointed by county parties. That means a 2-2 deadlock can block certification entirely.

In Arizona, based on Arizona Revised Statutes Title 16, the county board of supervisors serves as the canvassing board. These are elected officials who might face political pressure.

In Pennsylvania, as documented in the Pennsylvania Election Code, each county has its own rules—some use elected commissioners, others use appointed boards, and some use combinations of county officials.

There's no standard model. Every state does this differently.

What Could Go Wrong? Let's Look at Recent Examples

In November 2020, Wayne County, Michigan's canvassing board initially refused to certify results. Two Republican members voted against certification in a county that Biden won overwhelmingly.

According to reporting from the Detroit Free Press, they cited "discrepancies" in Detroit precincts—minor administrative issues that election experts said were routine and didn't affect vote counts. After public outcry and a Zoom meeting with angry constituents, they reversed their votes and certified.

But here's the scary part: they tried to rescind their certification the next day, according to follow-up reporting from Bridge Michigan. This happened after a phone call with President Trump.

The rescission didn't work legally. But the attempt shows how this process can be weaponized.

The 2-2 Deadlock Problem

Michigan isn't the only state where evenly balanced boards can deadlock.

A 2022 Brookings Institution analysis found that at least 14 states have county canvassing boards that could deadlock along party lines, potentially blocking certification in counties containing millions of voters.

Think about what this means in a close presidential election. You don't need to flip an entire state. You just need to block certification in one or two counties in a swing state.

Let's say it's 2028 and Pennsylvania comes down to a margin of 15,000 votes statewide. If Philadelphia County (which had over 750,000 votes in 2020) refuses to certify, the state can't produce a complete certified result. No complete certification means the state might not be able to submit electoral votes by the federal deadline.

What Happens When a County Won't Certify?

The answer varies by state, and that's part of the problem.

Some states have clear procedures. In Georgia, if a county refuses to certify, the State Election Board can step in and certify on the county's behalf, according to Georgia election law.

But other states have ambiguous laws. In Arizona, as detailed in a legal analysis from the Brennan Center, it's unclear whether the Secretary of State or courts can compel certification or certify on behalf of a refusing county. The law just says counties "shall" certify—but doesn't specify what happens if they don't.

This ambiguity is dangerous because it creates opportunities for litigation that could drag past critical deadlines.

Why Haven't We Fixed This?

Part of the answer is that until recently, this wasn't seen as a vulnerability.

County canvassing boards have existed for over a century. They traditionally consisted of professional election administrators or local officials from both parties who saw their role as ministerial—checking that procedures were followed, not making political judgments.

The norm was: if the votes are properly counted, you certify. Personal political preferences don't matter.

But as research from Ohio State University political scientist Edward Foley documents, this norm started eroding after 2020. When officials realized they could refuse certification and face minimal immediate consequences, the system's vulnerability became apparent.

The other issue is that fixing this requires state-by-state legislation. Congress can't simply impose uniform rules on county certification procedures—that's generally considered within state authority over election administration.

What's Being Done?

Some states have taken action since 2020.

Nevada passed legislation requiring county officials to certify results and creating explicit enforcement mechanisms, according to Nevada Assembly Bill 321. Officials who refuse can be removed and replaced.

Michigan clarified that certification is mandatory and created faster court procedures to compel it, as documented in Michigan Election Law amendments from 2023.

But many swing states haven't passed similar protections. Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, Arizona, and North Carolina still have ambiguous procedures for handling certification refusals at the county level.

The 2026 Window

Here's why this matters right now: the county officials who will certify the 2028 presidential election are being selected between now and early 2027.

In states where canvassing board members are appointed by county parties or elected county commissioners, those selections happen with almost no public attention. There's no media coverage of county canvassing board appointments in most places.

But based on the pattern we've seen since 2020, some of these positions are being filled by individuals who've publicly stated they believe the 2020 election was stolen—people who might see refusing certification as their duty rather than a violation of it.

Bottom Line for Democracy Defenders

County canvassing boards are the first formal checkpoint in election certification. They're composed of small groups of local officials with varying levels of legal clarity about their duties. Recent incidents show this stage is vulnerable to politically motivated obstruction.

Key vulnerabilities:

  • Many states have evenly balanced boards that can deadlock
  • Procedures for handling refusals are often ambiguous or slow
  • Board positions are being filled right now with minimal oversight
  • Officials who refused in 2020 faced limited consequences
