The Georgia Runoff System: How a Progressive Reform Created a New Vulnerability for Election Subversion

Epistemic status: High confidence on historical facts and legal requirements; medium confidence on strategic implications for future subversion attempts.

Reading time: 5 minutes

You might have noticed that Georgia keeps having runoff elections. In 2020, both Senate seats went to January runoffs. In 2022, another Senate runoff. This isn't random bad luck—it's baked into Georgia's election rules. And while the runoff system was originally created to protect Black voting power, it's now creating some concerning vulnerabilities for election subversion.

Let me walk you through why this matters for understanding threats to democracy.

How Georgia's Runoff System Actually Works

Georgia requires candidates to win more than 50% of the vote to win a general election. If no candidate hits that threshold, the top two candidates face off in a runoff election.

Here's the timeline: General elections happen in early November. If needed, runoffs happen exactly four weeks later according to Georgia's SB 202, passed in 2021.

That four-week window is important. Before 2021, Georgia gave voters nine weeks between general and runoff elections. The legislature shortened it to four weeks, which creates some serious time pressure we'll come back to.

The Original Purpose: Preventing Racial Minorities from Winning

Georgia adopted its runoff system in 1964, right after the Voting Rights Act began threatening white political control in the South. The timing wasn't coincidental.

The logic was straightforward: In a crowded field with multiple white candidates splitting the white vote, a Black candidate could win with a plurality (say, 40% of the vote). But if you force a runoff between the top two candidates, white voters could consolidate behind a single white candidate and prevent the Black candidate from winning.

The ACLU documented this history in their litigation against Georgia's runoff system. Eight other Southern states adopted similar rules during the same period for the same reason.

Here's the ironic twist: In 2020 and 2022, Georgia's runoff system helped elect two Democratic senators—Raphael Warnock and Jon Ossoff—in part due to increased Black voter turnout. The system designed to prevent Black political power ended up enabling it.

But that same system also creates new vulnerabilities.

The Certification Timeline Problem

Here's where things get concerning. Let me walk through the timeline:

General election: November 5, 2024 (using recent examples)

Counties must certify results: By November 15 (10 days later)

State must certify results: By November 22 (7 days after counties)

Runoff election: December 3 (4 weeks after general)

Runoff certification: December 13

Electoral College votes: December 17

Notice the problem? There's almost no buffer time between when runoff results must be certified and when the Electoral College votes.

In a normal election, there are about five weeks between state certification and Electoral College day. That gives time for recounts, legal challenges, and resolution of disputes. With a runoff, you have maybe four days.

How This Could Be Weaponized

Imagine this scenario in 2028:

Georgia is the tipping-point state for the Electoral College. The presidential race goes to a runoff (maybe a strong third-party candidate prevents anyone from hitting 50%). Now you have a presidential runoff election on December 3, 2028.

A county election board—maybe one controlled by election deniers—delays or refuses to certify the runoff results on December 13. They claim they need to investigate "irregularities."

By the time courts can intervene, it's December 16 or 17. The Electoral College is voting. Georgia might not have certified results to report.

Under federal law, the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 requires states to certify their electors by December 11 (six days before the Electoral College meets). If Georgia can't certify by then due to a runoff, what happens?

The law says governors can provide "conclusive" certification. But if the runoff hasn't been fully certified at the county level, does the governor have legal authority to certify it? Lawyers would argue both sides. Courts would need to resolve it. And you have maybe 48 hours.

This Nearly Happened in 2020

This isn't theoretical. In December 2020, Georgia held Senate runoffs while Trump was actively pressuring Governor Kemp and Secretary of State Raffensperger to overturn the presidential results.

Trump's January 2, 2021 phone call to Raffensperger—where he asked Raffensperger to "find 11,780 votes"—happened just two days after the runoff election, while counties were still certifying those results.

What if Trump had focused his pressure on delaying the runoff certification instead of trying to overturn the November results? The timeline would have been much tighter. Legal challenges would have had days, not weeks, to resolve.

Georgia's institutions held in 2020 because officials like Raffensperger refused to bend. But as we've learned from previous newsletters, you can't rely on individual courage. Systems need to work even when officials are willing to abuse their power.

The Four-Week Window Makes It Worse

Remember how Georgia shortened the runoff period from nine weeks to four weeks in 2021? This makes the vulnerability worse in two ways:

First: Less time means more chaos. Voters have less time to prepare. Election officials have less time to set up polling places. Campaigns have less time to organize. Chaos creates opportunities for disruption.

Second: The compressed timeline means problems in the general election certification can bleed directly into the runoff. If counties are still fighting about November results when the December runoff happens, both processes become entangled.

The New York Times documented how the shortened timeline also reduces early voting from three weeks to one week for runoffs, potentially suppressing turnout and making results more volatile.

Why Georgia Specifically Matters

You might be thinking: "Okay, but how often do elections actually go to runoffs?"

For most statewide races, rarely. But for presidential races in Georgia specifically, it's becoming more plausible:

Georgia is closely divided. In 2020, Biden won by 11,779 votes out of 5 million cast—a margin of 0.2%. In 2024, results were similarly close.

If a credible third-party candidate runs in 2028, they could prevent either major candidate from hitting 50%. This isn't far-fetched—Ross Perot got 19% nationally in 1992, and third-party candidates have been polling higher recently.

Georgia is also likely to be a tipping-point state. It's one of maybe seven genuinely competitive states that will decide the Electoral College.

So you have a closely divided, critical swing state with a runoff system that creates timeline vulnerabilities. That's concerning.

What Makes This Different from Other Vulnerabilities

We've covered various election subversion vulnerabilities in previous newsletters: certification delays, fake electors, pressure on election officials. What makes the runoff vulnerability different?

It's automatic. If no candidate gets 50%, the runoff happens automatically. No one has to engineer it.

It's time-constrained. The federal Electoral College timeline is fixed. You can't extend it without a constitutional amendment.

It's legally murky. The interaction between state runoff requirements and federal Electoral College deadlines has never been tested in a true crisis. Lawyers disagree on what would happen.

It's hard to fix. Georgia could eliminate the runoff system, but that requires the legislature to change the law. Given that the Republican legislature shortened the runoff window in 2021, they're unlikely to eliminate a system that could theoretically help them in a crisis.

What Could Be Done About This

A few possibilities, each with tradeoffs:

1. Eliminate the runoff requirement. Go to a plurality winner system like most states use. But this means candidates could win with 35% of the vote in a crowded field, which has its own democratic legitimacy problems.

2. Use ranked-choice voting. Alaska adopted this in 2020. You hold one election, voters rank candidates, and if no one gets 50%, you eliminate the last-place candidate and redistribute their votes until someone hits 50%. This solves the timeline problem but requires changing voting systems, which is complicated and politically contentious.

3. Extend the runoff window. Go back to nine weeks, or make it even longer. But this creates its own problems—you're essentially running two full election cycles back-to-back, which is expensive and exhausting.

4. Create legal clarity. Pass federal or state legislation explicitly clarifying what happens if a runoff can't be certified before the Electoral College deadline. At minimum, this would reduce legal uncertainty, though it couldn't eliminate timeline pressure.

5. Strengthen certification requirements. Make it clearer that certification is mandatory and can't be delayed for "investigation." The Electoral Count Reform Act tried to do this at the federal level, but state-level requirements matter too.

None of these is perfect. All require political will in a polarized environment.

Bottom Line for Democracy Builders

The Georgia runoff system illustrates a key pattern in election subversion vulnerabilities: Seemingly neutral procedural rules can create opportunities for crisis when combined with other factors.

The runoff system isn't inherently anti-democratic. In fact, it arguably makes elections more democratic by ensuring winners have majority support. But when you combine it with close elections, bad-faith actors, and federal timeline constraints, it becomes a potential pressure point.

This matters for 2028 planning because:

You need to think about timing vulnerabilities. It's not just about what officials can do, but when they can do it. Tight timelines create opportunities for disruption.

State-level procedural rules matter enormously. Georgia's specific runoff rules interact with federal Electoral College deadlines in concerning ways. Every state has its own procedural quirks that could be weaponized.

"Pro-democracy" reforms can have unintended consequences. The runoff system was designed (by its opponents) to protect majority rule. But in practice, it's creating new vulnerabilities. Democracy protection requires thinking through second- and third-order effects.
