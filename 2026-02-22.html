How the National Voter Registration Act Became a Battleground: Understanding the 1993 Law That Was Supposed to Make Registration Easier

Epistemic status: High confidence on the legal and historical facts (based on statute text, court documents, and agency reports). Medium confidence on which interpretation will prevail in ongoing litigation.

There's a law that was supposed to make voting easier that's now being weaponized to make it harder. And understanding how this happened reveals something important about how democratic safeguards can be turned against themselves.

Let me explain.

What the NVRA Actually Says

The National Voter Registration Act of 1993—often called the "Motor Voter Act"—was designed to increase voter registration. According to the Justice Department, it requires states to offer voter registration at DMVs, public assistance agencies, and by mail. The goal was straightforward: make registration accessible.

But buried in Section 8 is a requirement that states maintain "accurate and current" voter registration lists. This means removing people who've died, moved, or become ineligible.

The law includes specific protections. States can't remove voters simply for not voting. They must use a careful process: send a notice, wait through two federal election cycles, and only then remove someone if they haven't voted or confirmed their registration.

Here's where it gets interesting.

The Two Competing Interpretations

Section 8 contains language that's now at the center of multiple legal battles. It says states must make a "reasonable effort" to remove ineligible voters. It also says states can't use programs that "result in" removing eligible voters.

Voting rights advocates argue these provisions mean states must be cautious and follow the careful process outlined in the law. Removal programs should err on the side of keeping eligible voters registered.

But conservative groups like the Public Interest Legal Foundation have a different reading. They've filed lawsuits arguing that "reasonable effort" creates an affirmative obligation to aggressively purge voter rolls. In their view, states that don't remove enough voters are violating the NVRA.

This isn't a theoretical debate.

How This Plays Out in Practice

In 2018, Judicial Watch sued California claiming the state had more registered voters than eligible citizens in some counties. They argued this violated the NVRA's maintenance requirement. California settled and agreed to clean up its rolls.

The Public Interest Legal Foundation has filed similar suits in multiple states. Their 2020 lawsuit against Pennsylvania claimed over 21,000 dead people remained registered. They framed this as a NVRA violation requiring remedy.

Here's the pattern: Use the NVRA's maintenance language to force states to conduct aggressive purges. Then implement those purges in ways that remove eligible voters.

The Virginia Case Study

This dynamic played out dramatically in Virginia in October 2024. Governor Glenn Youngkin issued an executive order requiring daily checks of DMV records to identify potential noncitizens registered to vote.

The program removed over 1,600 voters in the 90 days before the election—violating the NVRA's "quiet period" that prohibits systematic purges close to elections. (This 90-day window exists precisely because mistakes are hard to fix quickly.)

The Justice Department sued. But here's what made it revealing: Evidence showed many removed voters were actually citizens. People had checked the wrong box on DMV forms years earlier. Naturalized citizens hadn't updated their records. The matching process was crude.

The Fourth Circuit ruled the purge violated federal law. But the Supreme Court, in a brief unsigned order, allowed it to continue. No reasoning was provided.

Why This Matters Beyond Virginia

The NVRA controversy reveals a broader pattern in election subversion strategy: taking laws designed to protect voting and reinterpreting them as tools for restriction.

The law says maintain accurate rolls. Sounds reasonable. But "accurate" is being weaponized to justify:

  • Mass challenges based on unreliable data matching
  • Purges that remove eligible voters who made paperwork errors
  • Last-minute removals that can't be corrected before elections
  • Shifting the burden of proof onto voters to prove their eligibility

    • Research from the Brennan Center found that purge rates increased significantly after the 2013 Supreme Court decision in Shelby County v. Holder, which weakened Voting Rights Act protections. Between 2014 and 2016, states previously covered by the Voting Rights Act purged voters at significantly higher rates than other states.

    The NVRA is now part of this arsenal.

    The Database Problem

    Here's something crucial to understand: the "accurate rolls" that sound so reasonable require matching databases that are fundamentally messy.

    A 2012 Pew study found that approximately 24 million voter registrations were inaccurate or no longer valid. That sounds alarming—until you realize it's mostly people who moved or died, and the system is slow to update.

    But matching records across databases is error-prone:

    Common names get flagged as duplicates. Someone named "John Smith" who moved might appear as two different people—or two different John Smiths might appear as one person.

    Missing middle names or initials cause mismatches. DMV records might have "Robert J. Garcia" while voter rolls have "Robert Garcia."

    Typos accumulate over time. One database has a birthdate of 1978, another has 1987.

    When you run an automated matching program across millions of records, even a 1% error rate means tens of thousands of eligible voters flagged for removal.

    The Supreme Court's 2018 Signal

    In Husted v. A. Philip Randolph Institute (2018), the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that Ohio's voter purge process didn't violate the NVRA, even though it used failure to vote as a trigger to begin the removal process.

    Justice Alito's majority opinion emphasized that the NVRA requires maintenance of accurate rolls. Not voting, combined with not responding to a notice, could justify removal.

    But Justice Sotomayor's dissent highlighted the practical impact: the process disproportionately affected low-income and minority voters in Democratic-leaning neighborhoods. In Cleveland, Hamilton County, and Columbus, voters in Democratic-leaning areas were purged at twice the rate of those in Republican-leaning areas.

    This decision opened the door. If failure to vote can trigger removal processes, the NVRA's "reasonable effort" language can justify increasingly aggressive programs.

    What This Means for 2026 and 2028

    We're likely to see more NVRA-based litigation demanding aggressive purges, especially in swing states. The pattern will be:

    File suit claiming the state isn't maintaining accurate rolls (NVRA violation). Pressure the state to implement a purge program. Design that program to disproportionately affect likely Democratic voters (but frame it as neutral maintenance). Implement it close enough to elections that removed voters can't easily re-register.

    The 90-day quiet period is supposed to prevent this. But as Virginia showed, that protection is now uncertain.

    A ProPublica investigation in 2024 documented how True the Vote and similar organizations were training activists to file mass voter challenges using NVRA maintenance language as justification. The trainings explicitly discussed overwhelming election offices with challenges and using the NVRA as legal cover.

    States that don't aggressively defend against these suits—or that have election-denier officials who welcome them—could see significant purges in late 2027 heading into 2028.

    The Competing Visions

    At its core, this debate is about two different visions of election integrity:

    Vision 1: Integrity means ensuring every eligible voter can vote. Registration should be easy and errors should be corrected. Removals should be careful and conservative.

    Vision 2: Integrity means ensuring no ineligible person votes. Registration should require verification and records should be aggressively maintained. Purges should be thorough even if some eligible voters are removed.

    Both sides claim the NVRA supports their vision. The law's ambiguous language—"reasonable effort" to remove ineligible voters while not "resulting in" removal of eligible ones—allows both interpretations.

    But the practical effect is asymmetric. Aggressive purges disproportionately affect eligible voters who are poor, mobile, or don't speak English as a first language. They tend to affect younger voters and renters more than older homeowners. The errors fall in predictable patterns.

    The Bottom Line

    The National Voter Registration Act was passed to expand access to voting. Thirty years later, it's being used to justify removing eligible voters from rolls.

    This matters for 2026-2028 because:

    Expect more litigation: Conservative legal groups will file NVRA suits demanding purges in swing states, potentially in late 2027. States will face pressure to implement aggressive removal programs or defend against claims they're violating federal law.

    The 90-day protection is weakened: The Supreme Court's Virginia ruling suggests the quiet period before elections may not be strongly enforced. This removes a key safeguard against last-minute purges that can't be corrected.

    Database matching is the mechanism: Understanding that "accurate rolls" requires matching messy databases is crucial. Any purge program relying on automated matching will remove eligible voters. The question is how many, and whether there's time to fix it.

    State-level defenses matter: Secretaries of state and governors who resist pressure to implement aggressive purges are critical. But they need to be elected first—making 2026 state races crucial.

    What Could Help

    Automatic re-registration: Some states automatically re-register voters who were purged if they show up to vote. This doesn't prevent the inconvenience but reduces disenfranchisement.

    Real-time notification: Voters should be notified immediately when they're removed from rolls, not weeks later. Text and email notification systems could help.

    Burden of proof: Laws could require states to prove a voter is ineligible before removal, rather than making voters prove they're eligible to be restored.

    Better databases: Investment in accurate, interoperable voter registration databases would reduce the matching errors that enable both legitimate mistakes and intentional abuse.
