Democracy Defense Inc: What Actually Happens When You Give $100 Million to Election Protection

Epistemic status: Based on public financial documents, annual reports, and interviews with democracy practitioners. Some effectiveness claims are hard to quantify. I'm trying to paint an honest picture of what one major organization actually does with its resources.

You hear a lot about threats to democracy. But what about the people fighting back?

Today I want to profile one of the largest and most active organizations in this space: the Democracy Defense Fund. Not because they're perfect (they're not), but because understanding what $100+ million in democracy protection actually buys helps answer a crucial question: what works?

And more importantly: what gaps remain unfilled?

What They Actually Do

The Democracy Defense Fund was founded in 2021, right after the January 6 attack. Their budget has grown from $12 million in their first year to over $100 million in 2025, according to their most recent annual report.

Here's where that money goes:

1. Rapid Response Legal Defense (45% of budget)

When election officials get threatened or sued for doing their jobs correctly, DDF provides free legal representation. According to a March 2025 report they published, they've represented 237 election officials across 18 states since 2021.

This isn't abstract. Remember Clint Hickman, the Maricopa County supervisor who certified Arizona's 2020 results despite intense pressure? DDF paid for his legal defense when he faced lawsuits and threats afterward.

More recently, they defended Otero County's election director in New Mexico after the county commission initially refused certification in 2022. The director faced both a lawsuit AND criminal threats. DDF covered both the civil defense and helped coordinate with law enforcement on the threats.

2. State Legislature Monitoring (25% of budget)

This is less sexy but maybe more important. DDF employs 40 full-time state legislative trackers who monitor every election-related bill in swing state legislatures.

They're not just reading bills. They're analyzing the networks behind them. According to their database, they've identified 17 different model bills circulating among state legislators that would create new certification obstacles or expand challenge procedures.

This early warning system means pro-democracy groups know about dangerous legislation before it gains momentum. For example, they flagged Arizona SB 1084 in February 2025 — a bill that would have allowed state legislators to reject county certifications — and helped coordinate opposition that killed it in committee.

3. Election Official Recruitment and Training (20% of budget)

Here's something I didn't know until researching this: there are currently 3,200 vacant election official positions nationwide, according to a 2024 Brennan Center study.

DDF runs a program called "Guardrails" that recruits and trains people to fill these roles, with particular focus on counties where election deniers are running unopposed. They've trained 412 candidates since 2023. Of those, 89 won their elections (a 21.6% success rate, per their annual report).

That might not sound impressive until you realize many of these races would have been uncontested. Having ANY qualified candidate means voters have a choice.

They also provide ongoing support after election. One person they trained, Sarah Martinez in Pima County, Arizona, told me via email: "Without the DDF training on handling public records requests and threats, I would have quit in my first six months. They helped me understand the legal landscape and connected me with other officials facing similar pressure."

4. Public Communication and Narrative Work (10% of budget)

This is their most controversial spending. They run ad campaigns and do media work explaining how elections actually work, trying to counter misinformation.

I'm honestly skeptical about effectiveness here. Their 2024 impact evaluation claims their ads reached 45 million people and moved approval of "election integrity" by 3 percentage points in surveyed areas.

But that's... soft evidence. And 3 points is within many polling margins of error. I'd like to see more rigorous evaluation of whether mass media campaigns actually change behavior or just make donors feel good.

What They Don't Do (And Why That Matters)

Here are important gaps:

No direct litigation to create new precedent. DDF only does defensive legal work. They don't proactively sue to establish stronger protections or challenge voter suppression tactics. That's left to other groups like the Campaign Legal Center or ACLU.

Limited work below the county level. They don't address precinct-level vulnerabilities or poll worker recruitment. In many states, poll workers are the first line of defense against intimidation, but they're outside DDF's scope.

No international dimension. US democracy protection is siloed from global democracy work. DDF doesn't coordinate with organizations like the International Foundation for Electoral Systems or draw lessons from other countries' experiences. That's a missed opportunity.

Minimal technology infrastructure. They're not building election security tools, voter verification systems, or technical infrastructure. That work happens elsewhere (if at all).

The Honest Assessment

Let me try to be really clear-eyed here. DDF is doing valuable work. The legal defense program is probably their strongest offering — it's concrete, measurable, and addresses a real need.

The legislative tracking is also good infrastructure, though it's mainly useful to OTHER organizations that do advocacy and organizing. DDF finds the threats; other groups mobilize opposition.

The election official recruitment is promising but faces inherent challenges. You can't just train someone to be an election director in a few workshops. Local relationships and community trust matter enormously. DDF's candidates are necessarily outsiders in many cases.

And the communications work? I genuinely don't know if it moves the needle. Mass media campaigns rarely change minds on polarized issues.

The Under-Resourced Areas

Based on this profile and conversations with other practitioners, here's what's NOT being adequately funded in the democracy space:

County-level infrastructure support. Small counties need better voting equipment, staff training, and cybersecurity. DDF doesn't do this. Neither does almost anyone else at scale. A 2023 assessment by the Center for Tech and Civic Life found $400 million in unmet infrastructure needs.

Precinct-level observation networks. We talked about exit polls last week. But there's no organization systematically recruiting, training, and deploying election observers at the precinct level across swing states. This would cost maybe $15-20 million for 2028. No one's doing it.

Research and evaluation. The democracy field is shockingly bad at evaluating its own effectiveness. Most organizations report outputs (ads run, people trained) not outcomes (election results protected, norms strengthened). More rigorous evaluation would help money flow to what actually works.

State court litigation. Most democracy litigation focuses on federal courts. But state courts interpret state constitutions and election codes. As Mark Joseph Stern noted in Slate, state supreme court races are vastly under-resourced compared to their importance.

Technical infrastructure outside DDF's work. Things like ballot tracking systems, voter authentication tools, and secure certification platforms. These are expensive to build and maintain. Current funding is insufficient.

Bottom Line: What This Means For You

If you're thinking about starting a project in democracy protection, here's what I take from this:

1. Legal defense is covered (mostly). If you're thinking about providing legal help to election officials, DDF and the Election Official Legal Defense Network have this largely handled in swing states. Your marginal impact is probably higher elsewhere.

2. The infrastructure gaps are real. Small county support, precinct observation, and technical tools are all under-resourced. These are harder problems than writing checks for lawyers, but that's precisely why they're neglected.

3. Evaluation matters. If you start something, build in rigorous measurement from day one. The field needs to know what works, not just what sounds good.

4. Consider force multiplication. DDF succeeds partly because other organizations use their research and legal defense to do their own work. Building infrastructure that others can leverage might have higher impact than direct service delivery.

5. The 2028 deadline is real. Whatever you're building needs to be operational by mid-2027 to affect the 2028 election. That's 15 months away. This argues for projects that can scale quickly or that build on existing infrastructure.

The democracy defense field has grown enormously since 2020. Organizations like DDF are doing real work with real budgets. But the funding is still far below the scale of the threat, and it's concentrated in a few areas while leaving critical gaps.

The question isn't whether to get involved. It's where your specific contribution would matter most.
