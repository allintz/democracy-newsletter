The Curiel Playbook: How Judicial Delegitimization Threatens Election Dispute Resolution

Epistemic status: High confidence on documented events; moderate confidence on future implications.

In May 2016, Donald Trump attacked Judge Gonzalo Curiel, who was overseeing the Trump University fraud case, claiming Curiel couldn't be impartial because of his "Mexican heritage." Trump repeatedly called him a "Mexican" (Curiel was born in Indiana) and suggested his rulings were biased due to Trump's immigration positions.

This wasn't just campaign rhetoric. It was a test run for something more dangerous.

Why This Matters for 2028

Here's the thing most people missed: Trump's Curiel attacks established a playbook for delegitimizing judicial review of election disputes before they even happen.

The basic logic goes like this: If you can convince your supporters that certain judges are inherently biased and illegitimate, then their rulings in election cases don't need to be followed. You're not defying the law—you're resisting a "corrupt" system.

According to NPR's contemporaneous reporting, Trump said Curiel had "an absolute conflict" and should recuse himself. House Speaker Paul Ryan called the comments "the textbook definition of a racist comment," but Trump didn't back down. He'd found something that worked with his base.

The 2020 Application

Fast forward to November 2020. Trump and his allies filed dozens of election lawsuits across swing states. When they lost—repeatedly—they didn't accept the judicial process as legitimate.

Instead, they attacked the judges themselves. According to analysis by the Brennan Center, Trump's team filed 62 election lawsuits and lost 61 of them. But rather than acknowledge defeat, Trump allies began categorizing judges as "Trump judges" versus "Obama judges" versus "establishment Republicans who betrayed us."

The logic was simple: Conservative judges who ruled against Trump weren't following the law—they were cowards afraid of the media. Liberal judges who ruled against Trump were partisan hacks. The only legitimate rulings were ones that went Trump's way.

Chief Justice John Roberts pushed back on this framework back in 2018 when Trump first deployed it, saying "we do not have Obama judges or Trump judges, Bush judges or Clinton judges." But the delegitimization campaign continued.

The Statistical Pattern Nobody's Tracking

Here's what worries me: There's no systematic database tracking pre-election attacks on judges who are likely to hear election disputes.

Think about it. We know which judges will probably handle certification challenges in each swing state. They're sitting judges on state supreme courts and federal district courts right now. Their names are public. Their backgrounds are public.

And yet, according to research by the Brennan Center published in 2022, we're not monitoring whether these judges are being systematically delegitimized before election disputes even arise.

In Arizona, the state supreme court currently has a 4-3 conservative majority. In Wisconsin, it recently flipped to a 4-3 liberal majority with Justice Janet Protasiewicz's election in 2023. In Pennsylvania, the supreme court has a 5-2 Democratic majority.

These courts will likely decide close election challenges in 2028. Are the judges already being targeted for delegitimization campaigns? We don't know because nobody's systematically tracking it.

How Delegitimization Actually Works

The Curiel playbook has three stages:

Stage 1: Establish the "bias" narrative early. This happened with Curiel months before any ruling. Trump didn't wait for an adverse decision—he preemptively declared the judge compromised.

Stage 2: Make it about identity and loyalty, not law. Curiel's heritage. "Obama judges." "Soros-backed prosecutors." The argument is that these officials cannot be impartial because of who they are, not because of what they've ruled.

Stage 3: Give supporters permission to disregard rulings. Once you've established a judge is "biased," their decisions don't carry moral weight. Following them becomes optional—a strategic choice rather than a legal obligation.

The 2022 Warning Sign

This isn't theoretical. In Otero County, New Mexico in 2022 (which I covered previously), county commissioners refused to certify election results partly because they claimed to distrust the voting machines. But according to reporting by the Santa Fe New Mexican, they also explicitly questioned whether state courts could force them to comply.

Commissioner Couy Griffin said the commission would "let the courts figure it out." When courts did rule against them, ordering certification, the commissioners complied—but grudgingly, while maintaining they disagreed with the court's authority.

That's the transitional phase. The next step is outright defiance.

The International Precedent

We've seen this playbook succeed elsewhere. According to Human Rights Watch's 2019 report on Venezuela, Nicolás Maduro systematically delegitimized the judiciary by packing courts with loyalists and then declaring opposition-aligned judges as "illegitimate." When these judges ruled against the regime, the government simply ignored the rulings.

The key was creating parallel legitimacy structures. Maduro supporters would say "we follow the real courts" (the packed ones), while opposition supporters recognized the independent judges. Eventually, judicial rulings became meaningless—just another front in partisan warfare.

What Makes US Courts Vulnerable

Here's the uncomfortable truth: The US judiciary's legitimacy rests almost entirely on voluntary compliance and public trust.

Courts have no army. No police force. Their power comes from the assumption that people will follow their orders because courts are seen as neutral arbiters of law.

Alexander Hamilton wrote in Federalist 78 that the judiciary "has no influence over either the sword or the purse" and "must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments."

What happens when a state governor or secretary of state decides a court ruling in an election dispute is "illegitimate"? What if they've spent months telling supporters that the judges are "radical activists" or "partisan hacks"?

The Constitution provides for federal court review. But as we saw in the travel ban cases in 2017, there are limits to how quickly federal courts can act, especially when state officials are implementing state election law.

The 2028 Scenario

Imagine this: It's December 2028. Pennsylvania's election results show the Democratic candidate won by 35,000 votes—closer than 2020, but clear. Several county canvassing boards in Republican-controlled counties refuse to certify, citing "irregularities."

The Pennsylvania Supreme Court—which has a Democratic-appointed majority—orders certification. The county boards comply under protest, but the Republican secretary of state (elected in 2026) refuses to certify the statewide results.

The secretary claims the state supreme court is "partisan" and "biased" and says he's following "the law as properly interpreted." He's been making this argument for months on conservative media. His supporters believe him.

What happens next? Federal courts could intervene, but that takes time—and the Electoral College deadline is approaching. Does the governor certify a different set of results? Do competing slates of electors get sent to Congress?

The Gap Nobody's Filling

Here's what's missing: Proactive judicial legitimacy protection.

We have election protection organizations. Voter access groups. Election administration support. But I can't find any organization systematically working to defend judicial legitimacy in election contexts before disputes arise.

Nobody's:

  • Tracking which judges are being targeted for delegitimization campaigns
  • Conducting rapid-response fact-checking of attacks on judges' backgrounds
  • Building public understanding of judicial independence and why it matters
  • Creating contingency plans for scenarios where state officials defy court orders

    • The closest thing is the Brennan Center's work on judicial elections and court reforms, but that's focused on judicial selection processes, not on defending sitting judges from coordinated delegitimization.

    Why This Is Hard

    There's a genuine dilemma here. Courts need to be above politics. Having advocacy groups "defend" judges risks making them look partisan.

    But silence in the face of coordinated delegitimization campaigns isn't neutrality—it's vulnerability.

    When Trump attacked Curiel, the American Bar Association issued a statement saying his comments "threaten the fundamental principle of judicial independence." But that was a professional organization's formal statement, not a public campaign to counter the delegitimization narrative.

    Most voters never heard the ABA's response. They heard Trump's attacks, repeated constantly.

    What Actually Might Work

    I'm genuinely uncertain about solutions here, but some possibilities:

    1. Judicial legitimacy monitoring networks. Track attacks on judges likely to hear election cases. Document patterns. Create rapid response when delegitimization campaigns start.

    2. Public education campaigns about judicial independence. Most Americans don't understand how courts work or why judicial independence matters. That's a vulnerability that can be exploited.

    3. Contingency planning for judicial defiance scenarios. War-game what happens if a state official refuses to comply with a court order in an election dispute. Identify legal and procedural responses in advance.

    4. Stronger enforcement mechanisms. Some states have criminal contempt statutes that could apply to officials who defy court orders. But prosecution takes time—probably too much time during an election crisis.

    The Harder Question

    There's a deeper issue: What if some judges actually are partisan?

    The Curiel attacks were baseless—Curiel was a widely respected federal judge following standard procedures. But judicial elections have become increasingly politicized. Some state supreme court races now feature heavy spending by partisan groups.

    According to the Brennan Center's 2022 analysis, spending in state supreme court races hit record levels, with interest groups pouring money into races that might determine redistricting and voting rights cases.

    If voters believe some judges are chosen specifically to deliver partisan outcomes, how do we rebuild institutional trust? Just saying "trust the courts" won't work if the judicial selection process itself looks captured.

    I don't have a good answer to this. But pretending the problem doesn't exist makes it worse.
