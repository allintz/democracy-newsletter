How Ballot Access Rules Became a Stealth Mechanism for Democratic Gatekeeping

Today we're diving into something that sounds boring but turns out to be surprisingly important: the rules for getting on the ballot in the first place.

I used to think ballot access was simple—if you want to run for office, you file some paperwork and you're good to go. But it turns out that the US has some of the most restrictive ballot access rules in the democratic world, and these rules have occasionally been weaponized to keep certain candidates or parties off ballots entirely.

This matters because while we've spent a lot of time discussing how officials might refuse to certify legitimate election results, there's a whole separate vulnerability: preventing certain candidates from appearing on ballots in the first place. It's a form of election manipulation that happens earlier in the process, which makes it harder to detect and reverse.

Let me walk you through how this works and why it's relevant for thinking about 2026 and 2028.

The Basic Framework: How Hard Is It to Get on a Ballot?

In most established democracies, getting on the ballot is fairly straightforward. In the UK, you pay a small deposit (£500) that you get back if you receive more than 5% of the vote. In Germany, you need minimal signatures and party registration.

In the US, it's dramatically more complex. Requirements vary wildly by state, and they can be quite onerous.

For presidential candidates, according to Ballotpedia's comprehensive survey, requirements range from just filing paperwork in some states to needing tens of thousands of signatures in others. In California, a minor party presidential candidate needs 196,964 valid signatures. In Florida, they need 145,040.

These aren't just "get some signatures" numbers. According to research by Richard Winger, a leading expert on ballot access laws, campaigns typically need to collect 2-3 times the stated requirement because many signatures get invalidated for technical reasons (wrong address format, signature doesn't match registration, person moved, etc.).

That means a minor party candidate in California might need to collect nearly 400,000 signatures to be confident of ballot access. That requires serious organizational capacity and resources.

The Historical Pattern: Ballot Access as Political Weapon

Here's where this gets more concerning. Ballot access rules haven't been stable—they've been repeatedly tightened, often in response to third-party challenges to major party dominance.

After George Wallace's American Independent Party won 13.5% of the national vote in 1968, many states substantially increased their ballot access requirements. As documented in Winger's analysis for the Election Law Journal, between 1968 and 1980, the number of states requiring over 5,000 signatures for a new party to gain ballot access increased significantly.

The pattern is clear: when outsider candidates perform well, the rules get tightened. This isn't necessarily nefarious—defenders argue it prevents ballot clutter and frivolous candidates. But it creates a ratchet effect where ballot access gets progressively harder.

More directly manipulative cases exist too. In 2020, according to reporting by Wisconsin Public Radio, Republicans in Wisconsin actually helped the Green Party gather signatures to get on the ballot, calculating that Green Party presence would split votes away from Biden. When the signatures were challenged for technical violations, the Wisconsin Supreme Court ruled 4-3 to keep the Green Party off the ballot.

This case is fascinating because both sides were trying to manipulate ballot access for partisan advantage—Republicans to add a party, Democrats to remove it. The ballot became a strategic game board rather than a neutral platform.

The Trump 2024 Cases: Ballot Access as Anti-Subversion Tool

Now here's where this gets especially relevant to our focus on democratic threats.

In 2023-2024, several states saw efforts to remove Donald Trump from presidential ballots using Section 3 of the 14th Amendment, which bars from office anyone who "engaged in insurrection" after taking an oath to support the Constitution.

The most prominent case was Trump v. Anderson in Colorado. According to the Colorado Supreme Court's December 2023 ruling, the court found that Trump had engaged in insurrection within the meaning of Section 3 and ordered him removed from the Colorado primary ballot.

Maine's Secretary of State made a similar determination shortly after, removing Trump from Maine's primary ballot.

The US Supreme Court reversed both decisions in March 2024, ruling unanimously in Trump v. Anderson that states cannot disqualify candidates from federal office under Section 3 without Congressional legislation establishing the enforcement mechanism.

I bring this up not to re-litigate those cases, but to show how ballot access rules can cut both ways. They can be:

  • Defensive democratic tools: Keeping candidates who allegedly attempted to subvert democracy off ballots
  • Offensive authoritarian tools: Keeping opposition candidates off ballots through technical rules

    • The same mechanism can serve opposite purposes depending on who's wielding it and why.

    Why This Creates Dangerous Vulnerabilities for 2026-2028

    Let me connect this to our ongoing concern about election subversion attempts.

    Imagine we're in October 2028. A contentious presidential race is underway. Now imagine that election officials in several swing states—perhaps newly elected secretaries of state from the 2026 elections—find technical reasons to remove a major party candidate from the ballot.

    Maybe they claim signature fraud in the petition process. Maybe they cite a technical violation of state election law. Maybe they invoke some creative interpretation of candidate eligibility requirements.

    The candidate would have legal recourse, of course. But here's the problem: ballot printing deadlines are real and immovable. According to guidance from the Election Assistance Commission, many states must finalize and begin printing ballots 45-60 days before the election to allow time for overseas and military voters.

    That means ballot access challenges in September or early October could create genuine time-pressure crises. Courts would need to rule quickly. Emergency appeals would be filed. The whole process would be compressed into days or weeks.

    And here's the kicker: unlike certification refusals (which happen after voting and are clearly contrary to voter intent), ballot access denials happen before voting. There's no "will of the voters" to point to yet. Officials can claim they're just enforcing eligibility rules, not overriding election results.

    The International Comparison: Russia's "Administrative Resources"

    This isn't hypothetical. We have clear examples from other countries of how ballot access manipulation works in practice.

    In Russia, according to research by political scientist Ora John Reuter published in the Journal of Democracy, the regime doesn't primarily rely on ballot stuffing or vote count manipulation (though those happen). Instead, it uses "administrative resources" to control who gets on ballots in the first place.

    Opposition candidates face selective enforcement of registration requirements, signature challenges, technical disqualifications, and legal harassment. According to reporting by The Moscow Times, in the 2021 parliamentary elections, hundreds of opposition candidates were barred from running through various administrative mechanisms.

    The brilliance (from an authoritarian perspective) is that each individual disqualification looks technical and legal. "They didn't file the right paperwork." "Their signatures were invalid." "They missed a deadline."

    The pattern only becomes clear when you zoom out and see that these "technical" problems consistently affect opposition candidates while ruling party candidates sail through the same process.

    What Makes the US Vulnerable (and Not Vulnerable)

    So how worried should we be about ballot access manipulation in the US?

    Let me break this down into structural factors:

    Protective factors:

  • Federal candidates, federal courts: Presidential and Congressional candidates can access federal courts quickly, which are less susceptible to state-level political pressure
  • Media attention: Major candidate disqualifications would generate massive coverage and scrutiny
  • Multiple decision points: Ballot access typically involves multiple officials and review stages, not single decision-makers
  • Established major parties: The Democratic and Republican parties have automatic ballot access in most states; these rules mainly affect third parties

    • Vulnerability factors:

  • Decentralized authority: Ballot access is determined state-by-state with different officials and rules, creating many potential pressure points
  • Complex technical requirements: The signature-matching, deadline, and paperwork requirements are genuinely complicated, providing legitimate-looking pretexts for challenges
  • Compressed timelines: Ballot printing deadlines create genuine time pressure that favors whoever acts first
  • Partisan officials: Secretaries of state and election boards making ballot access decisions are often partisan elected officials or appointees

    • The 2026 secretary of state races are particularly important here. According to analysis by States United Democracy Center, several swing states will elect secretaries of state who will have authority over ballot access determinations for 2028.

    A Specific Scenario to Watch

    Let me paint a specific scenario that concerns me, based on what we know about 2020-2022 attempts:

    Imagine that in early 2028, a presidential candidate faces coordinated ballot access challenges in multiple swing states. The challenges cite technical violations—signature irregularities, missed deadlines, procedural errors in petition gathering.

    Each challenge, viewed individually, looks plausibly legitimate. But viewed together, they form a pattern. They all emerge simultaneously. They all target the same candidate. They all come from officials or groups aligned with the opposing party.

    Courts would need to rule on multiple cases simultaneously under extreme time pressure. Some might rule for the candidate, others against. The legal landscape would be fragmented and unclear.

    Now it's October 15, 2028. Ballots are being printed. What appears on them?

    The scenario is worrying because it combines:

  • Distributed action (multiple states, harder to coordinate opposition)
  • Technical legitimacy (real rules being enforced, not obvious lawlessness)
  • Time pressure (hard deadlines favor whoever acts first)
  • Legal ambiguity (ballot access law is genuinely complex and varies by state)

    • It's the Brooks Brothers Riot principle applied earlier in the process: create chaos and confusion at a decision point with hard deadlines, and the confusion itself becomes a form of obstruction.

    What Actually Happened With RFK Jr. in 2024

    We actually have a recent test case that illustrates some of these dynamics.

    In 2024, Robert F. Kennedy Jr. ran as an independent presidential candidate. According to comprehensive reporting by Politico, he faced ballot access challenges in multiple states, with both Democrats and Republicans filing legal challenges depending on which party thought his presence would hurt them more.

    In New York, a judge ruled that Kennedy had falsely claimed a New York address on his petition and removed him from the ballot, finding he actually lived in California. Kennedy had claimed residence at a friend's house in Westchester County where he admittedly didn't live.

    This case is instructive because:

  • The technical violation was real (he did falsely claim residence)
  • The challenge was politically motivated (Democrats filed it to help Biden/Harris)
  • The legal process worked reasonably well (courts ruled on merits, appeals were heard)
  • But timing mattered enormously (the ruling came in August, creating serious complications)

    • Kennedy ultimately withdrew from the race in August 2024 and endorsed Trump, but the ballot access fights continued even after his withdrawal, with Kennedy trying to remove himself from ballots in some states while staying on them in others.

    The whole saga illustrated how complex and manipulable ballot access processes can be, even when courts are trying to rule fairly on the merits.

    Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Protection

    Here's my synthesis of what ballot access vulnerabilities mean for thinking about 2026-2028:

    The core insight: Ballot access manipulation is a form of election subversion that happens before voting, making it harder to rally public opposition ("the voters have spoken") and easier to justify through technical rule enforcement.

    The mechanism: Complex, state-level signature and paperwork requirements create multiple potential failure points that can be exploited through selective enforcement, coordinated challenges, and time-pressure tactics.

    The 2026 connection: Secretary of state races and state supreme court races in 2026 will determine who has authority over ballot access decisions in 2028. These down-ballot races deserve much more attention than they typically receive.

    The preparedness question: Unlike certification processes (which have received extensive legal and procedural attention since 2020), ballot access vulnerabilities haven't been systematically addressed. The rules remain complex and vary widely by state.

    Project idea for readers: There's a real opportunity for someone to create a "Ballot Access Early Warning System"—a monitoring project that tracks ballot access challenges in real-time, identifies patterns, and rapidly mobilizes legal resources when coordinated manipulation is detected. This would be similar to election protection hotlines but focused specifically on the pre-election ballot access phase.

    The existing organization Protect Democracy does some of this work, but there's room for a specialized project focused exclusively on ballot access monitoring and rapid response.

    The key lesson from authoritarian playbooks is that election manipulation is most effective when it happens early in the process, looks technical rather than political, and occurs across multiple jurisdictions simultaneously. Ballot access rules check all those boxes.

    We need to be watching these processes as carefully as we watch certification—maybe more carefully, since ballot access problems are harder to reverse once the election is underway.
