The Liar's Dividend: How AI Makes Everyone Doubt Everything

Epistemic status: High confidence on the core concept and documented examples. Medium confidence on how this will play out in 2028, because we're in genuinely unprecedented territory.

There's a paradox at the heart of AI and democracy that I've been thinking about lately. It's not what most people focus on.

Yes, AI can create incredibly convincing fake videos of candidates saying things they never said. Yes, deepfakes are getting better every month. But here's the thing: the most dangerous effect of AI on democracy might not be the fakes themselves.

It's that real things can now be dismissed as fake.

What political scientists call "the liar's dividend"

In 2018, researchers at Yale Law School published a paper introducing a term that's become increasingly important: the "liar's dividend." The concept is straightforward but devastating.

When the public knows that realistic fake audio and video can be created, any politician caught on camera doing something bad can claim it's a deepfake. Even if it's completely real.

The researchers, Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron, described it this way: "The mere existence of deepfake technology, and the public's awareness of it, may make it easier for liars to dismiss real evidence as fake."

This isn't hypothetical anymore.

How this is already being weaponized

In 2018, Gabon President Ali Bongo appeared in a video after weeks of absence following a stroke. Opposition leaders immediately claimed the video was a deepfake, according to The Guardian. This led to an attempted coup. French intelligence later verified the video was real, but the damage was done.

In Malaysia during the 2018 election, when a video surfaced appearing to show a candidate having sex, he claimed it was a deepfake - even though experts who examined the video found no evidence of manipulation, according to the Straits Times.

More recently in the US: in July 2023, when a video showed Florida Governor Ron DeSantis in an awkward exchange with voters, his campaign manager suggested without evidence that the video might be manipulated, Politico reported.

The pattern is clear. When video evidence of wrongdoing emerges, the first line of defense is increasingly "that's AI-generated."

Why this is especially dangerous for election subversion

Think about how this intersects with what we've covered in previous newsletters about certification and evidence standards.

Imagine this scenario in 2028: Election night in Pennsylvania. Early returns show Candidate A ahead. Then late-counted mail ballots shift the result to Candidate B.

A video surfaces showing election workers in Philadelphia allegedly mishandling ballots. Traditional news outlets verify it's real. Security footage timestamps match. Multiple sources confirm.

But Candidate A's supporters immediately flood social media claiming it's an AI fake. Not because they have evidence - but because they can. State legislators sympathetic to Candidate A start raising "concerns about AI-manipulated evidence." County canvassing boards in Republican areas cite "questions about the integrity of video evidence" as they refuse to certify.

The video is real. But in a world where everyone knows AI can fake anything, how do you prove that?

The epistemological crisis this creates

Here's what makes this so insidious: it's not really about the technology. It's about certainty.

Democracy requires a shared reality. Not shared opinions - we can disagree about policy. But shared facts. When Trump called Brad Raffensperger asking him to "find 11,780 votes," we had audio. When fake electors signed fraudulent certificates, we had the documents.

AI doesn't have to successfully fool everyone with fakes. It just has to make everyone uncertain about everything.

Political scientist Thomas Rid wrote in Foreign Affairs that "the mere existence of deepfake technology will make it easier to cast doubt on real recordings and videos, enabling people to deny saying or doing something they actually did."

This is what philosophers call an epistemological crisis - a breakdown in our ability to know things.

The verification arms race (and why it's not enough)

You might think: can't we just get better at detecting fakes?

Yes and no. Detection technology is improving. Research from UC Berkeley published in 2024 showed promising results using biological signals in video.

But there are three problems.

First, detection is always playing catch-up. By the time detectors catch one generation of fakes, the next generation is already better.

Second, even if detection is 99% accurate, who decides which detector to trust? If a county election board doesn't like what video evidence shows, they can find an "expert" to say it's fake. We've seen this playbook with everything from COVID to climate change.

Third, and most importantly: the goal isn't to fool the detectors. It's to create enough confusion that politically motivated actors can justify whatever they were going to do anyway.

What makes 2028 particularly vulnerable

We're hitting a perfect storm of factors:

Timing: The next presidential election is less than three years away. AI video generation has improved dramatically just in the past year. By 2028, creating convincing fakes will be trivial.

Infrastructure: As we covered in previous newsletters, many states still lack clear mechanisms to compel certification when county boards refuse. If those boards cite "concerns about AI manipulation" of evidence, existing law often doesn't clearly resolve it.

Precedent: The 2020 attempts to block certification created a roadmap. Adding "this evidence might be AI-generated" to that playbook makes it superficially more credible.

Asymmetry: This tactic helps whoever wants to dispute election results. It doesn't matter if you're ahead or behind. If you're behind, you claim the evidence of your loss is fake. If you're ahead, you claim any evidence of irregularities is real and any debunking is fake.

The deeper problem: AI as an excuse

Here's what I think is most important to understand: the technical details of deepfakes might be less relevant than the excuse they provide.

Between 2020 and 2022, officials who wanted to block certification needed to make up reasons. As we covered in the February 24 newsletter, they cited vague "irregularities" or "concerns."

Now they have a ready-made excuse that sounds sophisticated: "In an era of AI, we need to be extra careful about any video or audio evidence."

It sounds responsible. It sounds prudent. It's very hard to argue against publicly without sounding like you're being cavalier about a real risk.

But it's actually just a more polished version of the same bad-faith obstruction.

What this means for pro-democracy work

If the liar's dividend is the real threat, technical solutions alone won't solve it.

Some implications:

Evidence redundancy matters more: Single pieces of evidence - even video - can be disputed. Multiple independent sources, paper trails, and corroborating witnesses become crucial. This suggests election administration should prioritize creating multiple overlapping evidence streams.

Authentication infrastructure becomes critical: Not just detecting fakes, but affirmatively proving authenticity. Chain of custody for video. Cryptographic signatures. Timestamp verification from multiple sources.

Speed is essential: The longer the gap between an event and verification, the more time for doubt to spread. This suggests election administration needs rapid-response authentication capabilities.

Legal frameworks need updating: Election law currently doesn't contemplate "this real evidence might be fake" as a basis for refusing certification. Courts need clear standards for what constitutes sufficient proof of authenticity.

The optimistic case

There is a counter-argument worth taking seriously.

Maybe the solution is simpler than it seems: overwhelming evidence is hard to fake convincingly. Creating one deepfake video is getting easier. Creating 50 consistent pieces of evidence - video, audio, documents, witness testimony, security footage, metadata - that all tell the same story is still very hard.

And people might be smarter about this than we fear. Research from MIT published in 2020 found that with minimal training, people got significantly better at detecting deepfakes.

The liar's dividend only works if people are completely unable to distinguish real from fake. If the public develops reasonable heuristics - trust multiple independent sources, be skeptical of claims without corroboration, look for implausible details - the strategy might fail.

Bottom line for builders

If you're thinking about starting projects in the democracy space, here's what this suggests:

The gap: Most AI and election work focuses on detecting fakes. Very little focuses on authentication infrastructure that can prove real things are real. Even less focuses on the legal and procedural frameworks that determine how evidence is evaluated during certification disputes.

The opportunity: Build authentication tools designed specifically for election evidence. Work with election officials to implement chain-of-custody systems. Develop legal language for statutes that set clear standards for evidence evaluation.

Existing work: The Stanford Internet Observatory has done excellent work on AI and elections, though they've recently faced serious challenges. The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity is developing technical standards for content authentication. Neither specifically targets the certification process.

Project idea: A rapid-response authentication service for election officials. When video or audio evidence emerges during a disputed election, they need immediate expert analysis they can trust. This service would provide fast, credible assessment with detailed technical reporting that can withstand legal scrutiny. The key innovation would be speed and legal defensibility, not just technical capability.

The liar's dividend isn't about technology being too good. It's about truth becoming too hard to prove. That's a problem we can address - but only if we recognize it as primarily a procedural and legal challenge, not just a technical one.

The officials who will certify the 2028 election are being selected now, as we covered earlier this month. They need tools and procedures for this challenge before they're in the hot seat.
