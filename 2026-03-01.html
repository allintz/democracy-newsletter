The Canvass: The Most Important Election Process You've Never Heard Of

Date: Sunday, March 1, 2026

Epistemic status: High confidence on the mechanics and historical practice; moderate confidence on vulnerability assessments for 2028.

You've probably heard of "certification" in elections—the formal process where officials declare results official. But there's a step that happens before certification that almost no one talks about: the canvass.

And here's the thing: the canvass is where the actual counting happens. Certification just rubber-stamps it.

This distinction matters enormously. Because while we've spent two years worrying about officials refusing to certify results, there's a much earlier chokepoint where votes can be excluded, challenged, or simply not counted at all.

Let me explain how this actually works.

What Is a Canvass?

The canvass is the formal process of compiling and verifying election results before they become official. According to the National Conference of State Legislatures, it typically involves:

  • Collecting results from every precinct and polling place
  • Reconciling vote totals with the number of ballots cast
  • Resolving discrepancies between machines and paper records
  • Processing provisional ballots (determining which should count)
  • Processing late-arriving mail ballots (within legal deadlines)
  • Creating the official vote total

    • Only after the canvass is complete does certification happen. Certification is supposed to be ministerial—just signing off that the canvass was done properly.

    Here's why this matters: During the canvass, officials make hundreds of judgment calls about which ballots count. Should this provisional ballot be included? Does this signature match? Was this mail ballot postmarked on time? Each decision is defensible either way.

    The Provisional Ballot Problem

    Let's get concrete. In the 2020 election, according to the U.S. Election Assistance Commission, approximately 1.4 million provisional ballots were rejected nationwide.

    Pennsylvania alone had about 100,000 provisional ballots cast in 2020. The Pennsylvania Department of State reported that roughly 36,000 were rejected during the canvass—before certification ever happened.

    Biden won Pennsylvania by 80,555 votes. Notice the numbers there?

    Now, most of those rejections were probably legitimate—voters who showed up at the wrong precinct, people who'd already voted by mail, etc. The canvassing process worked as designed.

    But here's the vulnerability: Who decides which provisional ballots count happens during the canvass, not during certification. And in most states, there's minimal oversight of these decisions until after they're made.

    How This Could Be Weaponized

    Imagine this scenario for 2028:

    A county election board in a swing state is controlled by election deniers (this is already true in several counties). During the canvass, they take an unusually strict interpretation of provisional ballot rules. Maybe they reject ballots where signatures have minor variations. Or they exclude mail ballots with unclear postmarks, interpreting ambiguous USPS markings as "too late."

    Each decision is within their discretion. Each is defensible based on some reading of state law.

    The result: They exclude 5,000 ballots in a county that Biden won by 20,000 votes. In a state decided by 15,000 votes, this happens in four counties.

    The state total changes by 20,000 votes. Trump wins the state.

    Here's the problem: By the time this gets to certification, it's too late. The official count they're certifying already excludes those ballots. Legal challenges would have to prove each individual ballot decision was wrong—which is nearly impossible after the fact.

    The Pennsylvania Case Study

    We don't have to imagine this entirely. Something similar was attempted in Pennsylvania in 2020, though it failed.

    According to reporting from NPR, the Trump campaign and Pennsylvania Republicans tried to exclude mail ballots where voters were given an opportunity to "cure" minor defects (like a missing signature).

    The argument was that allowing curing violated the equal protection clause because not all counties offered it. If successful, this would have excluded tens of thousands of ballots—decided during the canvass process, not certification.

    It failed because Pennsylvania courts ruled against it. But the attempt showed the playbook: use the canvass process to exclude ballots, then certify the resulting totals.

    The Legal Gray Zone

    The scariest part is how little legal clarity exists around canvassing procedures.

    Most state election laws specify deadlines and general procedures, but they leave enormous discretion to local officials on judgment calls. A Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project report found that "canvassing standards vary widely even within states, with different counties applying different standards to identical ballot questions."

    Take signature matching on mail ballots. Research from the Brennan Center found that some counties reject 1% of mail ballots for signature mismatches, while neighboring counties reject less than 0.1%—a 10x difference with no clear legal standard driving it.

    This discretion is normally fine. Local officials use their judgment, and the vast majority do so in good faith.

    But it becomes dangerous when officials are selected for their willingness to interpret rules favorably to one party. Which is exactly what's been happening since 2020 in some jurisdictions.

    Why We Haven't Worried About This

    We've focused on certification refusals because they're dramatic and visible. An official announces they won't certify—it makes news, courts can intervene, public pressure builds.

    But canvass manipulation is invisible until it's done. No official announces "I'm going to strictly interpret provisional ballot rules to benefit my preferred candidate." They just do it, decision by decision, ballot by ballot.

    By the time anyone notices, the official count exists. It gets certified. Courts are reluctant to second-guess thousands of individual ballot decisions made during a complex technical process.

    What Other Countries Do

    Many democracies separate the counting process from partisan control entirely.

    In Canada, according to Elections Canada, the canvass is conducted by non-partisan "returning officers" who are career civil servants, not elected officials or political appointees. They follow strict national standards applied uniformly across all ridings (districts).

    Australia takes it even further. The Australian Electoral Commission conducts a "scrutiny" process where representatives from all parties watch every stage of counting and can challenge decisions. Disputed ballots go to an independent AEC officer, not the people doing the initial canvass.

    The U.S. system, by contrast, often puts the canvass under control of the same county boards that handle certification—meaning partisan elected officials or appointees make all the judgment calls.

    The 2026-2028 Window

    Here's why this matters right now:

    County election boards and canvassing boards are being filled this year for the 2028 election. Most of these positions get almost no media coverage or public attention.

    According to Democracy Docket tracking, election deniers are running for county election positions in at least 15 states, many in swing state counties.

    If they win, they'll control not just certification in 2028, but the entire canvassing process. They'll make the judgment calls about which provisional ballots count, which signatures match, which postmarks are valid.

    And there's very little legal framework to constrain those decisions or review them quickly enough to matter.

    What Could Help

    A few structural changes could reduce this vulnerability:

    1. Uniform canvassing standards within states. Instead of letting each county decide signature matching standards, state law could specify them. Some states like Colorado have done this—the Colorado Secretary of State provides detailed guidance that all counties must follow.

    2. Real-time ballot adjudication tracking. Several counties now livestream ballot processing and keep detailed logs of every decision to accept or reject a ballot. This creates a record that can be reviewed if totals seem off.

    3. Bipartisan canvassing requirements. Some states require that canvassing decisions involving disputed ballots must have representatives from both parties present. This doesn't prevent partisanship, but it makes one-sided manipulation harder.

    4. Expedited judicial review. Courts could establish fast-track processes for reviewing canvassing decisions before certification, rather than waiting until after official totals exist.

    But implementing any of these requires state legislation. And in many swing states, that means getting through state legislatures that are themselves controlled by gerrymandered majorities sympathetic to election skepticism.

    Bottom Line

    We've spent years focused on the visible chokepoint—certification refusals. But there's an earlier, less visible process where votes can be excluded just as effectively: the canvass itself.

    During the canvass, local officials make hundreds of discretionary decisions about which ballots count. These decisions are usually made in good faith. But when officials are selected for their partisan loyalty rather than their professionalism, that discretion becomes a vulnerability.

    The 2028 canvass will be conducted by officials being chosen right now, in county races that get almost no attention. If those positions are filled by people committed to finding reasons to exclude ballots from opposition areas, the damage will be done before certification even begins.

    For people thinking about democracy projects: We need rapid-response legal teams that can monitor canvassing in real-time in swing counties, with the capacity to challenge problematic ballot rejections during the canvass, not after. We also need legislative campaigns in swing states to establish uniform canvassing standards that reduce local discretion on ballot acceptance. Both of these need to happen in 2026-2027 to matter for 2028.

    The certification battles of 2020-2022 taught us that election officials matter. We need to learn the next lesson: the decisions made before certification might matter even more.
