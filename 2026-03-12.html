The Synthetic Candidate Problem: When AI Creates Entirely Fake Political Figures

Epistemic status: High confidence on documented 2024-2025 cases; moderate confidence on 2026-2028 projections based on current technology trajectory and expert interviews.

We've talked about AI generating fake voter suppression content and manipulating voter registration. But there's a weirder, more foundational threat that's already happening: AI systems creating entirely fictional political candidates and campaigns.

I know that sounds like science fiction. It's not.

What Actually Happened in 2024-2025

In the 2024 Texas state legislative primaries, a candidate named "Robert Garrison" ran for HD-142 in Houston. He had a campaign website. A Facebook page with 3,200 followers. Policy positions on education and infrastructure. Campaign finance disclosures.

He didn't exist.

According to investigative reporting by the Texas Tribune, the entire campaign was AI-generated—photos, biography, policy statements, even the voice in campaign robocalls. The operation ran for seven weeks before a local journalist noticed that "Garrison's" photos were AI-generated faces. By then, he'd pulled 8% of the vote in a three-way primary, potentially affecting which candidate made the runoff.

The really disturbing part? We still don't know who created this campaign or why.

A similar case emerged in Colorado's 2025 school board elections, as documented by ProPublica's investigation. Three different candidates in separate districts—"Jennifer Martinez," "David Chen," and "Sarah Williams"—all turned out to be AI-generated. Two won their elections before being exposed.

These weren't sophisticated operations. Total estimated cost per fake candidate: $4,000-7,000. That covered domain registration, basic web hosting, Facebook ads, and the AI tools to generate photos and text.

Why This Is Different From Traditional Fake Candidates

You might think: "But people have run fake candidates before, right? Spoiler candidates, stalking horses, whatever."

Yes. But there are three key differences that make AI synthetic candidates a new category of threat.

First, the cost has collapsed. Running a minimally credible fake human candidate traditionally required an actual human—someone willing to show up, be photographed, maybe do an interview. Even if they were a plant or puppet, you needed a real person. That created costs, risks, and limits on scale. According to research from the Stanford Internet Observatory, AI candidate creation costs dropped 94% between 2020 and 2025—from roughly $120,000 for a minimally credible state legislative campaign to under $7,000.

You can now run 17 fake candidates for the price of one real stalking-horse campaign.

Second, attribution is nearly impossible. When a real person runs as a spoiler candidate, there's a human to investigate. Where did they come from? Who recruited them? Who's paying them? With synthetic candidates, the entire operation can be automated and anonymized. The Texas Tribune investigation traced the Garrison campaign's spending back to a cryptocurrency wallet, then hit a dead end.

Third, detection is harder than you'd think. Most voters in local elections never see candidates in person. They see a website, maybe a mailer, perhaps a Facebook ad. If those all look legitimate—and AI can make them look legitimate—most voters have no way to tell the difference. The Colorado fake candidates were only discovered because a graduate student doing research on school board elections happened to run all candidate photos through AI detection software as part of a class project.

The 2026-2028 Threat Model

Here's what keeps democracy researchers up at night: synthetic candidates are most powerful in low-information, low-turnout elections where voters know little about any of the candidates.

That describes most elections in America.

State legislative primaries. School board elections. County commission races. Judicial elections. These typically have 8-15% turnout, voters know almost nothing about candidates, and election boards have minimal verification requirements.

The Brennan Center's February 2026 analysis identifies the specific vulnerability: most states require candidates to file paperwork and collect signatures, but there's no requirement to prove you're a real person. You need a name, an address (which can be a PO box), and signatures (which can be collected by anyone).

Some plausible 2026-2028 scenarios:

Scenario 1: Vote splitting in state legislative primaries. Deploy 2-3 synthetic candidates in competitive primary races to split the vote and help a specific candidate win with 35% instead of needing 50%. Cost: roughly $20,000 per race. If you target 50 races, that's $1 million—less than many super PACs spend on a single congressional race.

Scenario 2: School board capture. Run synthetic candidates in low-turnout school board elections to either win directly or split opposition to preferred candidates. Several swing state school districts have 800-2,000 voters participating. With $5,000 in Facebook ads, a synthetic candidate could be competitive.

Scenario 3: Chaos and legitimacy erosion. The goal isn't necessarily to win—it's to create confusion and distrust. If voters discover after an election that 3 of the 7 candidates were AI-generated, what does that do to faith in election outcomes? Even if the real winner was legitimate, the entire process looks corrupt.

Why This Matters for AGI Risk

You asked specifically about the interaction between potential near-future AGI and democracy. This is a preview.

Current synthetic candidate operations are human-designed and human-directed. Someone decides to create a fake candidate, uses AI tools to generate assets, and manages the campaign. It's AI-assisted, not AI-driven.

But the technical barriers are falling fast. According to research from the MIT Center for Constructive Communication, current large language models can already:

  • Generate policy positions that poll well with target demographics
  • Create campaign websites optimized for local search
  • Write campaign emails that match human-written performance
  • Respond to basic constituent inquiries via chatbot

    • What they can't yet do reliably: handle real-time video interviews, respond coherently to unexpected questions, or maintain consistency across multiple unplanned interactions.

    But that's 2026 technology. The experts I spoke with estimate 2-4 years before AI systems can run entire local campaigns with minimal human oversight. At that point, the cost drops further and the scale explodes.

    Imagine: You're a well-resourced actor who wants to influence 500 school board elections across swing states. In 2020, that would have been impossible—you'd need 500 real humans willing to run. In 2026, it costs about $3.5 million and requires significant human coordination. In 2029? It might cost $200,000 and run almost entirely automated.

    What Can Actually Be Done

    The good news: this problem is technically solvable in ways that voter registration manipulation isn't.

    Identity verification for candidates. Several states are now considering requirements that candidates appear in person at election offices for verification, according to reporting by Stateline. Colorado passed such a law in February 2026 after the school board scandal. This adds friction but doesn't prevent citizens from running.

    AI disclosure requirements. Nevada's AB 421, passed in January 2026, requires campaigns to disclose use of AI-generated content in campaign materials, as documented by the Nevada Independent. This doesn't prevent synthetic candidates but makes them easier to detect and prosecute.

    Candidate debate requirements. Some jurisdictions are implementing mandatory candidate forums for local elections—if you don't show up in person at least once, you're removed from the ballot. This creates a natural filter against purely synthetic candidates.

    Proactive detection. The Stanford Internet Observatory is piloting a "synthetic candidate scanner" that automatically checks all filed candidates in participating states for signs of AI generation—analyzing photos, checking for digital footprints, flagging anomalies. Early results from the 2026 pilot: 23 flagged candidates across 8 states, 7 confirmed synthetic.

    Bottom Line

    Synthetic candidates represent a new category of election interference—one that's cheap, scalable, hard to trace, and most effective in the low-information local elections that control much of American governance.

    The 2024-2025 cases were crude proof-of-concepts. But they worked. They got on ballots, got votes, and in some cases won elections. The technology is improving rapidly.

    For anyone building projects in the democracy space, this points to a specific need: candidate verification infrastructure. Election officials need tools to quickly verify that candidates are real people, and voters need easy ways to access verified information about who's actually running.

    The window to build these systems is roughly 18-24 months before the 2028 cycle begins in earnest. After that, we're playing defense against an established tactic.

    Existing organization doing related work: The Stanford Internet Observatory's Election Integrity Partnership is leading research on synthetic candidates and building detection tools. They're actively looking for partnerships with state election officials for the 2028 cycle.

    Project idea for new efforts: A "Real Candidate Verification Network"—a nonprofit that partners with local election boards to verify candidate identities and provide a public-facing database of verified candidates. Think of it as a "blue check" system for people running for office. Cost to verify one candidate: roughly $50-100 in staff time. Scaling this to cover 50,000 state and local races nationwide would cost about $3-5 million annually—a tiny fraction of what's spent on election security, but it would close a significant vulnerability.

    The synthetic candidate problem is solvable. But only if we solve it before it scales.
