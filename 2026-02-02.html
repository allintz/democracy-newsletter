Understanding the County Canvas: Where Local Officials Can Block Vote Certification

Epistemic status: High confidence on the mechanics and historical examples; medium confidence on how prevalent this tactic might become in future elections.

Here's something that surprised me when I started researching election subversion: Most people focus on dramatic moments like January 6th or fake electors schemes. But one of the most vulnerable points in our election system happens much earlier and much more quietly—at county canvassing boards.

Today we're looking at what happens after votes are counted but before results become official. This seemingly technical administrative step turns out to be a critical chokepoint where a small number of local officials can potentially block thousands of votes from being certified.

What is canvassing, exactly?

After Election Day, counties don't just announce winners. They go through a formal process called "canvassing" where local boards review the results and officially certify them.

Think of it like this: The voting machines and ballot counters give you the numbers. But those numbers don't become official until a canvassing board—usually 2-4 people appointed or elected locally—signs off saying "yes, we've reviewed this and these are the real results."

According to the Brennan Center, this happens at the county level first, then rolls up to the state. If counties don't certify, states can't certify. If states can't certify, Congress doesn't get electoral votes.

It's supposed to be a purely ministerial task. The board checks that procedures were followed, that the numbers add up, that everything looks normal. Then they sign the papers and move on.

The vulnerability: It's often just two people

Here's where it gets concerning. In many states, these canvassing boards are tiny.

In Michigan, for example, each county has a Board of Canvassers with exactly four members—two Democrats and two Republicans by law. You need three votes to certify. This means a single member can't block certification alone, but two members of the same party can deadlock the process.

In Arizona, the county Board of Supervisors handles canvassing. That's typically 3-5 people for the entire county. Maricopa County (Phoenix) has over 2.4 million registered voters, and their certification requires a majority vote from five supervisors.

Nevada uses county commissions, which are also usually 3-5 people. Pennsylvania uses county boards of elections, typically 3 members.

The key point: We're often talking about 2-3 individuals having the power to block certification for hundreds of thousands of voters.

This isn't theoretical—it's already happened

Wayne County, Michigan (November 2020)

The clearest example came in Wayne County, Michigan, which includes Detroit. On November 17, 2020, the four-member Board of Canvassers met to certify the results.

The two Republican members, William Hartmann and Monica Palmer, initially voted to block certification. Their stated reason was minor discrepancies in poll books in some Detroit precincts—the kind of small administrative issues that occur in literally every election and don't indicate fraud.

This created a 2-2 deadlock. The county couldn't certify.

After intense public pressure during a four-hour Zoom meeting with hundreds of angry citizens, Palmer and Hartmann reversed their votes and the board certified. But here's what made it even more troubling: Later that evening, both tried to rescind their votes again after reportedly speaking with Trump.

The Michigan Secretary of State had to clarify that you can't un-certify results once certified. But imagine if they'd held firm on that initial refusal.

Cochise County, Arizona (November 2022)

Fast forward to 2022. In Cochise County, Arizona, two Republican supervisors, Tom Crosby and Peggy Judd, refused to certify the election results by the legal deadline.

Their stated reason? They wanted more information about voting machine certification (even though the machines had been properly certified months earlier according to state procedures).

This blocked certification of about 47,000 votes. The deadline passed. Arizona's Secretary of State Katie Hobbs had to sue the county to force certification. A judge ordered them to certify, and they eventually complied.

But here's the concerning part: Crosby and Judd faced essentially no consequences. Initial criminal charges were filed, then dropped. The system relied entirely on a judge stepping in quickly.

Otero County, New Mexico (June 2022)

Even earlier, in the June 2022 primary, the Otero County Commission in New Mexico voted 2-1 to refuse certification based on unspecified "concerns" about Dominion voting machines (the same machines used successfully across the country).

This was a primary election, not even the general. The New Mexico Supreme Court had to order certification, which eventually happened. But again, it required emergency judicial intervention.

Why this tactic is particularly dangerous

1. It operates at a very local level. National organizations can monitor swing states, but can they monitor all 159 counties in Georgia? All 83 in Michigan? All 67 in Pennsylvania?

2. It requires capturing very few people. You don't need to convince millions of voters or hundreds of legislators. In some cases, literally 2-3 local officials can create a crisis.

3. It can be dressed up as "just asking questions." The Cochise County supervisors didn't say "we're overturning the election." They said they wanted more information. The Wayne County canvassers cited minor poll book discrepancies. It sounds reasonable if you don't understand that these are normal, expected variations.

4. The legal path to forcing certification is unclear in many states. Arizona and New Mexico had to go to court. Some states have unclear laws about what happens if a county refuses to certify. Can the state certify without them? Can they replace the board? How quickly can courts act?

5. It creates chaos and uncertainty even if it ultimately fails. Remember the safe harbor deadline we discussed last week? If county certification is delayed by refusals and lawsuits, it eats into that already-compressed timeline. Even if courts eventually force certification, the delay itself can be weaponized.

What protections exist (and where they fall short)

Some states have reformed their laws since 2020:

Michigan passed a law in 2023 clarifying that canvassing is ministerial and making it easier to replace canvassers who refuse to certify. But this required Democrats to control the legislature—and could be reversed if power shifts.

Nevada passed AB321 in 2021 making certification mandatory and creating penalties for refusal. But enforcement still requires someone to actually impose those penalties.

The problem is that most election law was written assuming good faith. The statutes say things like "the board shall certify" but don't specify what happens if they simply... don't. And going to court takes time.

The 2024 near-miss and looking ahead

We came surprisingly close to more refusals in 2024. According to reporting by CNN, there were at least a dozen instances where county officials in swing states publicly considered refusing to certify or made statements questioning whether they had to certify. Most backed down after legal pressure.

But here's what concerns me: The playbook is now public. Would-be election subverters have seen that:

  • You can refuse to certify
  • Courts will probably force you to eventually
  • But you probably won't face serious consequences
  • And you'll become a hero to election-denying activists

    • For the 2026 midterms and 2028 presidential election, this means we need to watch county canvassing boards in competitive states very carefully. If an election is close enough, blocking certification in just a few strategic counties could create a constitutional crisis.

    Bottom line for democracy defenders

    County certification is an underappreciated chokepoint in American elections. It's designed to be a routine administrative step, but it requires positive action by a small number of local officials—making it vulnerable to bad-faith refusal.

    The pattern we've seen is: Refuse to certify based on pretextual concerns → Face public/legal pressure → Eventually certify but face minimal consequences → Become a cause célèbre in election-denial circles.

    What would make this worse: A closer election where a single county's votes matter more. More counties refusing simultaneously (harder for courts to handle). State legislatures that are sympathetic to the refusal and slow to act. Officials willing to accept real penalties to become martyrs.

    What would help:

  • Clearer state laws making certification explicitly mandatory with no discretion
  • Automatic replacement mechanisms when officials refuse
  • Swift judicial review processes (pre-designed injunction procedures)
  • Actual enforcement of penalties to create deterrence
  • Better civics education so the public understands why "just asking questions" isn't appropriate at the certification stage

    • Project idea for the democracy space: Create a monitoring system for county canvassing board meetings in swing states. Most of these meetings are public but barely attended. Having trained observers (or even just good video recording and rapid response teams) could help catch refusals early and mobilize legal response. Organizations like All Voting is Local do some of this work, but it needs to scale significantly before 2028.

    The Wayne County canvassers reversed their votes because hundreds of citizens showed up (virtually) and made clear this wasn't acceptable. That kind of rapid response and public attention might be our best defense against this particular vulnerability—but it requires knowing where to look and being ready to act quickly.
