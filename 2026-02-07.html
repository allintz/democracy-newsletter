Democracy Deep Dive: Why Election Officials Keep Quitting (And Why That's a National Security Problem)

Epistemic status: High confidence on documented harassment trends and resignation rates; medium confidence on long-term systemic impacts, which are still unfolding.

---

Here's something that doesn't make headlines but should terrify anyone thinking about 2028: experienced election officials are quitting in droves, and they're being replaced by... well, sometimes nobody, and sometimes people who explicitly don't believe in the legitimacy of American elections.

This isn't about voter suppression or fake electors or certification refusals. This is about systematically dismantling election administration from the inside by making the job unbearable for competent professionals.

Let me show you why this matters.

The Scale of the Exodus

Between 2020 and 2022, roughly one in five local election officials left their positions, according to research by Reed College and the Democracy Fund. That's a 20% turnover rate in just two years.

In some swing states, it was much worse. A 2022 survey found that one-third of election officials felt unsafe because of their job. One-third felt pressured to resign or retire.

These aren't political appointees or partisan activists. These are county clerks and registrars who've been running elections for decades—the institutional memory of American democracy.

And they're leaving because the job has become genuinely dangerous.

What Actually Happens to Election Officials

Let me give you concrete examples of what "harassment" means in this context.

In Colorado, Clerk Tina Peters was sentenced to nine years in prison in 2024—not for being harassed, but for being the harasser. She used her position to facilitate a security breach of election equipment, believing she was exposing fraud.

That's one pattern: officials who became true believers in election denial and violated their duties.

But the more common pattern is good officials being driven out. Arizona's Maricopa County election officials received hundreds of death threats after 2020. County Recorder Stephen Richer had to hire private security and faced armed protesters outside his home.

Georgia election worker Shaye Moss testified to Congress about receiving racist threats after being falsely accused of election fraud by Trump and Giuliani. She and her mother had strangers showing up at their homes, threatening violence.

These weren't isolated incidents. A 2022 Brennan Center survey found that 77% of election officials reported feeling that threats against them had increased, and one in six had personally received threats.

Why This Creates a National Security Vulnerability

Here's the mechanism that should worry you: this isn't random chaos. It's a specific attack surface.

Election administration in the US is radically decentralized. We have roughly 10,000 election jurisdictions. Most are run by county-level officials with small staffs and tiny budgets.

When you drive out experienced officials, three things happen:

First, you lose institutional knowledge. Running an election is complex. You need to maintain voter rolls, program voting machines, train poll workers, handle provisional ballots, conduct recounts, and navigate constantly changing election laws. Experienced officials know how to do this. New officials are figuring it out on the fly.

Second, you create recruitment opportunities for bad actors. When positions are vacant, someone needs to fill them. In Nevada, multiple county clerk positions were targeted by candidates who explicitly questioned 2020 election results. Some won.

Third, you increase the baseline error rate. Even well-meaning but inexperienced officials make mistakes. Those mistakes become ammunition for claims of fraud, which generates more harassment, which drives out more officials. It's a doom loop.

The 2022 Test Case

The 2022 midterms gave us a preview of this dynamic in action.

According to reporting by ProPublica and Votebeat, numerous jurisdictions experienced problems directly traceable to official inexperience. In Colorado's Mesa County, after experienced staff departed following the Tina Peters case, the replacement team struggled with basic administrative tasks.

In Arizona, Maricopa County had printer malfunctions on Election Day that, while ultimately resolved, created hours of confusion. The county's election officials were competent and acted properly—but they were also exhausted and understaffed after two years of intense harassment.

Here's what matters: these problems didn't swing any elections in 2022. But they didn't need to. They demonstrated that the system is increasingly fragile.

The Playbook Is Now Documented

This isn't speculation about future tactics. The strategy is explicitly laid out in publicly available documents.

An investigation by Reuters documented how organized groups have been systematically attending election board meetings, filing mass information requests (overwhelming small offices), and recruiting election-denying candidates for administrative positions.

The far-right Cleta Mitchell, who was on Trump's January 2, 2021 call pressuring Georgia's Secretary of State, now runs the Election Integrity Network. The network explicitly trains activists to challenge voter registrations, attend canvassing board meetings, and—crucially—to run for election administration positions.

This isn't hidden. It's public strategy.

Why Existing Protections Don't Work

You might think: surely there are laws against threatening election officials?

There are. They don't work well for three reasons:

First, most harassment stays just below the prosecution threshold. Calling someone's office 50 times to call them a traitor isn't technically illegal. Showing up at public meetings to yell at them isn't illegal. The Brennan Center documented that most harassment is persistent and menacing but doesn't meet the legal standard for "true threats."

Second, local prosecutors often won't act. In many jurisdictions, the prosecutor is elected and doesn't want to alienate voters by prosecuting people who claim they're "just asking questions" about election integrity.

Third, protection resources are minimal. The Justice Department's Election Threats Task Force, created in 2021, has reviewed over 2,000 complaints but brought relatively few prosecutions. There's a mismatch between the scale of the problem and available resources.

Why 2028 Is the Real Test

Here's why this matters for 2028 specifically:

By 2028, we'll be eight years out from 2020. The officials who were experienced in 2020 will be even more likely to have retired or moved on. The officials hired in 2021-2024 will have experience, but in many cases, they'll have been selected specifically for their skepticism about election integrity.

The 2022 Electoral Count Reform Act fixed some of the vulnerabilities we saw in 2020—but it operates at the federal level, at the electoral vote counting stage. It doesn't address what happens at the county level, where the initial counting and certification happens.

If you wanted to disrupt a close election in 2028, you wouldn't necessarily need county officials to refuse certification (though that remains a risk). You'd just need them to be inexperienced enough to make errors that create ambiguity, or ideologically motivated enough to "ask questions" that delay certification past critical deadlines.

What the Research Tells Us

Political scientists who study democratic erosion consistently point to the importance of non-partisan election administration.

Research by Susan Hyde and Nikolay Marinov shows that when ruling parties control election administration, even democracies with strong formal institutions can experience backsliding. The mechanism is subtle: it's not that officials necessarily commit fraud, but they make marginal decisions that favor their party, and those margins add up.

Alberto Simpser's work on election manipulation emphasizes that undermining confidence in elections is itself a form of manipulation—even if actual fraud doesn't occur. When the public doesn't trust election results, democratic legitimacy erodes.

The US has historically had an advantage here: our election administration has been boring, professional, and credible. We're systematically dismantling that advantage.

Bottom Line for Democracy Defenders

If you're thinking about starting a project in the democracy space, here's what this analysis suggests:

The vulnerability: By 2028, many jurisdictions will have election officials who are either inexperienced due to high turnover, or ideologically motivated to question election legitimacy, or both. This creates systematic fragility even if no explicit subversion attempt occurs.

The gap: Most democracy funding goes to voter registration, turnout, and policy advocacy. Very little goes to supporting the actual humans who administer elections. There's a massive gap in providing resources, security, and professional development for local election officials.

Project ideas that could help:

  • Legal defense funds for election officials facing harassment. Make it clear that competent professionals will have legal support if they're targeted.
  • Rapid response teams for technical election administration support. When an experienced official quits, help jurisdictions maintain continuity with temporary expert assistance.
  • Public recognition programs for election officials. Counter the harassment narrative with systematic celebration of election workers as democracy heroes.
  • Security upgrades for election offices. Many local election offices have minimal physical security. Funding security improvements makes the job less dangerous.

    • Organizations already working on this: The Committee of Seventy's Poll Hero Project recruits and trains poll workers. The Center for Tech and Civic Life provides grants to election offices for infrastructure and training. The Democracy Defense Project offers legal support to election officials.

    The 2020 election subversion attempt failed partly because experienced officials knew their jobs and did them properly under immense pressure. We're systematically ensuring that those officials won't be there in 2028. That's not a prediction about the future—it's a description of what's happening right now.
