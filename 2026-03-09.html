The Surprisingly Weak Powers of State Attorneys General in Election Disputes

Epistemic status: High confidence on legal framework and historical examples; moderate confidence on how 2028 scenarios would actually play out given limited precedent.

When we think about who can stop election subversion, state Attorneys General often come to mind as powerful safeguards. They can file lawsuits, issue legal opinions, and theoretically prosecute officials who break election laws.

But here's the uncomfortable reality: In a real crisis, AGs have far less power than most people assume. And understanding these limitations is critical for 2028 planning.

What Can an AG Actually Do?

Let's start with what state Attorneys General can theoretically do during election disputes.

They can file lawsuits to compel officials to follow the law. If a county board refuses to certify results, the AG can ask a court to order certification. According to the National Association of Attorneys General, AGs serve as the state's chief legal officer and represent state interests in court.

They can issue formal legal opinions on how election laws should be interpreted. These opinions carry weight—many officials rely on AG guidance when making decisions.

In some states, they can also prosecute election crimes. This varies by state, but many AGs have at least concurrent jurisdiction with local prosecutors for election-related offenses.

Sounds powerful, right? Here's the problem.

The Timing Problem

Courts move slowly. Really slowly.

When Arizona's Cochise County delayed certification in 2022, Attorney General Mark Brnovich (a Republican) filed suit on November 28. According to Arizona Republic reporting, the county had until December 5 to certify under state law. The court issued an order... on December 1.

That's a three-day turnaround, which is remarkably fast for the legal system. And it barely worked—the county certified just one day before the final deadline.

But here's what keeps me up at night: What if they hadn't complied?

The Enforcement Gap

Court orders only matter if someone enforces them. And this is where AG power hits a hard wall.

State AGs cannot directly remove local officials from office in most states. They cannot physically force someone to sign a certification document. They cannot break into a county office and count votes themselves.

What they can do is ask a court to hold someone in contempt. But contempt proceedings take time—more hearings, more legal process. And even contempt orders ultimately rely on someone (usually a sheriff or state police) being willing to arrest the non-complying official.

The Michigan Election Law, for example, makes certification refusal a misdemeanor. But prosecution would take months, long past any election deadlines.

This creates a dangerous dynamic: Officials who refuse to certify face minimal immediate consequences, while the election outcome hangs in limbo.

When AGs Make Things Worse

Here's the scarier scenario: What if the AG doesn't want to enforce election law?

In 2020, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed a lawsuit asking the Supreme Court to throw out election results in four swing states. The suit, rejected by the Supreme Court, claimed Pennsylvania, Georgia, Michigan, and Wisconsin conducted their elections unconstitutionally.

This wasn't an AG protecting election integrity. It was an AG using his office to challenge valid results.

The pattern repeated in other states. According to States United Democracy Center tracking, multiple state AGs joined frivolous lawsuits challenging 2020 results or issued opinions supporting restrictions on vote counting.

When the state's top legal officer actively works against election integrity, there's no higher state-level authority to stop them. You have to go to federal court—another layer of delay and complexity.

The Partisan Election Problem

Here's a structural vulnerability most people miss: In 43 states, Attorneys General are elected in partisan elections, according to the National Association of Attorneys General.

This means they face the same political incentives as other elected officials. An AG from the same party as a president might face enormous pressure to not investigate or prosecute election subversion that benefits their party.

We saw this dynamic in Arizona. AG Brnovich eventually sued Cochise County—but only after significant public pressure and media coverage. The Arizona Republic reported that democracy advocates had been calling for action for days.

What if there hadn't been enough public attention? What if the case was more legally ambiguous? What if the AG was up for reelection the next cycle?

These aren't hypothetical questions for 2028. They're planning scenarios.

What About Criminal Prosecution?

One power AGs have that seems more forceful: criminal charges.

Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel charged 16 individuals who participated in the fake electors scheme with multiple felonies including forgery. According to her office's press release, charges included conspiracy and election law forgery.

But notice the timeline: The fake elector scheme happened in December 2020. Charges came in July 2023. That's two and a half years later.

Criminal prosecution is a powerful accountability tool, but it's not a tool that stops election subversion in real-time. By the time charges are filed, the political damage is done, the crisis has passed, and the outcome has been decided.

For deterrence? Maybe. For stopping certification refusal or vote count manipulation in November 2028? No.

The Federal Backup Isn't Strong Either

You might think: "Well, if state AGs fail, the U.S. Department of Justice can step in."

Yes—but with major limitations.

Federal jurisdiction over elections is narrow. The DOJ can prosecute violations of federal voting rights laws and federal election crimes. According to the DOJ's election crimes manual, federal jurisdiction typically requires interstate elements or denial of federally protected rights.

But garden-variety certification refusal or state election code violations? Those are state matters. The federal government can't just take over state election administration because a county board is acting badly.

The DOJ can file friend-of-the-court briefs. They can investigate. They can bring criminal charges eventually. But they face the same timing problem state AGs do—courts move slowly, and enforcement is uncertain.

What Actually Stopped 2020 Subversion?

Given these weak enforcement tools, why didn't election subversion succeed in 2020?

Mostly, it was individual officials making the right choice under enormous pressure.

Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger refused to "find 11,780 votes" despite a direct phone call from the president, as documented in the Washington Post's recording of the conversation.

Vice President Pence refused to reject electoral votes despite intense political pressure and constitutional theories from John Eastman claiming he had that power.

Arizona Governor Doug Ducey certified his state's electors despite Trump calling his cell phone during the certification ceremony, according to CNN reporting.

These weren't victories of institutional design. They were individuals choosing to follow norms when the incentive structure pushed them toward partisan advantage.

What happens when officials make the opposite choice? And when AGs can't or won't stop them?

The 2028 Vulnerability

Now imagine it's November 2028. Three or four county boards in a pivotal swing state refuse to certify results. The state AG files lawsuits.

But the counties have their own lawyers now. They've been planning this. They file motions arguing that they have discretionary authority during the canvass process (as we covered in the March 1 newsletter). They point to actual irregularities—which exist in every election if you look hard enough.

The cases move to state court. One judge issues an order to certify. The counties appeal. The state AG asks for expedited review. Days pass. The certification deadline approaches.

Maybe the state supreme court ultimately rules against the counties. But what if it takes eight days? Ten days? What if certification happens so late that Congress faces competing slates of electors and uncertainty about which slate to count?

According to research by Edward Foley at Ohio State, even temporary certification delays can create legal ambiguity that ends up in Congress—where partisan dynamics rather than law might determine the outcome.

The AG's lawsuit technically "worked"—but democracy still failed.

What Would Actually Help?

Given these limitations, what would make AGs more effective in real crises?

First: Automatic enforcement mechanisms. Rather than requiring AGs to sue and wait for courts, laws could specify that if certification doesn't happen by the deadline, the next authority in the chain (usually the Secretary of State) automatically certifies using the available results. Michigan added this type of provision in 2023, according to Brennan Center analysis.

Second: Emergency removal authority. Some states allow governors or secretaries of state to immediately remove and replace local election officials who refuse to perform ministerial duties. This needs to be expanded and clearly defined in statute.

Third: Pre-positioned legal teams. Rather than waiting for a crisis and then filing suit, AGs could have dedicated election protection units that prepare legal actions in advance, identify likely trouble spots, and have motions ready to file within hours.

Fourth: Criminal prosecution standards. Clear statutory language making certification refusal a felony (not a misdemeanor) with mandatory penalties could increase deterrence. Even if prosecution comes later, officials might think twice if the stakes are higher.

Fifth: Non-partisan AG selection. The most structurally ambitious reform would be moving toward appointed rather than elected AGs, potentially with bipartisan confirmation requirements. This is how the federal system works for U.S. Attorneys (presidential appointment with Senate confirmation), though they still face political pressures.

The Bottom Line

State Attorneys General sound like powerful guardians of election integrity. But in practice, their tools are slow, indirect, and dependent on compliance.

They can sue—but courts take days or weeks to rule. They can ask for contempt orders—but enforcement is uncertain. They can prosecute—but only after the crisis has passed. And if the AG themselves is hostile to election integrity, there's no higher state authority to appeal to.

The 2020 election held because individual officials made the right choices under pressure. The legal system's role was mostly after-the-fact accountability, not real-time protection.

For 2028, we need to design systems that don't depend on individual courage and that don't give AGs and courts weeks to resolve certification disputes. That means automatic mechanisms, emergency powers, and pre-positioned rapid response capabilities.

Key takeaway for democracy advocates: Don't count on state AGs to save elections in real-time. Their power is more limited than most people realize, especially under time pressure. Focus instead on (1) reforms that create automatic enforcement mechanisms that don't require litigation, (2) building political coalitions to elect AGs who will proactively protect election integrity, and (3) supporting organizations like States United Democracy Center that are developing rapid-response legal protocols to accelerate court proceedings when certification disputes arise.

The race to certify votes in November 2028 will be measured in days, not weeks. If we're relying on traditional AG powers and court processes, we've already lost too much time.
