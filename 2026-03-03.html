The Hidden Power of "Ministerial Duty" — And Why It Matters Less Than You Think

Today we're diving into a legal concept that's been thrown around a lot since 2020 but rarely explained well: ministerial duty.

If you've followed election certification battles, you've probably heard lawyers and election officials invoke this phrase. It sounds reassuring. The idea is that certification is supposed to be automatic — a "ministerial" task that officials must perform, with no discretion to refuse.

But here's the problem: this legal protection is way weaker than it sounds. And understanding why helps explain a major vulnerability heading into 2028.

Epistemic status: I'm moderately confident in this analysis. The legal principles are well-established, but their application in novel election disputes remains somewhat unpredictable.

---

What "Ministerial Duty" Actually Means

Let's start with the basics. In legal terms, a "ministerial duty" is a task that requires no judgment or discretion. You just do it. Think of a clerk filing a document that's been properly submitted — they can't refuse because they don't like what it says.

Election certification is supposed to be ministerial. According to legal scholars at the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project, once local officials have completed the canvass (tallying up the votes), certification should be a straightforward administrative act. The certifying official's job is to confirm that the canvass happened according to law, then sign the certificate.

Here's a concrete example: In Georgia, state law requires the county board of elections to certify results by 5 p.m. on the Monday following the election (or the Tuesday following a runoff). According to Georgia Code § 21-2-493, the board "shall" certify if the returns are complete. That word "shall" is supposed to mean it's not optional.

The theory is that certification is just confirming that the process was followed. It's not supposed to involve investigating fraud claims, adjudicating disputes, or refusing to certify because you don't like the outcome.

---

Why This Protection Is Surprisingly Weak

But here's where it gets tricky. What happens when an official just... doesn't do it?

We saw this play out in real time in 2022. In Cochise County, Arizona, two Republican supervisors on the three-member board refused to certify the election results. They cited concerns about voting machine certification. According to reporting from NPR, the county missed its statutory certification deadline.

Arizona's Attorney General went to court and got an order compelling the board to certify. The court ruled that certification was indeed a ministerial duty with no discretion to refuse. The board finally certified — but only after significant delay.

So the system worked, right? The court stepped in and fixed it.

Not exactly. Here's what actually happened:

First problem: Time. Getting a court order takes days or weeks. Arizona's certification deadline was November 28, 2022. The supervisors didn't certify until December 2, after the court intervened. That's a four-day delay. In a presidential election year with tight Electoral College deadlines, four days could be catastrophic.

Second problem: Compliance isn't guaranteed. The Cochise County supervisors eventually complied with the court order. But legally, what if they hadn't? [As we covered in a previous newsletter], courts have no enforcement mechanism of their own. They depend on other officials (sheriffs, marshals, governors) to actually enforce their orders. If a refusing official has local support and local law enforcement won't act, the situation becomes much more complicated.

Third problem: The legal standard is mushier than you think. Even though certification is "ministerial," officials can still claim they're exercising legitimate oversight. In Otero County, New Mexico in 2022, county commissioners refused to certify, citing concerns about Dominion voting machines. They eventually backed down after the Secretary of State threatened to remove them and criminal charges were floated. But they framed their refusal as performing their oversight duty, not violating it.

---

The Real Vulnerability: Officials Who Believe Their Own Arguments

Here's what worries me most for 2028. The Cochise and Otero cases involved officials who were eventually persuaded (or pressured) to comply. But the arguments they made weren't just bad-faith obstruction. They genuinely believed — or at least claimed to believe — that they had a responsibility to investigate concerns before certifying.

This creates a dangerous dynamic. Officials can refuse certification while maintaining they're actually fulfilling their duty by not rubber-stamping potentially fraudulent results.

And legally, this gray area exists because there are scenarios where certification might legitimately be delayed. According to the Brennan Center's analysis of certification laws, most state statutes are vague about what happens if there are genuine irregularities during the canvass. Can an official pause certification to investigate? For how long? Who decides if the concern is legitimate?

The law often doesn't say. Which means in a contested election, we'd likely see multiple simultaneous lawsuits in state and federal court, all trying to answer these questions under time pressure.

---

What Happened in Wayne County Shows the Pattern

The Wayne County, Michigan Board of Canvassers incident in November 2020 is the clearest preview of this vulnerability.

On November 17, 2020, the four-member board (two Republicans, two Democrats) initially deadlocked 2-2 on certifying Detroit's results. The two Republican members, Monica Palmer and William Hartmann, cited discrepancies between poll books and ballot counts in some Detroit precincts.

After intense public pressure during a Zoom meeting with hundreds of observers, they reversed their votes and certified. But later that night, Palmer and Hartmann tried to rescind their votes. They submitted affidavits saying they'd been pressured and wanted to change their certification to a "no" vote.

Michigan law doesn't allow rescinding certification votes, so this didn't work. But the attempt shows the mindset. These officials genuinely seemed to believe they had authority and responsibility to investigate discrepancies before certifying.

And from one angle, they weren't entirely wrong. If there were serious discrepancies in the poll books, shouldn't someone look into that?

The problem is that "looking into it" in the middle of certification, with no clear process or timeline, becomes a tool for obstruction.

The Legal Reforms That Might Help (But Probably Won't Exist in Time)

So what would actually fix this? A few things would help:

1. Crystal-clear statutory language. States could explicitly prohibit certification delays for any reason other than incomplete canvassing. No investigations, no discretion, no exceptions. Some states have moved in this direction since 2020, but many haven't.

2. Automatic "self-executing" certification. If an official refuses or a board deadlocks, certification could happen automatically by operation of law. Nevada's AB 321 implemented something like this for county boards. If they don't certify by the deadline, the Secretary of State can certify on their behalf.

3. Stronger penalties. Most states have criminal penalties for refusing to perform a ministerial duty, but they're rarely enforced and prosecution takes too long to matter during an election. Immediate removal from office, not just theoretical future prosecution, would be more effective.

4. Emergency judicial procedures. Courts could have expedited processes specifically for certification disputes, with 24-hour or 48-hour timelines for hearings and rulings. Some states have created these, but many haven't.

The reality is that very few states have implemented all of these reforms. And the ones that haven't are often the swing states where certification refusal is most likely.

---

Why This Matters for 2028

Here's the bottom line: "ministerial duty" sounds like a strong protection, but it's actually a legal argument that has to be litigated in the moment.

In 2020 and 2022, we got lucky. Refusing officials either backed down quickly or were overruled by courts in time for certification to proceed. But each incident took days or weeks to resolve, involved uncertain legal arguments, and depended on specific officials choosing to comply.

In 2028, if we see coordinated refusals in multiple counties across multiple swing states, the system could break down. Not because the law is on the refusers' side — it isn't — but because litigation takes time, compliance isn't automatic, and the window between Election Day and the Electoral College deadline is only 35 days.

The correct legal answer ("certification is ministerial, just do it") might not matter if bad-faith officials can create enough chaos and delay.

---

Bottom Line: What You Should Take Away

The concept of "ministerial duty" is real and legally sound. But it's a much weaker protection than it sounds like.

When you hear election officials or legal experts say "certification is ministerial, they have to do it," that's technically true. But enforcing that requirement in real time, under enormous political pressure, with tight deadlines and uncertain legal standards, is much harder than it sounds.

For democracy defenders: The vulnerability isn't really the legal theory. It's the implementation gap. Even when officials violate clear law by refusing to certify, resolving that violation takes time and institutional cooperation. Both are in short supply during a contested election.

For 2028 preparedness: States need much clearer laws with automatic enforcement mechanisms that don't depend on courts or other officials choosing to act. Self-executing certification, instant removal authority, and 24-hour judicial review would all help. But most states haven't done this work yet, and the clock is running out.

Project idea: Democracy organizations could create a "Ministerial Duty Enforcement Toolkit" — state-by-state guides for what legal remedies exist when officials refuse to certify, including template complaints, relevant statutes, emergency hearing procedures, and escalation paths. This would help local lawyers and election officials respond quickly rather than figuring it out in the moment. Current organizations like the Brennan Center and Protect Democracy do some of this work, but a comprehensive, practitioner-focused resource specifically for certification crises doesn't fully exist yet.

The legal tools to prevent certification refusal mostly exist. But they need to be ready to deploy in hours, not days or weeks. That infrastructure isn't fully built yet, and we're less than three years from the next presidential election.
