How State Legislative Majorities Can Change Election Rules Mid-Cycle: The Wisconsin Redistricting Case Study

Epistemic status: High confidence on the Wisconsin timeline and mechanisms; medium confidence on predictions about future applications.

You might think election rules are set well in advance. New voting machines get purchased years ahead. Registration deadlines appear in statute. Polling locations get determined months before Election Day.

But there's a less visible vulnerability: state legislatures can change fundamental election rules with simple majorities, often right before elections. And when one party controls the legislature through gerrymandering, they can reshape the electoral playing field unilaterally—even when they represent a minority of voters.

Let me walk you through how this works using Wisconsin as the paradigm case, because it demonstrates both the mechanism and why it matters for 2026 and 2028.

The Wisconsin Timeline: How Entrenchment Works

Here's what happened in Wisconsin between 2011 and 2024:

In 2011, Republicans controlled Wisconsin's legislature, senate, and governorship after the 2010 election. They used this trifecta to draw new legislative district maps following the census. According to an analysis by the Brennan Center, these maps were among the most heavily gerrymandered in the country.

The effects were stark. In 2012, Democrats won 174,000 more votes statewide in Assembly races (51.4% of the vote), but Republicans secured 60 of 99 seats. In 2018, Democrats won the governorship by 30,000 votes and got 53% of Assembly votes—yet Republicans retained 63 of 99 seats, according to the Wisconsin State Legislature's official results.

This created a self-perpetuating cycle. The gerrymandered legislature used its supermajority to:

  • Override gubernatorial vetoes on election administration
  • Change voting rules to favor their coalition
  • Restrict ballot access in ways that made defeating them harder
  • Defend their own maps in court using state resources

    • The maps remained in place until 2024, when Wisconsin's Supreme Court finally ruled them unconstitutional and required new districts. But that's thirteen years of entrenched minority rule.

    The Lame Duck Power Grab

    The starkest example came in December 2018, after Democrats won the governorship but before the new governor took office.

    The Republican legislature passed a sweeping set of laws during the "lame duck" session that stripped powers from the incoming Democratic governor and attorney general. These laws, documented in detail by the Milwaukee Journal Sentinel, included:

  • Moving the 2020 presidential primary election date (creating chaos for election administration)
  • Limiting early voting periods
  • Restricting the governor's ability to issue administrative rules (including election rules)
  • Requiring legislative approval for withdrawing from federal lawsuits (including cases about election law)

    • Governor-elect Tony Evers couldn't do anything. The laws passed on party-line votes and were signed by outgoing Governor Scott Walker before Evers took office.

    Think about the democratic logic here. Voters chose a new direction in November 2018. But the defeated party used its gerrymandered legislative majority to prevent that choice from having full effect—changing the rules after losing to make governing harder for the winners.

    Why This Mechanism Matters Structurally

    Unlike the fake electors scheme or pressure on election officials, this isn't illegal. It's just how American federalism works: state legislatures have broad authority over election rules.

    But notice what's different from normal democratic competition:

    Normal democracy: Party A passes laws → voters evaluate → voters potentially elect Party B → Party B can change the laws

    Entrenchment: Party A gerrymanders → Party A retains power despite losing statewide votes → Party A changes rules to make defeating them harder → the cycle continues

    The key vulnerability is that this operates through legal mechanisms. There's no smoking gun document. No one commits a crime. It's just...a legislature passing laws.

    Except the legislature doesn't actually represent the voters. A party getting 45% of votes but 63% of seats can claim a "mandate" to change election rules, and there's no clear point to challenge it.

    The 2020 Application: COVID Rule Changes

    This played out with real consequences in 2020. As COVID-19 emerged, Democratic Governor Evers wanted to expand absentee voting and extend deadlines. The Republican legislature blocked these changes.

    The fight went to the courts repeatedly. The Wisconsin Supreme Court (which had a conservative majority) sided with the legislature, blocking the governor's emergency orders to postpone the April 2020 primary and extend absentee voting.

    Wisconsin held its April 7, 2020 primary in-person during a pandemic surge. Milwaukee, a Democratic stronghold with a large Black population, had only 5 polling places open instead of the usual 180, according to reporting by the New York Times. Voters waited in line for hours.

    For the November general election, turnout was high because the presidential stakes were clear. But imagine a lower-salience election where these restrictions reduce turnout by just 2-3% in Democratic areas. That's all you need when you're changing the rules in a state Biden won by 20,682 votes out of 3.2 million cast.

    The 2026 and 2028 Risk

    Here's why this matters looking forward:

    Several swing states currently have the same structural setup as 2011-2024 Wisconsin: one party has gerrymandered legislative majorities that don't reflect statewide vote shares, giving them unilateral power over election rules.

    North Carolina is the clearest case. Republicans have drawn maps that typically give them 60-70% of legislative seats despite winning around 50% of statewide votes. In 2023, after gaining a supermajority, they overrode the Democratic governor's veto to pass new voting restrictions.

    Arizona saw similar dynamics after 2020. The Republican legislature passed multiple bills changing election procedures, many vetoed by the Democratic governor, in what the Arizona Republic documented as the most active period of election law changes in state history.

    Georgia passed SB 202 in 2021, a sweeping election bill that included provisions giving the legislature more power over election administration—exactly the Wisconsin playbook. The full text of the law shows it even allows the state legislature to suspend county election officials.

    Here's the pattern: after 2020, state legislatures in competitive states started changing election rules more aggressively. Some got blocked by governors or courts. But some didn't. And the 2026 midterms determine who controls these state legislatures going into 2028.

    If Republicans gain trifecta control (legislature + governor) in states like Wisconsin, Michigan, Pennsylvania, or Arizona before 2028, they could pass significant election changes in early 2027. By the time those changes affect the 2028 presidential election, it's too late to reverse them.

    What Defenders Can Actually Do

    The Wisconsin case also shows what can work—it just takes time and coordination:

  • Win state supreme court seats. Wisconsin's maps only fell after progressives won a state Supreme Court majority in April 2023. These races are low-salience but incredibly high-impact. Democracy defenders should prioritize them.
  • Challenge maps early and repeatedly. Wisconsin advocates filed multiple lawsuits over the 2011 maps starting in 2015. Most failed, but they created a paper trail and kept pressure on.
  • Focus on state legislative races in gerrymandered states. Even gaining a few seats can break supermajorities, restoring veto power to governors. North Carolina Democrats flipped just enough seats in 2024 to break the Republican supermajority, according to the News & Observer.
  • Support redistricting reform. Michigan passed an independent redistricting commission through a ballot initiative in 2018. The new maps used in 2022 were dramatically more competitive.
