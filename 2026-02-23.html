When Arizona Republicans Sent Fake Electoral Votes to Washington: A Detailed Anatomy

Epistemic status: High confidence. This newsletter draws on court filings, indictments, testimony transcripts, and investigative reporting with primary source documentation.

Let me walk you through exactly what happened in Arizona during the 2020 election aftermath. Not the broad strokes you might know, but the granular details of how Republican officials and activists created fraudulent electoral certificates and tried to get them counted. This case study matters because it shows the operational reality of election subversion—the meetings, the paper trails, the moment-by-moment decisions that nearly worked.

The Basic Setup

On December 14, 2020, Arizona's legitimate electors met in Phoenix to cast the state's 11 electoral votes for Joe Biden, who had won the state by 10,457 votes according to the certified results. That same day, in the same city, eleven Republicans gathered to sign certificates claiming they were Arizona's true electors and that Donald Trump had won the state.

These weren't random Trump supporters. According to the Arizona Attorney General's indictment filed in April 2024, they included the chair of the Arizona Republican Party (Kelli Ward), a sitting state senator (Anthony Kern), and other party officials. They knew Biden had won. They signed the documents anyway.

The Conditional Language Trick

Here's where it gets interesting. The fake electors initially didn't want to sign documents claiming they were the legitimate electors when they knew they weren't. So their legal advisers came up with conditional language.

According to testimony documented in the January 6th Committee's final report, Trump campaign attorney Kenneth Chesebro suggested the Arizona electors include language like "we sign as conditional electors" or note that they were acting in case ongoing litigation changed the results. The idea was to create plausible deniability while still producing documents that could potentially be used.

But here's the problem: The version that actually got sent to Washington didn't include this conditional language. The final certificates reviewed by federal investigators stated flatly that the signers were "the duly elected and qualified Electors for President and Vice President of the United States from the State of Arizona." No hedging. No conditions.

How They Tried to Make It Official

Creating fake documents is one thing. Getting them into the official process is another.

The fake Arizona electors needed their certificates to reach Vice President Pence and the National Archives—the same destinations where legitimate electoral votes go. According to the federal indictment of Trump and co-conspirators, they attempted several delivery methods:

First, they tried sending them through official channels. That didn't work because Arizona's governor, Doug Ducey (a Republican), refused to sign the fraudulent certificates. Governors typically authenticate their state's electoral certificates, and without that signature, the documents lacked official credibility.

So they sent them anyway. According to investigative reporting by CNN's Marshall Cohen, the fake certificates were mailed to the National Archives, which received them but noted they lacked the governor's signature and other authenticating features that legitimate certificates have.

The Missing Piece: State Legislature Cover

The Arizona effort relied on a theory you might remember from previous newsletters: that state legislatures have ultimate authority over electoral vote selection. Trump attorney John Eastman argued that if the Arizona legislature declared these were the real electors, that would somehow override the certified popular vote.

The Arizona legislature never did this. According to reporting by the Arizona Republic's Jen Fifield, legislative leaders—despite being Republicans—declined to take action. State Senate President Karen Fann and House Speaker Rusty Bowers both refused to participate in the scheme, with Bowers later testifying to the January 6th Committee about the pressure he faced.

Without legislative backing, the fake certificates were just pieces of paper signed by partisan activists. Legally meaningless but potentially dangerous if enough confusion could be created.

What Made Arizona Different

Arizona's fake elector scheme had some unique features compared to other states.

First, the timing was unusually organized. According to the multi-state timeline compiled by American Oversight, all seven fake elector meetings happened on December 14, 2020—the same day as legitimate electoral meetings. This wasn't coincidence. The Trump campaign coordinated the timing to create maximum confusion and provide a legal basis for claiming there were "competing slates" of electors.

Second, Arizona's fake electors were particularly well-connected politically. Kelli Ward, the state GOP chair, had direct communication channels to Trump campaign officials. According to text messages obtained by CNN, Ward texted Trump chief of staff Mark Meadows on January 4, 2021: "We need you to stop the counting." This wasn't a grassroots operation—it was coordinated from the top.

Third, Arizona had active litigation contesting the results longer than most states. The Trump campaign filed multiple lawsuits challenging Arizona's count, all of which failed. But according to analysis by election law expert Rick Hasen, the existence of these lawsuits provided a fig leaf of legitimacy—a claim that the fake electors were just preserving Trump's options in case he won in court.

The Paper Trail That Doomed Them

What ultimately made Arizona's fake electors vulnerable to prosecution was documentation. Lots of it.

The Arizona Attorney General's investigation, according to the 2024 indictment, uncovered emails, text messages, and recorded phone calls showing that participants knew Biden had won and understood that what they were doing was legally questionable. Some electors asked lawyers whether signing could create criminal liability. The lawyers reportedly assured them it wouldn't—advice that looks worse in hindsight.

There were also financial records. According to reporting by the Guardian's Hugo Lowell, some fake electors received payments or reimbursements for their participation, creating a paper trail of who knew what and when.

Most damaging were the internal Trump campaign documents. The January 6th Committee obtained memos showing that campaign lawyers, particularly Kenneth Chesebro and John Eastman, designed the fake elector scheme as part of a broader strategy to create enough confusion that Pence could justify delaying or refusing to count electoral votes from contested states.

Why It Nearly Worked Anyway

Here's what keeps me up at night about the Arizona case: It almost succeeded not because the plan was good, but because the system has so little redundancy.

The fake certificates reached Washington. They were received by the National Archives. They were physically present in the Capitol on January 6th. The only reason they weren't counted is that Vice President Pence refused to participate in the scheme. According to Pence's testimony to the January 6th Committee, he received enormous pressure to either delay the count or send electoral votes back to state legislatures—including from Arizona.

If Pence had delayed the count, even temporarily, it would have created a constitutional crisis. The Electoral Count Act (at the time) was ambiguous about what happens if the Vice President receives multiple certificates from the same state and the state's governor hasn't resolved the dispute. Arizona's situation was precisely the scenario the law didn't clearly address.

The fake electors also exploited something structural: the assumption that only legitimate electors would send certificates. There was no real verification process. The National Archives checks for proper formatting and signatures, but doesn't independently verify whether signers are actual electors. According to election administration expert David Becker, this was considered unnecessary because it was unthinkable that state political parties would submit fraudulent electoral certificates.

The Aftermath and Accountability

As of early 2026, the Arizona fake electors case is still working through the legal system. Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes filed charges in April 2024 against 18 individuals, including 11 fake electors and seven Trump campaign officials and attorneys who organized the scheme.

Several defendants have taken plea deals. According to reporting by Arizona's KJZZ radio, some lower-level participants agreed to cooperate in exchange for reduced charges, providing testimony about who directed the operation and what they were told about its legality.

But meaningful accountability has been slow. The fake electors weren't charged until more than three years after they signed the fraudulent certificates. None faced immediate consequences on December 14, 2020, or in the months afterward. This delay matters because it signals to future bad actors that election subversion attempts carry minimal immediate risk.

The broader federal case against Trump and co-conspirators, which includes the Arizona scheme as part of a multi-state conspiracy, has been delayed by immunity claims and procedural challenges. The Supreme Court's July 2024 ruling on presidential immunity complicated prosecution timelines significantly.

Structural Lessons for 2026 and 2028

The Arizona fake elector scheme exposes several structural vulnerabilities that remain relevant:

Verification gaps. The system still relies heavily on trust that only legitimate electors will send certificates. The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 improved some procedures, but according to analysis by the Brennan Center's Derek Muller, it doesn't create robust verification mechanisms for identifying fraudulent certificates.

State-level coordination. Arizona showed how state party infrastructure can be weaponized for election subversion. State party chairs, in particular, have significant authority and few formal checks on that authority. They can convene meetings, coordinate communications, and claim to speak for state party legitimacy.

The litigation smokescreen. Active lawsuits, even frivolous ones, provided cover for the fake elector scheme. As long as Trump had cases pending, participants could claim they were just preserving legal options. This suggests that flooding courts with election challenges serves a dual purpose: winning in court and creating cover for extra-legal schemes.

Conditional language ambiguity. The fake electors wanted plausible deniability through conditional language, but that language disappeared from final documents. This raises questions about who controlled final drafting and whether some participants were misled about what they were signing. It also creates legal ambiguity about intent.

Bottom Line: Key Takeaways for Democracy Work

If you're thinking about starting a project or supporting existing work in this space, the Arizona case study suggests several priorities:

Electoral certificate verification. The 2028 election would benefit from clear, public verification of which electoral certificates are legitimate. Some states have improved their authentication procedures since 2020, but there's no national standard. A project creating and publicizing official state-by-state verification checklists could help journalists and Congress quickly identify fraudulent certificates.

State party oversight. State political party officials have enormous power with minimal accountability. Investigative projects tracking state party leadership—who they are, what they're saying about election certification, what procedures they're following—could provide early warning of 2028 schemes.

Rapid legal response capacity. Arizona's fake electors operated in a legal gray zone, and it took years to bring charges. Organizations that can provide immediate legal analysis during post-election periods—identifying clearly illegal actions and demanding swift accountability—could deter future schemes. The States United Democracy Center does some of this work, but the need far exceeds current capacity.

Documentation and preservation. The Arizona case was prosecutable because investigators found detailed communications. Projects that systematically archive social media posts, public statements, and documents from state party officials and election deniers during critical periods could preserve evidence that might otherwise disappear. The Internet Archive does general preservation, but targeted election-focused archiving could be valuable.

The Arizona fake elector scheme failed in 2020 because a handful of officials—Ducey, Bowers, Pence—refused to participate. That's not a systemic safeguard. It's luck. For 2028, we need structures that don't depend on individual conscience.
