The Conspiracy Theory Spiral: How Election Fraud Claims Become Self-Perpetuating Infrastructure

Epistemic status: High confidence on the documented mechanisms and historical examples. Medium confidence on the specific organizational trajectories for 2026-2028, as these depend on variables like funding and political alignment.

---

Here's something that keeps me up at night about the 2028 election: We're not just facing individual bad actors anymore. We're watching conspiracy theories transform into permanent organizational infrastructure.

Let me explain what I mean.

The Old Model vs. The New Model

Traditional election challenges followed a predictable pattern. Someone loses. They contest the result. Courts review the evidence. The challenge fails or succeeds based on facts. Then it's over.

What's happening now is fundamentally different.

According to research by the States United Democracy Center, approximately 170 election deniers who reject the 2020 presidential election results now hold positions with authority over elections in key states. These aren't just fringe activists. They're secretaries of state, county election officials, and state legislators.

But here's the crucial shift: These officials aren't just making isolated decisions. They're building permanent infrastructure based on conspiracy theories.

The Conspiracy-to-Infrastructure Pipeline

Think of it as a four-stage process:

Stage 1: The Initial Claim

A conspiracy theory emerges. "Dead people are voting." "Non-citizens are voting in massive numbers." "Dominion machines are changing votes."

These claims get debunked. Courts rejected 63 out of 64 election lawsuits filed by the Trump campaign, with judges appointed by both parties finding no evidence of fraud.

Stage 2: The Belief Persists Anyway

Here's where it gets interesting. The lack of evidence doesn't kill the theory. Instead, believers reinterpret the absence of evidence as proof of a deeper conspiracy.

"We can't find the fraud because the cover-up is so sophisticated."

This creates what researchers call an "unfalsifiable" belief system. Every piece of contradicting evidence gets absorbed and reinterpreted.

Stage 3: Building Infrastructure Around the Belief

This is the critical transition. Believers don't just keep believing privately. They build organizations, systems, and procedures based on the conspiracy theory.

Take the example I covered earlier this week: The Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network. They've trained over 5,000 volunteers. They've built proprietary data matching technology. They've created legal support networks.

All of this infrastructure is predicated on the belief that massive voter fraud exists.

Stage 4: The Infrastructure Creates Its Own Reality

Here's where it gets really concerning. Once you have infrastructure built around a conspiracy theory, that infrastructure starts generating "evidence" that appears to confirm the theory.

Let me give you a concrete example.

Case Study: The Voter Roll Purge Spiral

A 2023 investigation by Votebeat documented what happened in several swing state counties.

Election officials who believed in widespread non-citizen voting started using aggressive data matching techniques. They compared voter rolls against jury duty declinations, driver's license databases, and other records.

The matching algorithms had high error rates. People with similar names got flagged. Naturalized citizens who had once declined jury duty got flagged. People who had typos in their records got flagged.

In Tarrant County, Texas, local officials challenged approximately 4,000 voters they suspected of being non-citizens. After investigation, it turned out that around 2,000 were actually eligible voters.

But here's the key: The believers didn't see this as "the system caught an error." They saw it as "we found fraud, but the establishment is protecting it."

The infrastructure designed to find fraud creates apparent evidence of fraud, even when that evidence is false positives. This apparent evidence justifies expanding the infrastructure. Which creates more false positives. Which justifies more expansion.

It's a self-perpetuating cycle.

Why This Matters More Than Individual Bad Actors

I used to think the main threat was individual officials refusing to certify elections. That's still a threat, and I've written about it extensively.

But infrastructure threats are more insidious for three reasons:

1. They're Harder to See

When a county commissioner refuses to certify, that makes headlines. When an organization trains 200 volunteer poll challengers in a county, that's just "civic engagement."

2. They're Harder to Stop

You can remove a refusing official. But how do you dismantle an organization with 5,000 trained volunteers, legal defense funds, and data systems?

3. They Operate Within Normal Democratic Processes

This is crucial. Most of what these organizations do is technically legal. Filing voter challenges is legal. Training poll watchers is legal. Requesting recounts is legal.

The problem isn't individual actions. It's the cumulative effect of thousands of actions based on false premises.

The AGI Connection

This is where your interest in AGI intersects uncomfortably with election integrity.

Right now, these conspiracy-to-infrastructure pipelines are human-powered. They require thousands of volunteers manually challenging voter registrations, manually monitoring polling places, manually reviewing footage.

But what happens when you can automate this?

Research by the Stanford Internet Observatory shows that generative AI can already create highly convincing fake evidence. We're talking about synthetic videos, forged documents, and fabricated datasets.

Imagine the voter roll purge spiral I described above, but powered by AI that can:

  • Automatically generate thousands of voter challenges based on pattern matching
  • Create convincing "documentation" of suspected fraud
  • Produce synthetic videos of alleged illegal voting
  • Generate entire datasets of "suspicious activity"

    • The infrastructure doesn't just perpetuate itself anymore. It scales exponentially.

    What Actually Works: Breaking the Cycle

    So how do you interrupt a conspiracy-to-infrastructure pipeline?

    Research by political scientists Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler suggests that simply providing correct information doesn't work. In fact, it often backfires through what they call the "backfire effect."

    But there are some strategies that show promise:

    1. Transparency That Preempts Conspiracy Theories

    One of the most effective interventions comes from Colorado. The Colorado Secretary of State's office publishes extremely detailed information about their voter roll maintenance process.

    They don't just say "we maintain voter rolls responsibly." They publish the specific criteria, the matching algorithms, the error rates, and the appeals process.

    This doesn't convince hardcore believers. But it prevents casual skeptics from getting pulled into the conspiracy-to-infrastructure pipeline.

    2. Imposing Costs on Bad Faith Challenges

    Several states have started requiring evidence before accepting voter challenges. Michigan's 2023 election law reforms require challengers to provide specific, individualized evidence of ineligibility.

    This doesn't prevent challenges entirely. But it dramatically increases the cost of mass challenges based on fuzzy data matching.

    In counties that implemented these requirements, voter challenges dropped by approximately 60-70%.

    3. Building Counter-Infrastructure

    This is the part that's most underfunded. Organizations like All Voting is Local and Protect Democracy are doing this work, but they're vastly outmatched in resources.

    What does counter-infrastructure look like?

    It means having trained volunteers who can attend county election board meetings. Having legal expertise ready when frivolous challenges come in. Having rapid response systems when officials face intimidation.

    The Brennan Center estimates that approximately $500 million per cycle would be needed to fully staff election official support networks across swing states. Current funding is roughly $50-75 million.

    The 2028 Timeline

    Here's why this matters urgently for 2028.

    The infrastructure being built right now—in early 2026—will be operational for the 2028 election. The Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network didn't spring up overnight. They spent 2021-2023 building their volunteer network.

    Any counter-infrastructure needs to start now. Not in late 2027. Not in 2028.

    Organizations need to be recruiting volunteers now. Building legal defense networks now. Creating transparency systems now.

    The conspiracy-to-infrastructure pipeline has a two-year build time. So does the counter-infrastructure.

    Bottom Line

    The main threat to the 2028 election isn't individual bad actors. It's organizational infrastructure built on conspiracy theories that generate their own confirming "evidence."

    This infrastructure is harder to see, harder to stop, and operates within normal democratic processes. It's also about to get turbocharged by AI capabilities.

    The only effective counter is building alternative infrastructure that provides transparency, imposes costs on bad faith challenges, and supports election officials. But this requires starting immediately, with sustained funding over multiple election cycles.

    For founders and funders: The highest-leverage intervention right now is probably county-level counter-infrastructure in swing states. This means:

  • Legal defense networks for election officials facing frivolous challenges
  • Volunteer networks to attend and document canvassing board meetings
  • Transparency platforms that publish election procedures before conspiracy theories can fill the information vacuum
  • Rapid response teams when officials face intimidation

    • The window to build this for 2028 is roughly the next 12-18 months. After that, you're in reactive mode rather than preventive mode.

    Existing organizations doing this work: All Voting is Local, Protect Democracy's election official support program, the Democracy Defense Fund's legal network. They're all dramatically underfunded relative to the opposition infrastructure.

    The conspiracy theories probably can't be eliminated. But the infrastructure built on top of them can be contained—if the counter-infrastructure exists.
