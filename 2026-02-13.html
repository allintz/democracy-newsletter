The Courage Trap: Why Individual Heroes Can't Save Democracy (And What Can)

Epistemic status: High confidence on the historical examples and their details; medium confidence on the generalizability of the pattern to future scenarios. This analysis draws on documented cases but involves some interpretation about future implications.

---

There's a story we keep telling ourselves about 2020. It goes like this: Democracy held because a few brave Republicans—Brad Raffensperger in Georgia, Rusty Bowers in Arizona, Mike Pence on January 6—refused to go along with efforts to overturn the election.

This story is true. But it's also dangerous.

Here's why: If we think democracy survived in 2020 because we got lucky with a handful of courageous individuals, then our strategy for 2028 becomes "hope we get lucky again." That's not a strategy. That's a prayer.

Today I want to dig into what I'm calling the "courage trap"—the mistaken belief that institutional safeguards can be replaced by individual integrity. We'll look at what actually happened in 2020, why depending on heroes is structurally fragile, and what kinds of systemic protections might actually work.

What Actually Happened in 2020

Let's start with the facts. Democracy held in 2020 at several critical points because specific individuals refused pressure to manipulate results.

Georgia: Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger refused Trump's request to "find 11,780 votes" in a January 2, 2021 phone call, as documented in the full recording obtained by the Washington Post. Raffensperger had already certified Georgia's results and rejected pressure to call a special legislative session.

Arizona: State House Speaker Rusty Bowers refused to convene the legislature to consider alternate electors, despite direct pressure from Trump and Giuliani. According to Bowers' testimony to the January 6 Committee, Trump called him repeatedly and asked him to decertify Arizona's electors. Bowers refused, citing his oath to the Constitution.

Michigan: Senate Majority Leader Mike Shirkey and House Speaker Lee Chatfield met with Trump at the White House on November 20, 2020. According to reporting from the Detroit News, Trump pressed them to intervene in Michigan's certification. They issued a statement afterward saying they would "follow the law" and allow the election process to proceed.

Federal level: Vice President Mike Pence refused pressure to reject electoral votes on January 6, despite a memorandum from Trump lawyer John Eastman claiming he had the power to do so. Pence consulted with constitutional scholars and issued a letter explaining he had no such authority.

So yes: Individuals made the difference. But here's the uncomfortable question: What if Raffensperger had been six months into the job instead of four years? What if Bowers had been facing a primary challenge from the right? What if Pence had believed he might be the 2024 nominee?

Why the Courage Model Is Structurally Fragile

The problem with depending on individual courage is that it treats democracy as a series of character tests rather than as a system.

Think about it this way: In 2020, we needed about 5-10 key people to hold the line. All of them faced enormous pressure—death threats, primary challenges, social ostracism from their party, presidential rage. According to a Reuters investigation, election officials across the country received thousands of threats, with some requiring police protection.

Most of them held. But that's a 90% success rate when we needed 100%.

Here's the math: If each key decision-maker has a 90% chance of resisting pressure (which seems generous given the incentives), and you need 10 people to all resist, your odds of complete success are 0.9^10 = 35%. That's worse than a coin flip.

And that assumes the pressure stays constant. In 2024 or 2028, the pressure will likely be more sophisticated. The people in these positions might be pre-selected for loyalty rather than independence. The party infrastructure might be more unified in supporting challenges.

Relying on courage means relying on an increasingly unfavorable probability distribution.

What Happens When You Don't Get Lucky

We actually have examples of what happens when the individuals in key positions don't hold the line.

The 1876 Hayes-Tilden Election: In the disputed 1876 presidential election, three states (Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina) sent competing slates of electors to Congress. According to historian Eric Foner's analysis, Republican state officials in these states certified results that were likely affected by voter suppression and intimidation of Black voters, while Democrats challenged them.

The dispute was resolved by an ad-hoc Electoral Commission that voted along partisan lines to award all disputed votes to Republican Rutherford Hayes. The "compromise" involved withdrawing federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction.

This wasn't a triumph of individual courage. It was a backroom deal that traded the presidency for the abandonment of Black civil rights—consequences that lasted for generations.

Hungary's Democratic Backsliding: For a more recent example, consider Hungary under Viktor Orbán. As documented in political scientists Kim Lane Scheppele and Laurent Pech's analysis for the Cambridge University Press, Orbán didn't rely on any single dramatic moment of democratic breakdown.

Instead, between 2010 and 2014, his government systematically changed institutional rules: packing the constitutional court, rewriting election laws, taking control of media oversight bodies, and redrawing district boundaries. No single change required dramatic individual resistance, because each change was technically legal under existing procedures.

By the time it became clear the system had fundamentally changed, the positions that might have served as checkpoints were already filled with Orbán loyalists or stripped of their power.

What Systemic Protection Actually Looks Like

So if individual courage isn't enough, what is?

The answer is boring: redundancy, constraints, and transparency. Systems that don't depend on any single decision-maker making the right choice under pressure.

1. Automatic, Transparent Processes

Consider parallel vote tabulation (PVT), which we discussed in an earlier newsletter. In the 2003 Georgian Rose Revolution, opposition groups conducted an independent count of votes using trained observers at polling stations. When the government announced implausible official results, the PVT provided immediate, credible evidence of fraud, according to research by the National Democratic Institute.

The key: The PVT didn't require any government official to be brave. It was an external verification system that made fraud detectable regardless of who held power.

2. Distributed Power and Clear Rules

The 2022 Electoral Count Reform Act, signed into law after January 6, made several important structural changes. According to an analysis by the Brennan Center for Justice, it:

  • Clarified that the Vice President's role is purely ceremonial (removing the ambiguity Eastman's memo exploited)
  • Raised the threshold for congressional objections to electoral votes
  • Limited the grounds for objections to votes
  • Clarified that state legislatures cannot override election results after Election Day

    • These changes don't require anyone to be heroic. They just make the exploit harder to attempt.

    3. Pre-commitment Mechanisms

    Some states have moved to what's called "risk-limiting audits"—statistical audits that verify election results by checking random samples of ballots. According to research published by the American Statistical Association, these audits can provide strong statistical evidence that results are correct without requiring a full recount.

    The key: These audits are triggered automatically and follow predefined statistical protocols. No individual has to decide whether to conduct them based on political considerations.

  • State legislative races that determine who controls election law changes
  • Secretary of State races in swing states
  • State Supreme Court seats that will rule on election disputes

    • But even more important: advocacy for systemic safeguards before the election cycle begins.

  • State legislatures pass election administration procedures for 2028
  • Election security funding gets allocated
  • Training programs for election workers are designed
  • Audit procedures are established

    • If we wait until 2028 to worry about election integrity, we're back to hoping for heroes.

    What You Can Actually Do About This

    Here's the bottom line for someone thinking about starting a project:

    High-leverage intervention: Support automatic verification systems that don't depend on individual courage. This might mean:

  • Advocacy for mandatory risk-limiting audits in swing states
  • Supporting independent parallel vote counting infrastructure
  • Funding election observer training programs
  • Backing bills that create automatic triggers for recounts or audits based on statistical thresholds

    • Existing organization to support: VotingWorks is a nonpartisan nonprofit that develops open-source election technology, including risk-limiting audit tools. They're working to replace outdated voting systems with transparent, verifiable alternatives that build verification into the process rather than depending on post-hoc checking.

    Project idea: A "Democracy Infrastructure Scorecard" that tracks which states have adopted systemic safeguards (audit requirements, transparent certification processes, clear chains of custody, etc.) versus which states rely primarily on discretionary decisions by officials. This would help donors and advocates target resources to states with the weakest institutional protections before 2028.

    The goal isn't to eliminate the need for principled people in positions of power. It's to build systems where even mediocre or politically motivated actors can't easily subvert elections—because the verification mechanisms, legal constraints, and transparency measures don't give them the opportunity.

    Bottom Line

    Democracy held in 2020 partly because a few key Republicans chose principle over party loyalty. That's worth celebrating. But it's a terrible model for future election security.

    Depending on individual courage means accepting that democracy runs on luck and character—both of which are in unpredictable supply. The alternative is building systems with automatic safeguards, distributed power, transparent processes, and verification mechanisms that don't require anyone to be heroic.

    The 2026 state elections matter because they'll determine who controls election administration in 2028. But the laws, procedures, and verification systems that get put in place between now and late 2027 matter even more.

    We can't rely on getting lucky with another Brad Raffensperger or Rusty Bowers. We need to build a system where we don't need to get lucky at all.
