The Fake Electors Scheme: How It Worked and Why the Mechanics Matter

Epistemic status: High confidence on documented facts and timeline; moderate confidence on legal interpretations and future risks.

When most people heard about "fake electors" in 2020, the response was often confusion. Wait—people just showed up and pretended to be electors? And that was supposed to work?

Today we're going to break down exactly how the fake elector scheme worked, why it was more sophisticated than it initially sounds, and what specific vulnerabilities it exploited. This matters because understanding the mechanics helps us see both where the scheme failed in 2020 and where similar attempts might succeed in future elections.

What Actually Happened: The Basic Timeline

Here's the simple version: After the 2020 election, 84 individuals across seven states signed certificates falsely claiming to be legitimate presidential electors, even though their candidate (Trump) had lost in those states.

These weren't random people off the street. Most were state Republican party officials, elected representatives, or party activists. On December 14, 2020—the same day the real electors met in state capitals—these groups gathered and signed official-looking documents declaring themselves the "duly elected and qualified electors" for their states.

The seven states were: Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, New Mexico, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. All seven had certified Biden victories. All seven had these alternate elector slates created anyway.

The Legal Theory (Such As It Was)

The scheme had a superficial legal veneer. The core argument went something like this:

"These states have ongoing legal challenges to the election results. If those challenges succeed, we'll need electors in place by the Electoral College deadline. So these are contingent electors—just in case."

There's actually historical precedent for contingent electors. In 1960, Hawaii's election was so close that the result was still being recounted when electors met. Both Democratic and Republican slates submitted certificates, and when the recount confirmed Kennedy's win, Hawaii's Democratic electors were accepted by Congress.

But—and this is crucial—in Hawaii, both slates explicitly acknowledged uncertainty and noted the ongoing recount. According to reporting by The New York Times, the 1960 Democratic electors noted they were acting "subject to the final determination" of the recount.

The 2020 certificates were different. As documented in charging documents from Michigan's attorney general, most of the fake elector certificates contained no contingency language. They declared flatly that these individuals were "the duly elected and qualified Electors."

This wasn't a safety measure. It was an attempted fait accompli.

The Three-Part Mechanism

Understanding why anyone thought this might work requires seeing the full three-part plan:

Part 1: Create the alternate slates. Get these certificates signed and submitted to the National Archives and to Congress. Make them look official enough that they could be confused with the real certificates.

Part 2: Create confusion about which slate is legitimate. This is where you need friendly governors, state legislators, or secretaries of state. If Arizona's legislature had passed a resolution "recalling" Biden's electors, or if the Georgia Secretary of State had "decertified" the results based on alleged fraud, it would create ambiguity about which elector slate Congress should count.

Part 3: Exploit that confusion on January 6. When Congress meets to count electoral votes, the Vice President (in his role as President of the Senate) faces competing slates from the same state. According to the plan outlined in the Eastman memos, Mike Pence could then either count the Trump electors or declare the state's electoral votes as disputed and set them aside entirely.

The scheme failed because Part 2 largely didn't happen. Despite enormous pressure, Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger refused to decertify results. Arizona Governor Doug Ducey certified Biden's win. Michigan's legislature didn't intervene. Without official state actors creating ambiguity, the fake certificates just sat there as obvious forgeries.

But here's what keeps me up at night: It almost worked in several states.

How Close Was It Really?

Let's look at Georgia specifically, because we have the most documentation.

On January 2, 2021, Trump called Raffensperger and asked him to "find 11,780 votes"—one more than Biden's margin. Trump suggested Raffensperger could announce a recalculation that "flipped" the result.

If Raffensperger had agreed, Georgia's fake electors would suddenly have had a state official claiming they were legitimate. When Congress met on January 6, there would have been genuine confusion about Georgia's electoral votes.

Now imagine this happening in three states instead of zero. Biden won with 306 electoral votes to Trump's 232. If Arizona (11 EV), Georgia (16 EV), and Wisconsin (10 EV) had been thrown into genuine dispute—with competing slates backed by at least some state officials—Biden would have been at 269 electoral votes. That's one short of the 270 needed to win.

Under the Electoral Count Act as it existed in 2020, it's genuinely unclear what would have happened. The law was notoriously ambiguous about how to handle competing slates.

The Vulnerability That Remains

The Electoral Count Reform Act passed in December 2022 tightened up some of these ambiguities. It raises the threshold for congressional objections to electoral votes. It clarifies that the Vice President's role is purely ministerial. It specifies that governors have the primary authority to certify electors.

But here's what it doesn't fix: the fundamental gap between state certification and congressional counting.

Here's the timeline that still worries election lawyers:

  • Election Day: November 3, 2028 (hypothetically)
  • State certification deadlines: Varies by state, typically mid-November to early December
  • Safe Harbor deadline: December 17, 2028 (under federal law, if a state certifies by this date, Congress generally must accept those results)
  • Electoral College meets: December 20, 2028
  • Congress counts votes: January 6, 2029

    • The vulnerability window is between state certification and the Safe Harbor deadline. If a state certifies Biden 2.0 as the winner on November 20, but then the state legislature passes a resolution on December 10 declaring the certification "void" due to alleged irregularities, what happens?

    The Electoral Count Reform Act says Congress should defer to the governor's certification. But what if it's a divided government—a Democratic governor and a Republican legislature? What if the legislature claims to have found actual evidence of fraud? What if a state court issues a contradictory ruling at the last minute?

    The reform act narrows these windows but doesn't eliminate them entirely.

    What Made 2020 Different (And Scarier)

    One detail that often gets lost: According to testimony documented in the January 6 Committee's final report, Trump campaign officials coordinated the fake elector scheme across multiple states using similar templates and language.

    This wasn't seven independent groups of confused partisans. This was a coordinated strategy developed at the campaign level and executed across swing states simultaneously.

    The report documents that campaign lawyers prepared the certificates, arranged the meetings, and in some cases explicitly told the fake electors that they would become the "real" electors once pending legal challenges succeeded.

    Some participants balked when they learned the legal challenges had already been rejected. According to charging documents from Arizona, at least one fake elector asked whether signing the certificate was legal, and was assured by Trump campaign officials that it was part of a legitimate legal strategy.

    The sophistication is what matters here. This wasn't a clumsy amateur operation. It was coordinated, it had legal cover (however flimsy), and it came remarkably close to creating the conditions needed for success.

    The Enforcement Question

    As of early 2026, criminal cases are proceeding in several states. Michigan, Arizona, and Georgia have all brought charges against fake electors or the attorneys who organized the scheme.

    But here's the pattern that concerns legal observers: Most defendants are arguing they believed they were participating in a legitimate legal contingency plan. They were told by attorneys that this was proper procedure. They thought they were preserving options while court cases proceeded.

    This creates what legal scholars call a "good faith" defense problem. It's hard to prove criminal intent when defendants can claim they relied on advice from attorneys (who themselves claimed a good-faith legal theory, however strained).

    According to analysis by Brookings Institution legal scholars, this might result in relatively few convictions, especially of the individual electors as opposed to the organizers.

    The implication: The personal cost of participating in such a scheme might remain low enough that recruiting future fake electors wouldn't be difficult.

    How This Could Work Better Next Time

    Let me be clear about what I mean by "work better"—I mean from the perspective of someone attempting election subversion. Understanding the failure points helps us prevent success.

    The 2020 scheme had three critical weaknesses:

    Weakness 1: No state officials provided cover. Solution: Elect secretaries of state or governors willing to create ambiguity about results. Several 2022 election-denying candidates came very close to winning secretary of state races.

    Weakness 2: The fake certificates were obviously fake. Solution: Get at least one state official to sign or endorse them, or create a last-minute "audit" that provides quasi-official justification for disputing results.

    Weakness 3: The plan was exposed too early. Solution: Keep the coordination quieter. Use state party infrastructure instead of campaign officials. Create more plausible legal theories in advance.

    None of these weaknesses are insurmountable from a subversion perspective. That's what makes this more than historical curiosity.

    The Parallel Vote Tabulation Model

    Here's a constructive analogy: In countries with less mature democracies, international observers often use something called Parallel Vote Tabulation (PVT).

    Independent observers watch the vote counting process and independently tabulate results. If the official count diverges significantly from the PVT results, it's evidence of manipulation. According to research by the National Democratic Institute, PVT has successfully exposed fraud attempts in dozens of countries.

    The fake elector scheme is like an inverse PVT. Instead of independent observers validating official counts, you create fake official counts to muddy the waters around legitimate results.

    The defense is the same: radical transparency in the counting and certification process, with enough independent observers that creating confusion becomes difficult.

    Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Protection

    The fake elector scheme failed in 2020 because state officials held the line. That's the good news.

    The bad news is that the scheme's logic was sound given certain assumptions. If you can create genuine ambiguity about election results—through friendly state officials, last-minute court rulings, or legislative interventions—then competing elector slates become a real problem that existing law doesn't fully resolve.

    For anyone working on democracy protection projects, this suggests three priorities:

    First, state official recruitment matters enormously. The 2026 races for secretary of state and state attorney general in swing states are absolutely critical. These offices provide the official imprimatur that can turn a fake elector scheme from obvious fraud into genuine constitutional crisis.

    Second, certification timeline compression is worth pursuing. The gap between Election Day and the Electoral College meeting creates the opportunity window. States that have shortened their certification timelines make last-minute manipulation harder.

    Third, transparent observer networks are protective. Organizations like VoteShield that build transparent, publicly auditable records of election administration make it harder to create convincing alternative narratives.

    The fake elector scheme isn't just history. It's a proof of concept. The fact that it failed doesn't mean similar attempts won't succeed—it means we know exactly what vulnerabilities to protect heading into 2028.

    Project idea: If I were starting an organization today, I'd focus on building comprehensive, real-time monitoring of state election certification processes in swing states, with clear alerts when officials deviate from standard procedures. Think of it as an early warning system—if Georgia's Secretary of State suddenly announces an unusual audit three days before the Safe Harbor deadline, every election lawyer in the country needs to know within an hour.

    The 2020 scheme failed partly because journalists and election lawyers could track and expose the coordination in real-time. Making that exposure faster and more systematic would be valuable insurance for 2028.
