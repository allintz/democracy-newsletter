How Other Democracies Fire Bad Election Officials (And Why We Can't)

Epistemic status: High confidence on factual details about institutional structures; medium confidence on comparative assessments of effectiveness, as formal structures don't always predict real-world outcomes.

Let me start with a scenario that keeps democracy nerds up at night.

It's November 2028. A county election director in a swing state refuses to certify results, citing vague "irregularities." Legal challenges begin. Weeks pass. The state can't compel certification quickly enough. The electoral college deadline approaches.

Here's the uncomfortable question: Can anyone actually fire this person? And if so, how fast?

The answer varies wildly depending on where you live. And when you look at how other democracies handle this problem, you realize the US has designed a system that makes it remarkably difficult to remove bad actors during the critical window when it matters most.

The German Solution: Professional Civil Service

Germany learned some hard lessons about democratic fragility in the 1930s. Their response was to professionalize election administration almost completely.

German election officials are career civil servants, according to Germany's Federal Returning Officer documentation. They're not elected. They're not political appointments that change with each administration. They're hired through competitive examinations, receive specialized training, and have strong job protections.

But here's the key difference: they can be removed for misconduct through an administrative process that doesn't require lengthy court battles.

If a German election official refuses to perform their legal duties, their supervisor can suspend them immediately and initiate removal proceedings. The process is designed to be fast—typically resolved within weeks, not months. There's still due process, but it's streamlined specifically because election administration requires reliability.

The trade-off? Less local democratic control over who runs elections. The benefit? No one can weaponize election official selection to undermine elections.

Mexico's Independent Electoral Institute

Mexico took a different approach after its own history of election manipulation by the dominant PRI party.

Following widespread fraud in the 1988 presidential election, Mexico created the Federal Electoral Institute (IFE, now INE) as a fully independent constitutional body, as detailed in research from the International Institute for Democracy and Electoral Assistance. The INE isn't part of any government ministry. It reports to no president or parliament.

The nine-member council is selected through a complex process involving all major parties, civil society input, and the legislature. Members serve a single nine-year term—they can't be reappointed, which reduces incentives for political loyalty. And crucially, they can only be removed through impeachment proceedings for serious misconduct.

But here's what makes this system work: the INE has clear, fast authority to remove local election officials who refuse to follow procedures. When a local official in the state of Baja California attempted to delay certification in 2019, according to reporting from the Wilson Center, the INE removed them within 72 hours and appointed a replacement.

The Mexican model explicitly rejects the US assumption that more local control equals more democracy. Instead, it treats election administration as requiring protection from local political pressures.

Australia: Compulsory Voting and Federal Authority

Australia offers another interesting contrast. Elections are run by the Australian Electoral Commission (AEC), a federal agency established under the Commonwealth Electoral Act 1918.

The AEC Commissioner is appointed by the Governor-General (essentially on advice of the government) but has statutory independence in how elections are conducted. According to the Australian Electoral Commission's governance framework, the Commissioner serves a statutory term and can only be removed by both houses of Parliament for misbehavior or incapacity—a high bar.

But local electoral officials—the equivalent of US county election directors—are federal employees. They're part of a professional service. They receive standardized training. They follow uniform national procedures. And they can be reassigned or terminated by the AEC for failing to perform their duties, through standard employment processes that take days or weeks, not months.

The result? In Australia's modern history, there are essentially no cases of local officials refusing to certify results. It's simply not possible in the same way, because the officials don't have the kind of local political identity and protection that US county officials have.

The US Problem: Slow Removal, Fast Deadlines

Now contrast this with the US system.

County election officials in most states are either elected (making removal nearly impossible before their term ends) or appointed by county boards (making removal dependent on local political dynamics). As I've covered in previous newsletters, many states lack clear legal mechanisms to force certification when officials refuse.

But even when removal is theoretically possible, it's agonizingly slow.

Take the example of Otero County, New Mexico in 2022. When the county commission refused to certify the primary election results, citing conspiracy theories about voting machines, New Mexico's Secretary of State had to file a lawsuit. The state supreme court ruled quickly—within days—that certification was mandatory.

But here's what took time: actually enforcing that order. The commission initially indicated they might still refuse. It took the personal intervention of county commissioners, significant public pressure, and the looming threat of criminal charges before they complied. The whole process consumed over two weeks during a primary election—a lower-stakes situation than a presidential general election.

In a general election for president, with 35 days between Election Day and the Electoral College deadline? Two weeks is an eternity. And if officials are willing to defy court orders, the escalation options—contempt citations, criminal charges, forced removal—all take additional time.

Why This Matters for 2028

The comparative analysis reveals something uncomfortable: most other democracies have solved this problem by reducing local control over election administration.

They've made a calculation that the risks of local officials subverting elections outweigh the democratic benefits of local election official selection. They've prioritized reliable, professional administration over local political accountability for election officials.

The US has made the opposite choice, largely by accident. We didn't design our system with these trade-offs in mind. We inherited highly decentralized election administration from an era when elections were genuinely local affairs, and the idea of coordinated national subversion attempts wasn't really on anyone's radar.

But now we're stuck with a system where:

  • Bad actors have strong job protections (if elected) or depend on local political networks (if appointed)
  • Removal processes are slow relative to election deadlines
  • There's no fast, professional alternative authority that can take over
  • Each of the roughly 10,000 election jurisdictions is a potential chokepoint

    • According to analysis from the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project, as of 2023, 28 states have no clear statutory authority for rapid removal and replacement of election officials who refuse to perform mandatory duties during an election cycle. The gaps in the law assume officials will comply with court orders—an assumption that has been repeatedly tested since 2020.

    What Could Actually Work?

    Looking at international examples, there are a few possible reforms:

    Option 1: Emergency appointment authority. Give governors or secretaries of state statutory power to immediately suspend and replace election officials who refuse to certify, with judicial review after the fact rather than before. This is similar to the Mexican model. Several states, including Michigan and Nevada, have moved toward versions of this since 2020, though the laws are largely untested.

    Option 2: Professional election service. Move toward the German model—make election administration a specialized civil service career, with robust job protections and clear, fast removal procedures for misconduct. This would require massive restructuring and is probably politically impossible in the short term.

    Option 3: Automatic backstops. If certification doesn't happen by a deadline, it happens automatically, perhaps with a state-level official attesting to the county results. This is the approach some states have recently adopted, though it creates its own vulnerabilities around what counts as "results" in disputed situations.

    Option 4: Parallel authority. Create a backup certification mechanism—perhaps a panel of retired judges or a bipartisan state board—that can certify results if the regular process fails. This adds complexity but provides redundancy.

    None of these are perfect. All involve trade-offs between speed, due process, local control, and protection against abuse.

    The Bottom Line

    Other democracies have largely solved the "bad election official" problem by making election administration more professional and less local. They've accepted that this means less democratic control over who runs elections, in exchange for more reliable elections.

    The US has been unwilling or unable to make that trade-off. We're still operating on a model where election administration is fundamentally local, and removal of bad actors is slow by design.

    This worked fine when we could assume good faith. It's a glaring vulnerability when we can't.

    For anyone thinking about projects in this space: There's urgent work to be done advocating for state-level legislation that creates fast removal and replacement mechanisms for election officials who refuse to perform mandatory duties. This needs to happen in 2026 and 2027, before the 2028 election cycle, and it needs to happen in swing states where the risk is highest.

    Organizations like the National Association of State Election Directors are working on model legislation, but they need policy support, legal analysis, and state-level advocacy capacity. The window for getting these laws on the books before they're needed is closing.

    The international comparison makes clear: democracies that want to protect election integrity eventually have to choose between unlimited local control and reliable, professional administration. The US has been trying to have both. That's getting harder to sustain.
