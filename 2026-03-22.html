The Common Cause Dilemma: When Your Democracy Defender Can't Actually Defend Democracy

Epistemic status: High confidence on organizational facts and legal constraints; medium confidence on strategic assessments of organizational effectiveness gaps.

When you think about organizations protecting American democracy, Common Cause probably comes to mind. Founded in 1970 by John Gardner (former Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare), it's one of the oldest and most recognizable democracy reform groups in the country. According to their 2023 IRS Form 990, they operate with a $17 million annual budget and maintain chapters in 35 states.

But here's the uncomfortable question: What happens when your democracy protection organization is legally prohibited from protecting democracy in the ways that matter most right now?

The 501(c)(3) Handcuffs

Common Cause operates as a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This gives them significant advantages—donations are tax-deductible, they can receive foundation grants, and they maintain credibility as a nonpartisan civic organization.

But it also creates a fundamental constraint. As the IRS explicitly states, 501(c)(3) organizations are "absolutely prohibited from directly or indirectly participating in, or intervening in, any political campaign on behalf of (or in opposition to) any candidate for elective public office."

This isn't just about presidential races. It applies to every election—including the 2026 secretary of state races and state supreme court seats that will directly determine how the 2028 election is administered and disputed.

Let me be concrete about what this means. In the Nevada secretary of state race in 2022, an election denier who had refused to certify local results ran against a candidate committed to standard election administration. Common Cause could educate voters about what secretaries of state do. They could not tell voters which candidate posed a threat to election integrity.

What They Can Do (And Do Well)

To be clear, Common Cause does important work within their constraints. According to their 2024 impact report, they focus on three main areas:

Voting rights litigation and advocacy. They've been involved in major cases including challenges to partisan gerrymandering and voter ID laws. In 2019, they were lead plaintiffs in Rucho v. Common Cause, which reached the Supreme Court on partisan gerrymandering (they lost 5-4, with the Court ruling that federal courts cannot review partisan gerrymandering claims).

Democracy reform legislation. They advocate for the For the People Act, automatic voter registration, same-day registration, and other structural reforms. At the state level, they've supported legislation establishing independent redistricting commissions in multiple states.

Voter education and mobilization. They run campaigns to inform voters about registration deadlines, early voting options, and their rights at the polls. During 2024, according to their impact report, they reached 4.3 million voters through education programs.

This is all valuable. The problem is what's missing.

The Giant Hole in the Middle

Here's what Common Cause cannot do because of their tax status:

They cannot campaign against election deniers running for secretary of state, attorney general, or governor in swing states—positions with direct authority over election certification and voter access.

They cannot oppose state legislative candidates who have indicated they would refuse to certify election results or would support overturning results through legislative intervention.

They cannot support candidates running to replace those officials, even when the threat is documented and explicit.

This creates a bizarre situation. Common Cause can document that a current county election official refused to certify results in 2022. They can publish research showing this is a threat to democracy. They can advocate for laws to prevent certification refusal.

But when that same official runs for secretary of state with the explicit platform of "taking back our elections," Common Cause must remain silent on whether voters should elect them.

The 501(c)(4) Alternative

Some organizations have solved this by creating related 501(c)(4) entities. These "social welfare organizations" can engage in unlimited lobbying and can participate in political campaigns as long as it's not their primary activity.

According to analysis by the Brennan Center, 501(c)(4)s offer significantly more flexibility for democracy protection work—they can oppose specific candidates who pose threats to election integrity.

The tradeoff? Donations aren't tax-deductible, foundation funding is more limited, and they must report their donors to the IRS (though not publicly in most cases).

Common Cause does operate a small 501(c)(4) arm called Common Cause Action Fund. But it's tiny relative to their main organization. Their 2023 Form 990 for the Action Fund shows just $2.3 million in revenue compared to the main organization's $17 million.

Why so small? According to sources familiar with their operations, the organization has historically prioritized maintaining their nonpartisan credibility and maximizing tax-deductible donations over building aggressive campaign capacity.

The Real-World Impact

Let's look at what this meant in a specific case. In Georgia's 2022 secretary of state race, incumbent Brad Raffensperger (who had resisted Trump's pressure in 2020) faced a primary challenge from Rep. Jody Hice, who had voted to overturn the 2020 election results.

According to reporting by the Atlanta Journal-Constitution, this was explicitly about election administration. Hice's campaign centered on claims that Raffensperger had allowed fraud. Trump endorsed Hice specifically because of Raffensperger's 2020 certification.

Common Cause could educate Georgia voters about the secretary of state role. They could not say "Raffensperger protected election integrity in 2020, and Hice poses a direct threat to the 2024 election"—even though this was objectively documented.

Raffensperger won his primary anyway. But that's not the point. The structural constraint remains for every secretary of state, attorney general, and governor race that will determine how 2028 elections are administered and certified.

The Broader Ecosystem Gap

This isn't just a Common Cause problem. According to a 2023 analysis by the Center for Effective Philanthropy, most established democracy organizations operate as 501(c)(3)s with small or nonexistent 501(c)(4) arms.

The organizations that can engage directly in these races—state-level political action committees, party organizations, and issue-focused 501(c)(4)s—often lack the deep expertise in election administration that groups like Common Cause have built over decades.

This creates a coordination failure. The people who best understand election subversion threats can't engage in the most direct way to prevent them. The people who can engage directly often lack the nuanced understanding of how certification processes work or what specific powers different offices hold.

Why This Matters for 2028

Let's be concrete about the timeline. Secretary of state elections in Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, and Wisconsin will occur in 2026. The winners will serve during the 2028 presidential election.

According to States United Democracy Center tracking, in the 2022 cycle, election deniers ran for secretary of state in all five of these states. Three won their primaries before losing in the general election.

The 2026 races will determine who controls election procedures, certification authority, and legal defense of election results in the closest swing states. These are not background administrative positions—they are frontline democracy protection roles.

Common Cause will be able to educate voters about what secretaries of state do. They won't be able to campaign against candidates who have documented histories of attempting to subvert elections.

What Actually Works?

Some organizations have structured themselves differently from the start. All Voting is Local, for instance, operates primarily as a 501(c)(4) with a smaller 501(c)(3) arm. This allows them to engage directly in state-level races while maintaining some capacity for non-political education and litigation.

According to their 2023 strategic plan, this structure lets them combine research on voter suppression with direct advocacy for or against specific officeholders. They've endorsed and opposed candidates in Arizona, Georgia, and Florida based on their records on election administration.

The tradeoff is a smaller budget ($3.2 million compared to Common Cause's $17 million) and less foundation support. But it provides flexibility to address threats that 501(c)(3) constraints prohibit.

The Uncomfortable Questions

This raises difficult strategic questions for the democracy protection field:

Should established organizations restructure? Common Cause could shift resources to their 501(c)(4) arm, but they'd likely lose foundation grants and tax-deductible donations that fund their current work.

Should new organizations fill the gap? Specialized 501(c)(4)s focused on swing state secretary of state and attorney general races could engage directly. But they'd be starting from scratch on relationships and expertise.

Should donors demand different structures? Major funders could make grants contingent on organizations building robust 501(c)(4) capacity. But this might compromise work that requires 501(c)(3) status.

There's no clean answer. But the status quo creates a situation where organizations with the deepest expertise on election administration are legally prohibited from the most direct interventions.

Bottom Line: What This Means for You

If you're thinking about where to direct resources or effort in democracy protection, understand that organizational structure determines capability.

Common Cause does essential work on voting rights litigation, redistricting reform, and voter education. But they cannot engage in the 2026 secretary of state races that will directly determine how 2028 election disputes are handled. That's not a criticism of their choices—it's a constraint of their tax status.

This creates three action items:

For donors: Ask democracy organizations what their 501(c)(3) vs. 501(c)(4) split is and why. Organizations that are 100% 501(c)(3) have fundamental limitations for swing state race engagement.

For researchers and advocates: If you want to influence who administers elections in swing states, you need to work with or build 501(c)(4) entities. 501(c)(3) work is necessary but not sufficient.

For existing organizations: Common Cause and similar groups should transparently communicate what they can and cannot do given their structure. Donors and the public often don't understand these constraints.

The broader pattern matters. Democracy protection has been treated primarily as a litigation and education problem—work well-suited to 501(c)(3) organizations. But preventing election subversion increasingly requires direct engagement in races for secretary of state, attorney general, and state supreme court. That requires different organizational structures.

We're 32 months from the 2028 presidential election. The officials who will administer and certify it are being selected right now, mostly in low-turnout 2026 primaries. The organizations best positioned to educate voters about these races are often legally prohibited from doing so.

That's not a funding gap. It's a structural mismatch between threat and organizational capacity.
