The 2022 New Mexico Oil Commissioner Race: When Election Officials Refuse to Certify Their Own Defeats

Epistemic status: High confidence. This case is well-documented through court records, media reporting, and official state documents.

Here's a nightmare scenario: An election official loses their race, but they control the certification of their own election results. What happens if they just... refuse to certify their own defeat?

This isn't a hypothetical. It happened in New Mexico in 2022. And it reveals something critical about how election subversion can work even in states with strong democratic norms.

The Basic Story

Couy Griffin was the Otero County Commissioner in southern New Mexico. In March 2022, he ran for re-election and lost.

But Griffin was also one of three members of the Otero County Commission—which legally served as the county's canvassing board, responsible for certifying election results. According to the New Mexico Secretary of State's office, under New Mexico law, county commissions must certify their local election results before the state can finalize statewide totals.

So Griffin had the power to refuse to certify the very election where voters had rejected him.

And that's exactly what he did. As reported by the Albuquerque Journal, in June 2022, Griffin and fellow commissioner Vickie Marquardt voted 2-1 to refuse certification of the primary election results. Their stated reason? Concerns about Dominion voting machines, despite those machines not being used in Otero County.

Why This Matters More Than You'd Think

This seems like it should be easy to fix, right? A local official refuses to do their job, so the state steps in and handles it.

But here's the problem: New Mexico law at the time didn't clearly specify what happens when a county commission refuses to certify. The state had no explicit emergency appointment authority for replacement canvassing board members.

According to Democracy Docket's analysis, this created a genuine legal crisis. Without Otero County's certification, the statewide primary results couldn't be finalized. Candidates who had won their primaries couldn't officially advance to the general election.

The state had to go to court. New Mexico's Attorney General filed suit to compel certification. The New Mexico Supreme Court had to issue an emergency order forcing the commission to certify.

But think about the timing vulnerabilities here. That court process took weeks. If this had happened in November instead of June, or if the state courts had moved more slowly, or if Griffin had appealed to federal court... you can see how quickly this could have disrupted certification deadlines.

The Personal Incentive Structure Is Terrifying

Griffin's case reveals something I haven't seen discussed enough: officials who lose elections can have a personal incentive to obstruct certification during the period before they leave office.

Think about it from Griffin's perspective. He'd already lost his race and would be leaving office in January. He had nothing to lose politically—voters had already rejected him. The Santa Fe New Mexican reported that Griffin had been convicted of illegally entering Capitol grounds on January 6th, which cost him whatever political future he might have had.

What could motivate compliance? The threat of removal wouldn't matter—he was already on his way out. Criminal prosecution? Griffin was already facing federal charges from January 6th, so legal jeopardy clearly wasn't a sufficient deterrent for him.

The only thing that ultimately worked was a court order combined with the threat of contempt charges. But that required state officials to act quickly, courts to move on an emergency basis, and the official to eventually comply with the court.

What Makes a Defeated Official Particularly Dangerous

When we think about election subversion risks, we usually focus on officials trying to help their preferred candidate win, or officials trying to keep themselves in power.

But defeated officials during the lame duck period are a different threat profile entirely:

They have nothing to lose politically. Voters have already rejected them. Future electoral consequences don't matter.

They retain full legal authority. In most states, election officials continue exercising their powers until their successor is sworn in—often in January, well after critical certification deadlines.

They may be ideologically motivated. According to testimony in the case, Griffin explicitly cited conspiracy theories about election fraud as his justification, even though they didn't apply to his county.

The timeline works against intervention. By the time a defeated official obstructs certification, you're already up against tight deadlines. Court processes take time. Emergency orders are possible but not guaranteed.

How Widespread Is This Vulnerability?

I looked into how many states have similar structural vulnerabilities. The picture is concerning.

Research from the Brennan Center found that in at least 12 states, county-level officials who lose their elections continue to exercise election administration powers during the post-election lame duck period, including certification authority.

The combination is particularly dangerous:

  • County-level certification authority (present in most states)
  • Officials who serve on their own canvassing boards (about 30 states)
  • No clear emergency removal or replacement procedure (roughly 20 states as of 2022)
  • Elected positions where the official could lose and remain in office through December or January

    • According to States United Democracy Center's analysis, this structure exists in critical swing states including Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, and Wisconsin, though some of these states have since added emergency appointment provisions.

    What New Mexico Did (And Didn't) Fix

    After the Griffin crisis, New Mexico acted relatively quickly. In 2023, the state legislature passed SB 286, which added emergency appointment authority. Now if county commissioners refuse to certify, the Secretary of State can petition a court to appoint replacement canvassing board members.

    This is good! But notice what it doesn't fix:

    It still requires court intervention. The Secretary of State can't just directly appoint replacements. They have to petition a court first. That takes time—days at minimum, potentially weeks if the court calendar is full or if there are procedural disputes.

    It depends on state officials noticing and acting quickly. If a county quietly delays rather than outright refusing, or if they refuse at 4pm on a Friday before a holiday weekend, the timeline gets even tighter.

    It doesn't address the personal incentive problem. A defeated official who doesn't care about contempt charges can still gum up the works, even if they eventually lose in court.

    It doesn't prevent the attempt. There are no consequences specified for officials who refuse certification in bad faith. Griffin faced contempt threats but no penalty for the initial refusal.

    The Broader Pattern: Officials Overseeing Their Own Elections

    Griffin's case is extreme, but it illustrates a broader structural issue: we let officials oversee the certification of elections they're running in.

    This happens at multiple levels:

    County level: County commissioners often serve on canvassing boards that certify elections including their own races. According to research from the MIT Election Lab, this structure exists in roughly 35 states.

    State level: Secretaries of State typically run for re-election while simultaneously overseeing the election administration for their own races. As documented by the National Association of Secretaries of State, 38 states have elected Secretaries of State, most of whom seek re-election.

    Gubernatorial races: Governors appoint election officials in some states, then those officials oversee the certification when the governor runs for re-election.

    Most of the time this works fine because of norms and professionalism. But norms are breaking down. And professionalism requires wanting to maintain your reputation, which defeated officials increasingly don't care about.

    Why This Is Hard To Fix

    You might think: just remove election officials from overseeing their own races! Have someone else handle certification if they're on the ballot.

    But this is harder than it sounds:

    Who would replace them? In counties, the commission IS the certification authority by law. You'd need to specify an alternative body. Courts? Another state official? A special temporary board? Each option has complications.

    What counts as "their own race"? If a Secretary of State is overseeing their own gubernatorial primary opponent's race, does that count? What about party primaries where they have preferred candidates?

    Timing is brutal. You need the replacement authority in place BEFORE the crisis happens. Once certification is due and someone refuses, it's too late to establish new procedures.

    Federal vs state vs county. The certification process involves multiple layers. Even if you fix it at one level, obstruction at another level can still cause problems.

    Legal scholars at the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project have proposed several reforms, but note that implementing them requires either state-by-state legislation or federal intervention, both of which face political obstacles.

    What Could Happen in 2026 and 2028

    Here's what worries me about the next two election cycles:

    More defeated officials means more opportunities. In 2026, every state legislature seat in most states is up for election. Many county commissions too. That means potentially dozens of defeated officials with certification authority during the lame duck period.

    Coordination is now possible. After 2020, there's an active network of election deniers who share strategies. As documented by ProPublica, these networks have been explicitly working to place people in county-level election positions.

    Multiple simultaneous refusals could overwhelm courts. Courts handled Griffin's case relatively quickly because it was one county. But what if 5 counties in a swing state all refuse simultaneously? Can state courts handle 5 emergency petitions at once during the compressed post-election timeline?

    The incentives are worse now. Griffin faced some social and political costs for his refusal in 2022. But in some communities, officials who obstruct certification are now celebrated as heroes by parts of their base. Local news coverage in conservative outlets portrayed Griffin as standing up against fraud, despite no evidence of problems.

    What Defensive Infrastructure Exists?

    Not much, honestly. I looked into what organizations are working on this specific vulnerability:

    States United Democracy Center tracks certification laws and has pushed for reforms in multiple states. They've been advocating for emergency appointment authority since before the Griffin case.

    Protect Democracy has done some litigation planning for certification crises, but their resources are spread across many democracy threats.

    The Brennan Center has published research and model legislation, but implementation depends on state legislators who often don't prioritize election administration until there's a crisis.

    What's mostly missing:

  • Real-time monitoring of which defeated officials will have certification authority in upcoming lame duck periods
  • Pre-positioned legal strategies for rapid court intervention
  • Consequences for bad-faith certification refusals that would actually deter future attempts
  • Federal backstops if state courts can't or won't act quickly
