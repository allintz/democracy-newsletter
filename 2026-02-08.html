Why Election Subversion Focused on State Legislatures: Understanding the "Independent State Legislature" Theory

Epistemic status: High confidence on legal history and documented events; medium confidence on future implications given ongoing legal uncertainty.

If you've been following election subversion attempts, you might have noticed something curious: many of the pressure campaigns in 2020 didn't target governors or election officials. They targeted state legislatures.

Trump's team called Republican legislative leaders in swing states. They organized hearings where Rudy Giuliani presented fraud allegations. In Pennsylvania, Republican legislators introduced a resolution to dispute the election results. In Arizona, lawmakers held their own "election integrity" hearings.

Why? It wasn't random. It was based on a specific constitutional theory about who actually controls federal elections.

The Theory: State Legislatures as Supreme

The U.S. Constitution's Elections Clause (Article I, Section 4) says: "The Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives, shall be prescribed in each State by the Legislature thereof."

The Presidential Electors Clause (Article II, Section 1) says each state shall appoint electors "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct."

Here's the question: Does "Legislature" mean just the legislative body? Or does it mean the state's lawmaking process, which includes the governor's veto, state constitutional constraints, and state court review?

The "Independent State Legislature" (ISL) theory argues the first interpretation. It claims state legislatures have special, nearly unchecked power over federal elections. Governors can't veto their election laws. State constitutions can't restrict them. State courts can't review their actions.

This sounds like an obscure legal debate. But it has massive practical implications.

Why This Matters for Election Subversion

If state legislatures have this special power, they could potentially:

1. Override election results directly. If a legislature believes (or claims to believe) an election was fraudulent or mismanaged, could it simply appoint different electors? The ISL theory suggests maybe yes.

2. Ignore governors and state courts. In 2020, several states had Democratic governors or state supreme courts that blocked legislature actions. Under ISL theory, those checks might not apply to federal elections.

3. Change rules right before or after elections. Normal lawmaking requires executive approval and constitutional compliance. ISL theory could allow legislatures to act unilaterally.

This is why Trump's legal team focused on state legislatures in their post-election strategy. According to documents from the January 6th Committee, the plan explicitly relied on getting state legislators to intervene.

The Historical Context

This isn't a new theory. It has roots going back decades, but it gained prominence in Bush v. Gore (2000).

In that case, three Supreme Court justices wrote a concurrence suggesting Florida's legislature had special authority under Article II. Chief Justice Rehnquist argued that Florida's Supreme Court had improperly changed election rules that the legislature had set, potentially violating the legislature's constitutional prerogative.

This was just a concurrence, not binding law. But it planted seeds.

The theory re-emerged in 2015, when Justice Thomas wrote that state courts might lack authority to override legislatures on federal election matters. Then in 2020, as courts evaluated pandemic-era voting changes, several justices signaled sympathy for ISL ideas.

The 2020 Test Case: Pennsylvania

Pennsylvania provides the clearest example of how this played out.

Before the 2020 election, Pennsylvania's Supreme Court extended the mail ballot deadline by three days due to postal delays. The court cited the state constitution's free and equal elections clause.

Republicans argued this violated the ISL theory. The state legislature had set the deadline, they said, and state courts couldn't override that for a federal election.

The U.S. Supreme Court didn't rule on the merits. But four justices indicated they wanted to hear the case, just one vote short of the majority needed to grant review. Justice Alito wrote that the issue "calls out for review."

In the end, Biden's margin in Pennsylvania was large enough (80,000+ votes) that the late-arriving ballots didn't matter. But in a closer election, this could have been decisive.

What Happened in Moore v. Harper (2023)

The theory finally got its full Supreme Court hearing in Moore v. Harper, a case about North Carolina's congressional maps.

North Carolina's legislature drew heavily gerrymandered districts. The state Supreme Court struck them down under the state constitution. Republicans argued the ISL theory: state courts couldn't review the legislature's federal election maps.

In June 2023, the Supreme Court rejected the strongest version of ISL theory, ruling 6-3 that state courts can review legislative election laws. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that state constitutions do constrain state legislatures, even for federal elections.

But—and this is crucial—the Court didn't fully kill the theory. Roberts wrote that state courts must act within "ordinary judicial review" and can't "exceed the bounds of ordinary judicial review to the point of usurping the legislature's role."

What does that mean? Nobody really knows yet. It's vague enough to invite future litigation.

The Ongoing Vulnerability

Here's why this matters going into 2028:

The pathway still exists, it's just narrower. A state legislature couldn't completely ignore state courts. But it could argue that a particular court ruling "exceeded ordinary judicial review" and should be overridden.

It's been normalized as a legitimate legal argument. In 2020, this was a fringe theory. Now it's been extensively briefed and argued at the Supreme Court. Future attempts would cite Moore v. Harper's language about "ordinary judicial review."

Three justices still support a stronger version. Justices Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch dissented in Moore v. Harper, supporting broader legislative power. In a future case with different facts, the vote could shift.

The theory provides cover for legislative intervention. Even if courts ultimately reject it, the theory gives legislators a constitutional-sounding justification for overriding election results during the critical certification period.

How This Could Play Out in 2028

Imagine this scenario:

  • The 2028 presidential election in Wisconsin is decided by 8,000 votes.
  • Republicans control the Wisconsin legislature. A Democrat is governor.
  • Conservative groups file lawsuits claiming election irregularities in Milwaukee.
  • The Wisconsin Supreme Court (assume it has a liberal majority) orders some ballots excluded or recounted.
  • Republican legislators argue the court "exceeded ordinary judicial review" under Moore v. Harper.
  • They pass a resolution declaring they will appoint their own electors to "preserve the legislature's constitutional prerogative."
  • This creates competing slates of electors.

    • Even if this ultimately fails in federal court, it creates chaos during the December certification period. And with three sympathetic Supreme Court justices, it might not fail.

    The Counter-Arguments

    To be fair, there are strong reasons to think this wouldn't work:

    Moore v. Harper established that state courts have review power. A legislature couldn't just ignore a state court ruling entirely.

    The Electoral Count Reform Act (2022) made it harder to object to electors. It would require one-fifth of both houses to force a vote on an objection, not just one member from each.

    Federal courts would likely intervene. The Purcell principle (courts shouldn't change election rules close to Election Day) cuts both ways—it could also stop last-minute legislative interventions.

    Public backlash would be severe. Openly overriding voters would be politically costly.

    But here's the thing: in 2020, several of these safeguards existed, and subversion was still attempted. It failed due to individuals—like Georgia's Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger—refusing to go along, not because the structural protections were ironclad.

    Bottom Line for Democracy Defenders

    The Independent State Legislature theory gives state legislatures a constitutional-sounding argument for intervening in federal elections. While the Supreme Court rejected the strongest version in Moore v. Harper, it left enough ambiguity for future attempts.

    This matters because:

  • State legislature composition in swing states (Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Michigan, Arizona, Georgia, North Carolina) will be crucial in 2026 midterms
  • These legislatures could face pressure to intervene if 2028 is close
  • The legal theory is now "normalized" through Supreme Court briefing
  • The vague "ordinary judicial review" standard invites manipulation

    • For project ideas: State-level organizations working on fair redistricting and legislative elections are strategically important, because they determine who controls these powerful bodies. Groups like All Voting is Local work on protecting voter access at the state legislative level, while the National Democratic Redistricting Committee focuses on state legislature composition through redistricting.

    The key insight: election subversion doesn't require a sudden coup. It requires legal-sounding theories that give officials permission to override voters. The ISL theory is one of those theories, and it's still alive.
