When Courts Won't Enforce Their Own Orders: The Alabama Redistricting Case and Judicial Non-Compliance

Epistemic status: High confidence on the facts and timeline of the Alabama redistricting case, which are extensively documented in court records. Moderate confidence on broader patterns of non-compliance, which depend on how you categorize different types of resistance to court orders.

You probably think that when the Supreme Court issues a ruling, states have to follow it. That's technically true—but what happens when they just... don't?

Let me tell you about Alabama's redistricting saga, because it reveals something deeply unsettling about how judicial enforcement actually works in America. And it has direct implications for what might happen if courts need to intervene in election disputes in 2028.

The Basic Story

In June 2023, the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in Allen v. Milligan that Alabama's congressional map likely violated the Voting Rights Act by diluting Black voting power. Alabama has seven congressional districts. Black residents make up 27% of the state's population. But the map only had one majority-Black district—about 14% representation.

The Court said Alabama probably needed to draw a second majority-Black district. This wasn't a close call—Chief Justice Roberts joined the liberal justices in the majority.

So Alabama redrew its maps, right?

No. Alabama's Republican-controlled legislature passed a new map with still just one majority-Black district in July 2023—barely a month after the Supreme Court ruling.

What Happened Next

The plaintiffs went back to the three-judge federal district court panel that had originally heard the case. In September 2023, that court unanimously rejected Alabama's new map and ordered the state to draw a map with two majority-Black districts.

Alabama appealed to the Supreme Court again, asking them to stay the lower court's order. In October 2023, the Supreme Court refused to intervene, effectively telling Alabama: draw the second district.

Alabama finally complied in October 2023—but only after four months of defiance following the initial Supreme Court ruling, and only after losing at every single level of appeal.

Why This Matters

Here's what should worry you: Alabama faced essentially zero consequences for this defiance.

No state officials were held in contempt. No fines were imposed. No one lost their position. The state successfully delayed implementation of a Supreme Court decision for months through simple non-compliance, then grudgingly followed the order only when it became clear no higher court would rescue them.

This wasn't an accident or confusion about what the Court wanted. According to reporting in the Washington Post, Alabama Republicans were quite explicit that they disagreed with the ruling and wanted to test its limits.

The Enforcement Problem

The United States doesn't have a federal police force that goes around enforcing court orders against states. When courts rule against state governments, enforcement relies on a combination of:

  • Contempt proceedings (which take time and don't guarantee compliance)
  • Political pressure (which only works if officials care about their reputation)
  • Eventually, compliance (when it becomes clear the courts won't back down)

    • But notice what's missing: any mechanism for swift enforcement.

    A 2019 paper in the University of Chicago Law Review by Nicholas Stephanopoulos examines this pattern across multiple contexts. He finds that state and local governments increasingly resist court orders they dislike, calculating—often correctly—that the costs of defiance are minimal.

    Historical Context

    This isn't entirely new. Remember Worcester v. Georgia from the 1832 newsletter? (Quick refresher: the Supreme Court ruled that Georgia couldn't impose its laws on Cherokee territory. Georgia ignored the ruling. President Andrew Jackson reportedly said "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." The Cherokee were removed anyway.)

    But Worcester happened in 1832, when federal power over states was genuinely contested and the Court's authority wasn't fully established.

    What's different now is that this is happening in 2023, with a state openly defying a clear Supreme Court ruling in a voting rights case—a core democratic function—and facing minimal consequences.

    The 2028 Connection

    Now imagine it's December 2028. A federal court has ordered a county in a swing state to certify its election results. The county refuses, citing [pick your reason: irregularities, fraud claims, whatever].

    The court issues an order demanding compliance. The county... just doesn't comply.

    What happens?

    Based on the Alabama precedent, here's what we'd expect:

  • The court would need to schedule contempt hearings (takes days or weeks)
  • The county might appeal the order (more delay)
  • Eventually, if all appeals fail, the county might face fines or other sanctions
  • Maybe, after weeks or months, they'd comply

    • But the Electoral College meets on December 17, 2028. If a county can delay certification for even two weeks through simple non-compliance, that might be enough to throw a state's results into chaos.

    Why Courts Struggle With This

    Federal judges have limited tools for dealing with defiant government officials. According to research by Jamal Greene at Columbia Law School, contempt proceedings against state officials are:

  • Slow: They require multiple hearings and findings
  • Limited in scope: Judges can fine officials or theoretically jail them, but these are extraordinary measures rarely used
  • Politically fraught: Federal judges are reluctant to escalate conflicts with state governments

    • Most importantly, courts generally assume compliance. The system isn't built for officials who simply refuse to follow orders.

    What Would Make This Worse

    Several factors could amplify this problem in 2028:

    Coordination across multiple jurisdictions: If officials in three counties in one swing state all refused to certify, and officials in two counties in another swing state did the same, courts would need to pursue parallel contempt proceedings in multiple venues simultaneously.

    Ideological cover: In Alabama, state officials believed they were defending state sovereignty and disagreeing with the Court's interpretation of the Voting Rights Act. In 2028, officials might believe they're preventing fraud or protecting election integrity—giving them a narrative that makes defiance feel principled rather than obstructionist.

    Tight timelines: Alabama's redistricting ultimately got resolved before the 2024 elections. But election certification has hard constitutional deadlines. A county that delays for two weeks might accomplish its goal even if courts ultimately rule against it.

    Sympathetic appellate judges: Alabama kept appealing, hoping to find a friendlier court. In election disputes, officials might calculate that even if they lose initially, they might win on appeal—or at minimum, the appeals process itself creates helpful delay.

    What Research Shows

    A 2022 Stanford Law Review article by Bertrall Ross and Douglas Keith examines judicial enforcement of election laws specifically. They find:

  • Courts are particularly reluctant to intervene aggressively in election administration
  • Judges often cite concerns about "judicializing" political processes
  • Even when courts rule clearly, enforcement often relies on voluntary compliance
  • State officials have learned that defiance carries minimal risk

    • The authors call this "soft non-compliance"—states technically acknowledge court authority while finding ways to minimize or delay implementation.

    International Comparisons

    Other democracies handle this differently. In Germany, if a state (Land) government defies a federal court order, Article 37 of the Basic Law allows the federal government to use "federal execution" to enforce compliance—essentially, direct federal intervention.

    Canada's system of parliamentary sovereignty means that while courts can declare laws unconstitutional, enforcement mechanisms are clearer because the federal government has more direct authority over provincial compliance.

    The US system, with its strong federalism and separation of powers, creates more enforcement gaps.

    What Could Be Done

    Several reforms could address this:

    Automatic penalties: Laws could specify that officials who fail to certify by required deadlines automatically face fines or removal, without requiring separate court proceedings.

    Expedited contempt procedures: Special fast-track contempt proceedings for election-related orders, with hearings required within 24-48 hours.

    Federal enforcement authority: Giving the Justice Department explicit authority to enforce election-related court orders, similar to how they can enforce civil rights orders.

    Backup certification authority: If an official fails to certify by a deadline, authority automatically transfers to a backup official (secretary of state, governor, etc.) without requiring a court order.

    Some states have implemented versions of these. Michigan's 2023 election law reforms, which I mentioned briefly in a previous newsletter, include automatic backup certification if a county board deadlocks. But most states lack such provisions.

    The Deeper Problem

    The Alabama case reveals something troubling about American democratic infrastructure: our system assumes good faith compliance with judicial orders.

    When officials simply refuse to comply—betting that the enforcement mechanisms are too slow or too weak to stop them—the system struggles.

    This isn't just an abstract constitutional law problem. It's a specific, documented vulnerability that was tested in 2023 and found to be exploitable.

    And unlike some of the scenarios we've discussed in previous newsletters, this one doesn't require conspiracy or coordination. A single official who's convinced they're doing the right thing can create a crisis just by refusing to follow a court order.

    Bottom Line for Democracy Advocates

    If you're working on election protection for 2028, here's what the Alabama redistricting saga teaches:

    Don't assume court orders will be swiftly enforced. Plan for the possibility that officials might defy orders and count on appeals and delay. This means:

  • Litigation needs to happen earlier, before hard deadlines approach
  • Multiple parallel enforcement mechanisms (political pressure, public transparency, backup officials) matter more than just winning in court
  • Automatic enforcement provisions in state law are vastly better than relying on courts to compel compliance after the fact

    • Existing project doing this work: The Brennan Center's State Court Initiative is working on judicial independence and enforcement issues at the state level. But as far as I can tell, no major organization is specifically focused on fast-track enforcement mechanisms for election orders.

    Project idea: An "Election Order Enforcement Project" could develop model legislation for automatic enforcement of election-related court orders, create rapid-response legal teams ready to pursue expedited contempt proceedings, and build political pressure campaigns to raise reputational costs for officials who defy court orders. The window for getting such legislation passed in swing states is probably mid-2026 to early 2027—shortly after this year's state legislative elections but well before the 2028 election crunch.

    The Alabama case showed that even the Supreme Court can be successfully defied for months with minimal consequences. That's a vulnerability that won't fix itself.
