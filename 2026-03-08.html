The Nevada Fake Electors Case: What the Criminal Indictments Tell Us About 2020's Coordination

Epistemic status: High confidence. This newsletter draws on publicly available court documents, indictments, and investigative reporting. I acknowledge that some details remain under litigation and may be contested by defendants.

You've probably heard about the "fake electors scheme" from 2020. I've mentioned it in passing before. But I want to zoom in today on one specific case that tells us something important about how coordinated the whole operation actually was—and what that means for thinking about 2028.

The Nevada fake electors case is particularly revealing because, unlike some other states where charges focused on state-level actors, Nevada's indictment laid out the national coordination in unusual detail. Let me walk you through what happened and why it matters.

What Actually Happened in Nevada

On December 14, 2020—the day actual electors were supposed to meet—six Nevada Republicans gathered in Carson City and signed documents claiming to be the state's "duly elected and qualified" electors. They submitted these certificates to Congress and the National Archives.

This might sound like a local initiative by overzealous Trump supporters. It wasn't.

According to Nevada's December 2023 indictment, the Nevada fake electors were recruited and directed by Trump campaign officials working at the national level. The indictment names six Nevada Republicans as defendants but identifies multiple unnamed co-conspirators who coordinated the scheme across states.

Here's what makes Nevada's case particularly instructive: the indictment describes how Trump campaign attorney Kenneth Chesebro circulated detailed instructions to Republican parties in multiple states about how to organize fake elector meetings. The Nevada participants received templates for the fraudulent certificates, instructions about timing, and guidance about submitting the documents to Congress.

The "Just in Case" Cover Story

One defense you'll hear is that these were "contingent" or "alternate" electors—a backup plan in case Trump won his legal challenges. According to reporting by CNN, this language appeared in some states' certificates.

But Nevada's certificates didn't say "contingent" or "alternate." According to the indictment, they declared flatly that the signers "ARE the duly elected and qualified Electors." This mirrors what happened in several other states.

More importantly, by December 14, Nevada's legal challenges were already dead. Biden had won Nevada by over 33,000 votes—a 2.4 percentage point margin. Trump's lawsuits in Nevada had been dismissed. The state had already certified Biden's victory on November 24.

So what were they contingent on, exactly? The answer appears in the indictment: the fake certificates were part of a plan to create enough confusion that Vice President Pence could delay or reject the count on January 6.

The National Coordination Infrastructure

What I find most significant about the Nevada case is what it reveals about organizational structure.

The Nevada Attorney General's investigation documented how the scheme operated through several layers:

Layer 1: National legal architects. Attorneys developed the legal theory and strategy, created template documents, and provided coordination across states.

Layer 2: Campaign operatives. Trump campaign officials recruited participants, arranged logistics, and transmitted instructions from the legal team to local actors.

Layer 3: State-level implementers. Local Republican officials and party members actually signed the documents and submitted them.

This structure is important because it created plausible deniability at each level. Local participants could claim they were following instructions from campaign lawyers. Campaign operatives could claim they were just facilitating what local parties wanted. And the legal architects could claim they were exploring all possible options.

Why This Matters for 2028

Here's the thing that keeps me up at night: this infrastructure still exists.

The Nevada scheme failed in 2020 primarily because key officials—Vice President Pence, governors who refused to submit fraudulent certificates, secretaries of state who documented what actually happened—refused to play along. But as I noted in a previous newsletter (very briefly!), the dependence on individual integrity is not a structural safeguard.

What worries me is that future attempts could be more sophisticated in three ways:

First, better legal coverage. The 2020 fake elector schemes were legally sloppy. Certificates had incorrect language. Submissions were poorly timed. The legal theory was half-baked. None of this is hard to fix. According to analysis by Protect Democracy, several conservative legal groups have been developing more refined theories about how state legislatures could intervene in presidential elections.

Second, more careful participant selection. Many 2020 fake electors now face criminal charges. Future schemes would likely recruit participants who understand they're part of a coordinated legal strategy (and are willing to fight in court) rather than people who thought they were performing a ceremonial backup function.

Third, state-level official buy-in. The 2020 scheme depended on forging certificates without cooperation from governors or secretaries of state. But as of 2026, several swing states have elected secretaries of state who disputed 2020's results. If someone sympathetic is actually in the office with certification authority, you don't need to forge anything.

The Accountability Gap

Here's where things get uncomfortable. As of early 2026, the consequences for the 2020 fake elector scheme remain limited.

Nevada charged six people in December 2023. Michigan charged 16 people in July 2023. Georgia included the fake elector scheme in its broader RICO case against Trump and 18 others.

But many participants face no consequences at all. According to CNN's comprehensive investigation, 84 people across seven states signed fraudulent elector certificates. Most haven't been charged.

This creates a dangerous precedent problem. If you're planning a 2028 operation, you're looking at the 2020 prosecutions and thinking: "Most people got away with it. The ones who were charged are fighting in court. And even if I'm eventually charged, that's years away."

The slow pace of accountability makes the scheme look less risky than it actually was.

What We Can Learn from Nevada's Approach

One reason I focused on Nevada is that their investigation took a different approach than most other states.

Rather than charging just the fake electors themselves, Nevada's indictment treated the scheme as a conspiracy and named the local participants while describing the national coordination structure. This is legally significant because it establishes that this wasn't six random Nevada Republicans making a mistake—it was part of a deliberate multi-state operation.

According to Politico's reporting on the Nevada indictment, this approach follows the framework prosecutors use for organized fraud schemes: document the coordination, establish the conspiracy, and show that participants knew they were part of something larger.

This matters for future deterrence. If potential fake electors in 2028 understand they could face conspiracy charges that expose the whole operation, not just isolated fraud charges for their individual actions, the calculus changes.

The Documentation Problem

One thing that made the 2020 fake elector prosecutions possible: participants created a detailed paper trail. They signed official-looking certificates. They submitted documents to federal agencies. They sent emails coordinating logistics.

Future efforts might be less documented. According to ProPublica's investigation of the fake elector schemes, much of the national coordination happened through informal conversations, encrypted messages, and verbal instructions that left minimal records.

The Nevada case relied heavily on emails and documents the participants themselves created. That evidence exists because the 2020 operation moved quickly and messily. A more careful 2028 operation could be much harder to prove after the fact.

This is why I keep coming back to the importance of preventive infrastructure rather than reactive prosecution. By the time you're gathering evidence for a criminal case, the election has already been disrupted.
