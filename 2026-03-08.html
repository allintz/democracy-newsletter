The Hidden Network: How Cleta Mitchell Built a Volunteer Army to Challenge Elections

Reading time: 5 minutes

If you've been following election integrity debates, you've probably heard of the Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network. But there's a good chance you haven't heard much about the woman who actually built it: Cleta Mitchell.

This matters because Mitchell has created something more durable than any single lawsuit or challenge. She's built institutional infrastructure—training programs, data systems, legal networks, and volunteer pipelines—designed to operate election after election. Understanding how this network actually works is crucial for anyone thinking about democracy protection between now and 2028.

Let me walk you through what she's built, how it operates, and what makes it different from previous voter challenge efforts.

Who Is Cleta Mitchell?

Cleta Mitchell is a longtime Republican attorney and activist. According to ProPublica's 2022 investigation, she's been involved in conservative politics for decades, but gained national prominence in January 2021 when she participated in Trump's phone call pressuring Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger to "find 11,780 votes."

After that call became public, her law firm asked her to resign. But instead of retreating, she doubled down.

In April 2021, Mitchell joined the Conservative Partnership Institute as a senior legal fellow. Within months, she'd partnered with the Heritage Foundation to launch what became the Election Integrity Network.

The Three-Part Infrastructure

Mitchell's operation rests on three distinct but interconnected systems. Let me explain each one.

First: The training pipeline. According to the Heritage Foundation's own materials, the Election Integrity Network has trained over 5,000 volunteers across battleground states as of early 2026. These aren't random protesters—they receive structured training on election law, voter roll analysis, and challenge procedures.

The training happens year-round through state-level chapters. CNN reported in 2023 that these sessions teach volunteers how to request voter registration data, identify supposed "discrepancies," file formal challenges, and serve as poll watchers or election observers.

This is different from historical voter challenge efforts in a crucial way. Previous programs were typically reactive—they'd mobilize around a specific election and then disappear. Mitchell built something that operates continuously, recruiting and training new volunteers in off-years when media attention is minimal.

Second: The data infrastructure. The network doesn't just train people—it gives them tools.

According to Documented's reporting in 2024, the Election Integrity Network provides volunteers with access to proprietary software for analyzing voter rolls. This software compares state voter registration databases against other datasets—change-of-address records, property records, Social Security death files—looking for supposed anomalies.

Here's the problem: these data matching systems produce extremely high false positive rates. When you compare imperfect databases with different formatting standards and data quality issues, you get tons of apparent "matches" that aren't actually matches.

The Brennan Center found in their 2022 analysis that voter roll matching systems typically flag 5-15% of legitimate registrations as problematic. But volunteers trained to look for fraud interpret these technical errors as evidence of wrongdoing.

Third: The legal support network. This might be the most important piece.

Individual volunteers filing challenges can be brushed aside or intimidated by county attorneys. But Mitchell's network provides coordinated legal backing. According to Democracy Docket's tracking, the network connects volunteers with experienced election attorneys who provide templates, guidance, and sometimes direct representation.

This means when election officials try to dismiss frivolous challenges, they face coordinated legal pushback. And when one county develops a successful challenge strategy, it gets systematically replicated across other jurisdictions.

How It Actually Operates

Let me give you a concrete example of how this works in practice.

In Georgia's 2024 primary, volunteers with the Election Integrity Network filed challenges against approximately 38,000 voter registrations across multiple counties. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution documented that these challenges used nearly identical language and data analysis methods—clear evidence of coordinated training.

County election boards had to investigate each challenge individually. This consumed enormous staff time in the months before the election, when officials should have been focused on logistics and voter education.

Here's what makes this effective: even when most challenges fail, the process achieves several goals. It ties up administrative resources. It creates opportunities for sympathetic election board members to approve some challenges. It generates news coverage about "voter roll problems." And it trains volunteers in election procedures and builds their confidence for future actions.

The Georgia Secretary of State's office reported that approximately 62% of the 2024 challenges were eventually rejected as lacking sufficient evidence. But that means 38% were approved—representing thousands of voters who lost registration, at least temporarily.

Some of those voters likely discovered the problem only when they showed up to vote.

The Difference From Previous Efforts

You might be thinking: didn't Republicans try mass voter challenges before? What's new here?

Good question. Yes, the Republican National Committee ran ballot security programs in the 1980s that led to a consent decree banning such activities until 2018. I mentioned this in a previous newsletter, so I won't rehash it.

But Mitchell's network operates differently in three key ways.

First: it's technically legal. The activities—requesting public voter data, filing challenges, serving as poll watchers—are all permitted under election law in most states. The network carefully stays within legal boundaries, even when the cumulative effect is voter suppression.

Second: it's decentralized. Rather than top-down RNC coordination (which created legal vulnerability), Mitchell built a network of state-level chapters with local volunteers. This makes it much harder to challenge legally or shut down administratively.

Third: it's permanent. Previous efforts were campaign-cycle projects. This operates year-round, building institutional knowledge and capacity that compounds over time.

According to Mother Jones' investigation in 2025, the network's budget grew from approximately $5 million in 2022 to over $20 million in 2025, with funding from major conservative donors including the Bradley Foundation and DonorsTrust.

That funding supports paid state coordinators, data infrastructure, legal support, and continuous training programs.

Why This Matters for 2028

Here's what concerns me most about this infrastructure.

By November 2028, the Election Integrity Network will have been operating for seven years. That's seven years of volunteer recruitment, training refinement, legal strategy development, and data infrastructure improvement.

The volunteers trained in 2022 will be highly experienced by 2028. The data systems will be more sophisticated. The legal playbook will incorporate lessons from hundreds of previous challenges.

And because this operates at the state and county level, it can exploit the jurisdictional fragmentation I've discussed in previous newsletters. A coordinated challenge campaign across 50 counties in three swing states could create enough chaos to affect certification deadlines or public confidence in results.

The Democracy Docket analysis I linked earlier notes that the network is specifically focusing on counties with narrow margins in recent elections—exactly where challenges could be most disruptive in a close presidential race.

The Broader Opposition Ecosystem

Mitchell's network doesn't operate in isolation. It connects to and coordinates with several other organizations.

True the Vote provides some of the data analysis tools and methodology. According to NPR's reporting, True the Vote has distributed its voter roll analysis software to allied groups.

America First Legal, the organization founded by Trump advisor Stephen Miller, provides legal support in some cases. Politico reported in 2024 that AFL has filed coordinated lawsuits challenging state voter roll maintenance in swing states.

And state-level election denial groups feed volunteers into Mitchell's training programs. In Arizona, for example, groups that emerged from the 2020 election challenges now serve as recruitment pipelines for the Election Integrity Network.

This creates a mutually reinforcing ecosystem where organizations specialize in different tactics but coordinate on strategy.

What's Being Built vs What's Theater

One challenge in analyzing this landscape is distinguishing between genuine infrastructure building and political theater.

Some election denial activities are primarily about fundraising, media attention, or activist energy. They make noise but don't create durable capacity.

Mitchell's network is different. The sustained investment in training, data systems, and legal infrastructure indicates they're building something designed to last and scale.

The ProPublica piece I mentioned earlier quotes Mitchell explicitly talking about building "permanent infrastructure" that operates "election cycle after election cycle."

This is a different model from previous Republican election efforts, which tended to be cyclical—ramping up before elections and then largely dissolving.

Epistemic Humility Note

I want to acknowledge uncertainty about effectiveness here.

We have good data on training numbers, budget growth, and challenge volume. But it's harder to measure actual impact on election outcomes.

The challenges filed so far have primarily targeted legitimate voters, but most have been rejected. So you could argue the system is working—frivolous challenges get dismissed, and eligible voters ultimately keep their registration.

But that misses three problems. First, even rejected challenges impose administrative costs and delays. Second, some percentage of wrongly challenged voters won't discover the problem until Election Day. Third, the process trains and emboldens volunteers for more sophisticated future efforts.

So the real question isn't whether current challenges are succeeding, but whether the infrastructure being built now will be more effective in 2028 with three more years of development.

I think the evidence suggests yes, but reasonable people could disagree about the magnitude of the threat.

Bottom Line

Cleta Mitchell has built something unprecedented in recent American election history: a permanent, volunteer-based infrastructure for systematically challenging voter registrations and election procedures.

This infrastructure operates legally within existing election law, making it difficult to counter through litigation. It's decentralized across states and counties, making it difficult to shut down administratively. And it's been building institutional capacity for five years, with another two years before the 2028 election.

For anyone building pro-democracy projects, this creates a specific challenge: you need countervailing infrastructure, not just reactive responses.

That means:

  • Legal defense networks that can rapidly support election officials facing coordinated challenges
  • Voter protection programs that proactively monitor challenge filings and help affected voters
  • Data transparency tools that expose false positive rates in challenge data
  • Election official training on efficiently processing and rejecting frivolous challenges
  • Public education about voter rights when facing registration challenges
