The Electoral College's Winner-Take-All Problem: Why 51% of Pennsylvania Equals 100% of Its Power

Epistemic status: High confidence on historical facts and constitutional mechanics; moderate confidence on reform feasibility given political constraints.

---

Here's something that might surprise you: the Electoral College's biggest vulnerability isn't the faithless elector problem or the contingent election mechanism we've discussed before. It's something much simpler and more fundamental—the winner-take-all system that 48 out of 50 states use to award their electoral votes.

This matters tremendously for anyone worried about election subversion in 2028. Because winner-take-all creates what security researchers would call a "single point of failure"—it means that flipping a handful of votes in one state can flip all of that state's electoral votes, making targeted subversion dramatically more efficient than it would be under almost any other system.

Let me walk through why this is so concerning, and why it's getting surprisingly little attention in democracy protection circles.

How Winner-Take-All Actually Works

The Constitution doesn't require winner-take-all allocation of electoral votes. Article II, Section 1 gives state legislatures broad authority to choose how electors are appointed: "Each State shall appoint, in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct, a Number of Electors..."

That phrase—"in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct"—means states can divide their electoral votes however they want. And initially, they didn't use winner-take-all at all.

In the 1789 presidential election, several states had their legislatures directly choose electors without any popular vote. Others held district-based elections where each congressional district picked one elector. According to the National Archives, winner-take-all only became the dominant system gradually, as states realized it gave their preferred party maximum influence in close national elections.

By 1836, all but one state had adopted winner-take-all. Today, only Maine (since 1972) and Nebraska (since 1992) use a different system—they award two electoral votes to the statewide winner and one to the winner of each congressional district.

The Attack Surface Problem

From a security perspective, winner-take-all creates an enormous "attack surface"—the technical term for the collection of points where a system can be compromised.

Let's use Pennsylvania as a concrete example. Pennsylvania has 19 electoral votes. In 2020, Biden won the state by 80,555 votes out of about 6.9 million cast—a margin of about 1.2%.

Under winner-take-all, Biden got all 19 electoral votes. Trump got zero.

Now think about what this means for a would-be election subverter. If you could flip just 40,278 votes in Pennsylvania—through fraudulent ballot challenges, improper purges, selective certification refusals, whatever—you'd flip all 19 electoral votes. That's a 38-vote swing in the Electoral College from a relatively small manipulation affecting 0.6% of Pennsylvania's votes.

Compare this to a proportional system. If Pennsylvania allocated its 19 electoral votes proportionally based on each candidate's vote share, Biden would have received about 10 electoral votes and Trump about 9. To achieve the same 38-vote Electoral College swing, you'd need to flip enough votes to change Pennsylvania from roughly 50-50 to roughly 100-0—essentially the entire state. That's obviously impossible through subtle subversion.

This isn't just theoretical. The fake electors scheme we covered previously targeted exactly this vulnerability. By trying to submit alternative slates of electors from seven swing states, Trump's team was attempting to flip entire states' worth of electoral votes based on disputed vote margins.

The Concentration Risk

Winner-take-all also creates what I'll call "concentration risk"—it focuses all the incentives for subversion on a small number of competitive states.

Right now, there are really only seven genuinely competitive swing states in presidential elections: Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. These seven states have a combined 93 electoral votes.

Under winner-take-all, these 93 votes are potentially "flippable" through state-level manipulation. If you're building an election subversion infrastructure, you know exactly where to focus: get control of county election boards in Maricopa County, Arizona; Fulton County, Georgia; Wayne County, Michigan. Train your volunteer challengers in Milwaukee and Philadelphia. That's it. You don't need a national operation—you need a targeted operation in about a dozen counties.

The Heritage Foundation's Election Integrity Network, which we've covered before, understands this perfectly. Their volunteer training and legal support is concentrated almost entirely in these swing states, because winner-take-all makes everywhere else electorally irrelevant.

A more distributed system would force would-be subverters to operate nationally, making coordination much harder and detection much easier.

Why Don't We Just Change It?

This seems like an obvious fix, right? If winner-take-all creates such dangerous vulnerabilities, why don't states just switch to proportional allocation or district-based systems?

The short answer is: partisan incentive problems.

Imagine you're Pennsylvania, a swing state that slightly leans Democratic. If Pennsylvania switches to proportional allocation while other states keep winner-take-all, Democrats lose influence. Pennsylvania's 19 electoral votes would typically split 10-9 instead of going entirely to one candidate. But Texas's 40 electoral votes would still go entirely to Republicans, and California's 54 would still go entirely to Democrats.

According to analysis by FairVote, if only swing states adopted proportional allocation, it would systematically advantage the party that controls the most electoral votes in safe states—currently Republicans.

This creates a collective action problem. The only stable equilibrium is either everyone keeps winner-take-all, or everyone switches to something else at the same time. But coordinating 50 state legislatures to change simultaneously is essentially impossible, especially when the current system advantages whichever party happens to be winning.

The National Popular Vote Compact Workaround

There is one creative attempt to solve this: the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact.

Here's how it works: states pass laws agreeing to award all their electoral votes to whoever wins the national popular vote—but these laws only take effect once states representing 270+ electoral votes have joined the compact.

The clever part is this solves the collective action problem. No state disadvantages itself by joining, because the change only happens once enough states join that it doesn't matter what system they use—the national popular vote winner will get 270+ electoral votes automatically.

As of early 2026, according to National Popular Vote's tracker, 17 states plus DC have joined, representing 209 electoral votes. They need 61 more electoral votes to reach the 270 threshold.

But there's a major problem: the compact has stalled. Almost all the states that have joined lean Democratic. The remaining states needed are either Republican-controlled or politically divided, and Republican legislatures have shown little interest in joining, likely because they correctly perceive the Electoral College as currently favoring Republican candidates in close elections.

The Constitutional Uncertainty

There's also a real question about whether the National Popular Vote Compact is even constitutional.

Some legal scholars argue it violates the Compact Clause (Article I, Section 10, Clause 3), which requires congressional consent for interstate compacts that increase state power relative to the federal government. Defenders argue it doesn't require consent because it's just states exercising their Article II authority to choose how to appoint electors.

This would almost certainly be litigated if the compact ever took effect. And we have no idea how the current Supreme Court would rule—you'd have originalists who might say states have plenary authority under Article II, balanced against concerns about interstate compacts reshaping federal elections without congressional involvement.

My guess? If the compact determined the outcome of a close presidential election, the litigation would make Bush v. Gore look simple and uncontroversial by comparison.

What This Means for 2028

Here's the uncomfortable bottom line: winner-take-all is almost certainly here to stay for 2028, and probably beyond.

The National Popular Vote Compact won't reach 270 electoral votes in time. Individual states won't unilaterally switch to proportional systems because it would disadvantage them. And there's essentially zero chance of a constitutional amendment—those require two-thirds of Congress plus three-quarters of state legislatures.

This means the 2028 election will have the same structural vulnerability we've had for decades: control of a few thousand votes in a handful of swing state counties can determine the presidency.

For anyone working on democracy protection, this has several implications:

First, county-level defenses in swing states matter enormously. We've covered some of these in previous newsletters—legal defense networks for election officials, transparent canvassing processes, rapid response teams. But the strategic importance of these defenses is even higher than it might appear, because winner-take-all means that blocking subversion in one county can save an entire state's electoral votes.

Second, detection systems need to be hyper-focused on swing states. We talked before about the need for real-time statistical monitoring and parallel vote tabulations. Those resources should be concentrated in Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, North Carolina, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. That's where the highest-value attacks will occur.

Third, legal preparation for post-election litigation is critical. Winner-take-all means close elections will trigger intense litigation, because the stakes are so high. Groups like Protect Democracy and the Brennan Center that prepare for election litigation should be war-gaming scenarios now for how 2028 disputes might unfold in swing states.

Finally, state-level reforms that reduce subversion opportunities matter more than national reforms. Since we're stuck with winner-take-all, improvements to Pennsylvania's canvassing process, or Michigan's certification safeguards, or Georgia's election security measures have outsized importance. Democracy funders should recognize that state-level infrastructure grants in swing states have much higher expected value than equivalent grants elsewhere.

Project Idea: Electoral Systems Analysis Network

There's a real gap here in the democracy protection space: nobody is doing deep quantitative analysis of how different electoral system vulnerabilities interact with specific subversion tactics.

What I'd love to see is an "Electoral Systems Analysis Network"—basically a red team for democracy that models different attack scenarios under different electoral rules.

For example: what's the minimum number of provisional ballots you'd need to wrongly reject in Pennsylvania to flip the state, under different vote margin scenarios? How does that number change if Pennsylvania adopted Maine-style district allocation? What if it used ranked-choice voting? How do different audit requirements change the probability of detecting ballot-level manipulation?

This kind of analysis could help prioritize reforms and defenses based on which ones most reduce attack surface. Organizations like the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project or Verified Voting might be natural hosts for this work.

Bottom Line

Winner-take-all allocation of electoral votes is probably the single biggest structural vulnerability in US presidential elections. It concentrates all the incentives for election subversion on a small number of swing states, and it means that relatively small-scale manipulation can flip large numbers of electoral votes.

This system is almost certainly not going to change before 2028, and possibly not for decades after. The National Popular Vote Compact is stalled, individual states won't move unilaterally, and constitutional amendments are politically impossible.

For democracy protection advocates, this means accepting that the 2028 election will be decided in about seven swing states, and that county-level subversion in those states poses an existential threat. Resources and attention should be concentrated accordingly—not evenly distributed nationally, but laser-focused on the specific jurisdictions where winner-take-all creates maximum vulnerability.

The depressing part is that this has been obvious for a long time, and we're essentially stuck with it. The hopeful part is that once you accept this constraint, it actually simplifies strategic planning. You know exactly where the highest-value targets are, which means you know exactly where your defenses need to be strongest.
