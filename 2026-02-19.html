Topic: How Local Election Officials Actually Get Chosen (And Why It Matters More Than You Think)

Reading time: 5 minutes

---

When we talk about protecting elections, we usually focus on the big names: secretaries of state, governors, members of Congress. But there's a massive vulnerability hiding in plain sight at the county level, where most of the actual work of running elections happens.

Here's the thing: we barely pay attention to how local election officials get their jobs. And the methods vary wildly—some are appointed by partisan county commissioners, some are elected in low-turnout races, some are career civil servants. These differences aren't just administrative trivia. They create very different incentives and vulnerabilities when someone tries to subvert an election.

Let me show you why this matters.

The Hidden Power of County Election Officials

In most states, county-level officials are responsible for the actual mechanics of elections. They maintain voter rolls, operate polling places, count ballots, and—critically—certify results before sending them to the state.

According to research by the MIT Election Data and Science Lab, approximately 10,500 separate jurisdictions administer elections in the United States. That's 10,500 different offices where someone needs to make decisions about how voting happens.

After 2020, we learned these officials face real pressure. The Brennan Center documented that one in three election officials felt unsafe because of their job, and 17% reported receiving threats. One in five said they were likely to leave their position before 2024.

The question is: what happens when these positions turn over? Who replaces them, and how?

Four Very Different Selection Methods

Let me walk through the main ways Americans choose local election officials, because each method creates different vulnerabilities.

Method 1: Partisan Election

In some counties, voters directly elect their election administrator—often called a "county clerk" or "clerk and recorder." Sounds democratic, right?

The problem is turnout. According to research by the Election Assistance Commission, county-level election administrator races typically see 15-25% turnout in off-year elections, and many races are uncontested.

This creates an opening. In 2022, according to reporting by Reuters, at least six county clerk positions in swing states were won by candidates who questioned the 2020 election results. These officials now control ballot counting and certification in their counties.

Method 2: Appointment by County Commissioners

Many counties use appointed election directors chosen by county commissioners (who are themselves elected, usually on a partisan basis).

This method is common in states like North Carolina and Georgia. The Voting Rights Lab documented that after 2020, multiple county boards attempted to replace experienced election directors with candidates seen as more sympathetic to election fraud claims.

In Otero County, New Mexico, county commissioners refused to certify the June 2022 primary election results, citing distrust in voting machines. The New Mexico Supreme Court had to order certification. This wasn't a rogue election official—it was the board that appoints the election official.

Method 3: Bipartisan Election Boards

Some states use bipartisan or multipartisan boards rather than a single official. For example, many counties in Pennsylvania have three-member boards of elections with mandatory party balance.

The theory is that bipartisan oversight prevents partisan manipulation. According to research by Persily and Stewart in the Journal of Law and Politics, this structure generally increases public confidence in results.

But there's a catch: bipartisan boards require both parties to act in good faith. When one party embraces election denialism, the "balance" becomes "one normal official and one election denier," which can create deadlock. In 2022, multiple Pennsylvania county boards split 2-1 on certification decisions along partisan lines.

Method 4: Professional/Civil Service Appointment

A smaller number of jurisdictions use professional civil service systems where election administrators are hired based on qualifications rather than political connections, similar to how city managers work.

This is less common but exists in some larger counties. The advantage is obvious: you get people with actual expertise who aren't beholden to partisan pressures. The disadvantage? Less direct democratic accountability—though whether that's actually a disadvantage for election administration is debatable.

Why Selection Method Matters for Subversion Attempts

Here's where this gets concrete for 2026 and 2028.

Different selection methods create different vulnerability profiles:

Elected positions are vulnerable to low-information, low-turnout races where extreme candidates can win primaries and then coast in general elections where voters know little about the candidates.

Appointed positions are vulnerable to pressure from the appointing authority—county commissioners can threaten to fire or replace officials who don't cooperate with subversion attempts.

Bipartisan boards are vulnerable to deadlock and legitimacy battles when one party's representatives refuse to certify results.

Civil service positions are more insulated from immediate political pressure but vulnerable to budget cuts, policy changes, or political leaders creating parallel structures to bypass them.

None of these systems is perfectly secure. But they fail in different ways, which means attackers need different strategies for each.

The 2024-2025 Turnover Wave

Here's what worries me about the current moment.

The Brennan Center's 2022 survey suggested roughly 30-40% of local election officials were considering leaving their positions. That's potentially 3,000-4,000 positions turning over between 2022 and 2026.

Many of these positions are being filled right now—through elections that happened in November 2024 and 2025, or through appointments being made by county boards in 2025 and early 2026.

In most counties, these transitions are happening quietly, without much press coverage or public attention. We don't have good systematic data on who's replacing experienced administrators or what their views on election integrity are.

That's a problem, because by the time we're paying attention in late 2027 or early 2028, these people will already be in place, learning the systems, and building relationships with state officials.

What History Teaches Us

There's precedent for why this matters so much.

During Reconstruction, according to historian Eric Foner's research, Southern states systematically replaced local election officials to enable the suppression of Black voters. The tools included restrictive appointment requirements, loyalty tests, and simply refusing to seat officials from opposition parties.

The mechanisms were often administrative rather than violent: changing who counted votes, who verified voter qualifications, and who certified results. The 1876 presidential election crisis turned on disputed returns from South Carolina, Florida, and Louisiana—disputes that started with county-level certification fights.

More recently, scholars like Pippa Norris have documented how authoritarian-leaning governments often start by capturing election administration at the local level before moving to more visible national-level changes. It's less dramatic than a coup, but potentially more durable.

Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Protection

If you're thinking about where to focus energy in the democracy space, local election official selection deserves way more attention than it currently gets.

The strategic timing matters: many of these positions are filled in 2025-2026 for the officials who will administer the 2028 election. We're in that window right now.

Here are three directions worth exploring:

Project idea #1: County election official tracking database. Nobody has a comprehensive, updated database of who holds these 10,500+ positions, how they got there, and when their terms expire. Creating and maintaining this would be valuable infrastructure for election protection efforts. It's not glamorous, but it's foundational.

Project idea #2: Professional development support. Organizations like the Election Center provide training for local election officials, but they're chronically underfunded. Expanding professional education—especially for newly appointed or elected officials—could help ensure baseline competence and adherence to best practices regardless of political views.

Project idea #3: Legal intervention capacity. When county officials refuse to certify results (as in Otero County, New Mexico), there need to be rapid legal response mechanisms. Groups like Protect Democracy do this work, but the capacity needs to scale to handle potential simultaneous crises in dozens of counties.

Existing organization doing this work: The Election Official Legal Defense Network provides free legal support to election officials facing threats or pressure. They're directly addressing the harassment issue that's driving experienced officials out of the field.

The bigger strategic point: election subversion doesn't require capturing the presidency or even a governor's office. It can work by controlling enough county-level choke points to create chaos and disputed results. That means defenders need to think about the whole system, not just the high-profile positions.

And that work needs to happen now, while these positions are being filled, not in October 2028 when it's too late to do anything about who's in office.
