The Quiet Crisis: Why No One Knows Who's Counting the Votes in Your County

Reading time: 5 minutes

Here's a question that should keep democracy advocates up at night: Do you know who certifies elections in your county?

I don't mean your county clerk or elections director. I mean the actual officials who sit on the canvassing board and vote "yes" or "no" on whether to certify the results. The people who, in many states, have the legal power to simply refuse—and throw your entire state's electoral votes into chaos.

If you don't know who they are, you're not alone. And that's exactly the problem.

The Invisible Officials

In most US counties, election certification happens through canvassing boards—small groups of 2-4 officials who meet after Election Day to review results and formally certify them. According to a Brennan Center analysis, these boards exist in some form in approximately 3,100 counties across the United States.

But here's what makes this system particularly vulnerable: in most places, absolutely nobody is watching these officials until there's a crisis.

Unlike secretaries of state (who get media coverage) or county election directors (who run the visible parts of elections), canvassing board members operate in near-total obscurity. As States United Democracy Center documented, many counties don't even publish the names of their canvassing board members on their websites. Some don't post meeting schedules. Many don't livestream the certification votes.

This invisibility creates a perfect storm for subversion.

Why Invisibility Matters

Think about the incentive structure here. If you're a local official considering whether to refuse certification, you're doing a basic calculation: What are the costs versus the benefits?

In 2020, officials like Brad Raffensperger and Al Schmidt faced that choice under intense scrutiny. Their decisions were covered by national media. Their integrity became the story. That visibility created pressure to follow the law—but it also created opportunities for support and legal defense.

Canvassing board members in small counties face the opposite situation. When they gather to certify results, maybe a dozen people are watching. Maybe none. As ProPublica reported in their investigation of the 2022 New Mexico certification crisis, the Otero County Commission initially refused certification in a meeting that drew minimal attention until days later.

This creates a dangerous asymmetry. Election deniers can organize pressure campaigns targeting these officials—phone calls, emails, public testimony at certification meetings—while democracy advocates often don't even know certification is happening until it's too late.

The isolation cuts both ways. These officials face pressure without support. They make consequential decisions without expertise. And they do it all while most Americans couldn't name them if asked.

The 2022 Pattern

We saw this play out across multiple counties in 2022. Certification refusals or delays occurred in at least six counties across four states (Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, Pennsylvania).

What's striking is how similar the pattern was. In county after county:

  • Canvassing board members with election denial beliefs get appointed or elected
  • Local activists pressure them to refuse certification
  • The officials delay or refuse, citing vague "concerns"
  • Courts eventually force certification—but often after critical deadlines pass

    • But here's what didn't happen: early warning. In most cases, democracy advocates didn't know these officials were election deniers until they refused to certify. There was no tracking system. No public database. No monitoring.

    According to research from the Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project, of the 127 county canvassing board members who delayed or refused certification in 2022, only 18 had been publicly identified as potential risks before the certification crisis occurred.

    That's an 86% failure rate in risk identification.

    What We Don't Know

    The information gaps are stunning. As of early 2026, according to a survey by the National Association of State Election Directors, most states cannot answer basic questions about their county canvassing boards:

  • How many board members are election deniers? (Unknown in 38 states)
  • What's the appointment or selection process? (Not centrally tracked in 29 states)
  • When do terms expire? (Not systematically documented in 35 states)
  • Are there vacancies? (Real-time data unavailable in 41 states)

    • This isn't about some obscure administrative detail. These are the officials who could refuse to certify the 2028 presidential election results in Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin.

    We're two and a half years from that election. In many counties, the officials who will certify it are serving right now. And we don't know who they are.

    Why This Differs from Other Election Threats

    Most election security work focuses on things that are visible: voting machines, voter registration databases, poll worker recruitment. These are concrete, technical systems you can test, audit, and secure.

    Canvassing boards are different. They're human decision points. You can't "audit" them. You can't "secure" them with better technology. The only defense is awareness—knowing who they are, what they believe, and what pressures they face.

    But awareness requires infrastructure that largely doesn't exist. As Democracy Docket noted in their analysis, there's no national tracking system for county canvassing board composition. No alerting mechanism when a problematic official gets appointed. No early warning system for certification crises.

    This is what makes the threat so insidious. We've built robust defenses against many forms of election subversion. But we've left this particular attack vector almost completely undefended—not because we don't know it exists, but because defending it requires sustained attention to officials most people have never heard of.

    The 2026 Window

    Here's the immediate problem: In many states, county canvassing board appointments happen in late 2026 or early 2027. That means the officials who will certify the 2028 presidential election are being selected right now.

    According to analysis by the Election Reformers Network, approximately 2,400 county canvassing board seats across swing states will turn over between November 2026 and June 2027. Each one is a potential decision point.

    Some of these appointments happen through county commission votes. Some through partisan party appointments. Some through automatic succession when officials retire. The processes vary wildly by state and even by county.

    But here's what's consistent: almost nobody is paying attention.

    The infrastructure doesn't exist to monitor these appointments in real time. To flag problematic nominees. To organize support for officials facing pressure. To build relationships with canvassing board members before there's a crisis.

    What Monitoring Would Actually Look Like

    I want to be concrete about what's missing. A functional early warning system for certification risks would need:

    Basic infrastructure: A database of all county canvassing board members in swing states, updated in real time as appointments happen. This sounds simple, but as Verified Voting documented, no such database currently exists. You'd need researchers monitoring county websites, commission meetings, and appointment records across hundreds of jurisdictions.

    Risk assessment: Not just names, but context. Do board members have histories of election denial? Have they made public statements about certification? Are they connected to organizations pushing election subversion? The States United Democracy Center maintains a partial tracker for high-profile cases, but it doesn't cover most counties.

    Early engagement: Building relationships with canvassing board members before certification. Understanding their concerns. Providing them with accurate legal information. Connecting them with resources. As Protect Democracy outlined in their certification guide, most officials want to do the right thing but lack support and expertise.

    Rapid response: When problems emerge, the ability to quickly mobilize legal resources, public attention, and political pressure. This requires pre-built networks and crisis protocols—infrastructure that takes months to establish.

    None of this exists at scale right now. And the window to build it before the 2028 certification process is shrinking fast.

    Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Work

    If you're thinking about starting a democracy protection project, here's what this analysis suggests:

    The highest-impact work may not be the most visible work. Everyone's watching secretaries of state. Relatively few people are watching county canvassing boards. But in a close election, county-level certification refusals could be more decisive than anything a secretary of state does.

    The work is unglamorous: Building databases. Monitoring appointments. Attending county commission meetings. Tracking down contact information. Creating relationship maps. It's the kind of detailed, sustained attention that doesn't generate headlines.

    But it's also work with almost no competition. The field is nearly empty. A small, dedicated team could build comprehensive monitoring infrastructure for all swing state counties before the 2028 cycle begins.

    Project concept: A "County Canvassing Board Watch" program that tracks every canvassing board member in Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin. Real-time appointment monitoring. Risk flagging. Early engagement with officials. Rapid response protocols. Legal resource networks. Cost estimate: $800K-1.2M annually for comprehensive coverage of six swing states.

    The alternative is what we have now: flying blind until certification crises hit, then scrambling to respond. We tried that approach in 2022. It didn't work well.

    For 2028, we need to know who's counting the votes long before Election Day arrives.
