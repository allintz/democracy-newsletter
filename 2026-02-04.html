The Fake Electors Scheme: How the 2020 Plot to Submit Fraudulent Electoral Votes Almost Worked

Epistemic status: High confidence. This newsletter draws from court filings, indictments, and investigative journalism that has documented the scheme extensively. Some details about internal deliberations rely on witness testimony and may be incomplete.

When most people think about attempts to overturn the 2020 election, they picture the January 6th Capitol riot. But arguably the most dangerous plan was happening quietly in state capitols and law offices across the country: a coordinated effort to create fake slates of electors who would claim to be the "legitimate" Electoral College representatives from seven states Joe Biden had won.

This wasn't a spontaneous protest that got out of hand. It was a deliberate legal scheme with a paper trail, organizational meetings, and a specific theory of how it could work. Understanding it matters because the infrastructure and playbook still exist—and could be deployed more effectively in a close future election.

What Actually Happened

Here's the basic timeline. On December 14, 2020, the real Electoral College met in all 50 states to cast their votes. Biden electors voted in the states Biden won, Trump electors in the states Trump won. Standard procedure.

But on that same day, groups of Trump supporters gathered in seven states Biden had won—Arizona, Georgia, Michigan, Nevada, New Mexico, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin—and signed documents claiming they were the "duly elected and qualified" electors. According to a detailed CNN investigation, 84 people total participated in these fake elector meetings.

These weren't just symbolic protests. The fake electors signed official-looking certificates with state seals. They used language claiming they were the legitimate electors. And critically, these documents were then transmitted to Congress and the National Archives—the same places the real electoral certificates go.

In Michigan, for example, the fake electors met in the basement of the state Republican Party headquarters. According to charging documents from Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel, they signed certificates falsely claiming to be "the duly elected and qualified Electors for President and Vice President of the United States of America for the State of Michigan."

The Theory Behind the Scheme

Why go through this elaborate charade? Because there was a legal theory—however dubious—for how it could actually work.

The plan had several potential pathways, as documented in the January 6th Committee's final report. The basic logic went like this:

Scenario 1 - The "Dueling Electors" Theory: If Congress received two sets of electoral certificates from the same state, it would create confusion about which slate was legitimate. Under the old Electoral Count Act of 1887, the rules for resolving such disputes were genuinely ambiguous. Trump's legal team believed Vice President Pence could exploit this ambiguity to either reject both slates (reducing Biden's total below 270) or accept the Trump slates.

Scenario 2 - The "Pending Litigation" Cover: By January 6th, Trump's campaign was still pursuing legal challenges in several states. The fake elector certificates could be framed as "conditional" or "contingent"—there just in case the lawsuits succeeded and courts ordered the results overturned. If that happened, you'd need Trump electors ready to go, since the December 14th deadline would have passed.

Scenario 3 - The State Legislature Switcheroo: The most aggressive version involved state legislatures actually attempting to revoke their initial certification and submit the Trump slates as replacements, with the fake certificates already in place to effectuate the change.

The scary thing? None of these scenarios required violence or a military coup. They just required enough officials willing to interpret ambiguous rules in Trump's favor, combined with pre-positioned paperwork that looked official enough to create doubt.

How Close Did It Come to Working?

Closer than most people realize.

The scheme failed primarily because of three factors, not because the plan itself was implausible:

1. The fake electors weren't actually certified by anyone with authority. In most states, the governor certifies which electors are legitimate. No governor was willing to certify Trump electors in a state Biden won, despite significant pressure. According to testimony documented by the January 6th Committee, Trump personally called officials in multiple states, including his infamous call to Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger asking him to "find 11,780 votes."

2. Mike Pence refused to go along. On January 6th, Pence had the fake certificates in front of him alongside the real ones. Trump and his advisors, particularly lawyer John Eastman, pressured Pence to use the competing slates as justification to delay the count or send them back to state legislatures. Pence consulted with advisors and concluded he lacked the constitutional authority to do this. As he wrote in his letter that day, "It is my considered judgment that my oath to support and defend the Constitution constrains me from claiming unilateral authority."

3. The paperwork had problems. Some of the fake elector certificates included language noting they were "conditional" or only valid if court challenges succeeded, which undermined their use as legitimate competing slates. According to reporting by The New York Times, this language was added in some states specifically because participants were uncomfortable claiming to be the legitimate electors when they clearly weren't.

But here's what's chilling: these were all contingent factors, not structural safeguards.

If governors in Georgia and Arizona had been willing to certify Trump electors despite Biden's victory? The certificates would have looked legitimate. If Pence had been more loyal to Trump than to his constitutional interpretation? He could have thrown the process into chaos. If the fake electors had all used consistent, confident language? The documents would have been harder to dismiss.

What Made This Different from Past Disputes

The United States has actually had "dueling electors" before. In 1876, three states (Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina) sent competing slates during the Hayes-Tilden election. But that situation was genuinely ambiguous—there were real disputes about voter intimidation and counting irregularities, and both parties had plausible claims.

The 2020 fake electors were fundamentally different. There was no genuine dispute about who won these states by January 6th. Court challenges had been rejected dozens of times. Recounts had confirmed the results. Election officials from both parties had certified the outcomes.

As legal scholars have noted in the Duke Law Journal, what made the 2020 scheme unprecedented was the creation of fake competing slates despite there being no legitimate ongoing controversy about the vote count results. The electors weren't preserving potentially valid claims—they were manufacturing a dispute that didn't exist.

This matters because it means the scheme didn't require any actual evidence of fraud or irregularities. It just required enough partisan officials willing to create official-looking paperwork and enough ambiguity in the process to make it work.

Why This Could Be More Dangerous Next Time

The fake elector scheme failed, but everyone involved learned from the attempt. Here are the vulnerabilities that remain:

The infrastructure exists. State party organizations now know exactly how to organize alternate elector slates, where they need to meet, what documents they need to create, and where to submit them. The logistical learning curve has been solved.

The legal theories are documented. Law review articles have now analyzed every aspect of the scheme—which means both how to defend against it and how to execute it more effectively. The Eastman memos laying out the legal theory are publicly available. Any competent election lawyer could refine the approach.

The Electoral Count Reform Act helps but doesn't solve everything. Congress passed reforms in 2022 that clarify the Vice President's role is purely ceremonial and raise the threshold for objecting to electoral votes. But as election law expert Rick Hasen has argued, these reforms don't prevent states from submitting fake elector certificates in the first place—they just make it harder to use them successfully once they reach Congress.

Criminal prosecutions may create perverse incentives. Multiple fake electors have now been charged in states like Michigan, Nevada, and Georgia (alongside Trump himself in Georgia). This might deter future participants—or it might create a perception that future attempts need to be even more carefully orchestrated and legally defensible.

The close-election scenario remains plausible. The scheme only makes sense in a genuinely close election where flipping one or two states would change the outcome. The 2024 election wasn't particularly close, but 2028 easily could be. We've had extremely tight Electoral College margins in 2000, 2004, and 2016.

The Deeper Vulnerability

Here's what keeps election experts up at night about the fake elector scheme: it exploited a fundamental assumption built into American election law.

The system assumes that official documents are trustworthy. When Congress receives electoral certificates with state seals and official signatures, the default assumption is that these represent the legitimate will of the state. The Electoral Count Act (even the reformed version) doesn't include robust procedures for verifying whether the people who signed electoral certificates were actually the legally certified electors.

Why would you need such procedures? Because historically, nobody tried to just make up fake certificates. The social norm against doing so was strong enough that the legal system didn't need extensive safeguards.

This is a classic case of what political scientists call "norm erosion." Democratic systems rely on a mix of formal rules and informal norms. When actors start violating previously unthinkable norms—like creating fraudulent official documents—it exposes gaps in the formal rules that nobody thought needed to exist.

As political scientists Steven Levitsky and Daniel Ziblatt argue in their research on democratic erosion, this is how democratic backsliding typically happens—not through obvious coups, but through the systematic exploitation of previously respected norms and procedural ambiguities.

Bottom Line: What This Means for Democracy Defenders

The fake elector scheme matters because it revealed a specific, exploitable vulnerability in how the United States transfers presidential power. Unlike vague concerns about "democratic erosion," this is a concrete mechanism with a documented playbook.

For anyone thinking about starting projects in the democracy space, here are the key takeaways:

  • The certification process needs better authentication mechanisms. Right now, verifying that electoral certificates come from legitimate electors relies too heavily on state seals and official letterhead. There's room for projects that help Congress, the National Archives, or state officials verify the chain of custody for these critical documents.
  • State-level reforms matter enormously. The fake elector scheme required people willing to sign false documents in state capitols. Several states have now passed laws explicitly criminalizing the submission of fake electoral certificates, but many haven't. Organizations like Protect Democracy are working on model legislation.
  • The most dangerous scenario remains one where state officials actually certify fake slates. This means the real battle is ensuring secretaries of state and governors follow legal certification requirements, regardless of political pressure. Support networks for officials facing pressure—like The Elections Group, which provides peer support for election administrators—are critically important.
  • Legal clarity isn't enough without enforcement. Even after the Electoral Count Reform Act, the system still depends on officials choosing to follow the law rather than partisan advantage. This suggests that cultural and institutional support for election officials matters as much as legal reform.
  • Close elections are higher risk. The fake elector scheme only makes sense if flipping one or two states changes the outcome. This isn't an argument for landslide victories (which aren't really controllable), but it does mean that election protection resources should be concentrated in states likely to be close and contested.

    • The fake elector scheme failed in 2020—but it failed because of individual choices by officials like Mike Pence and Brad Raffensperger, not because the system made it impossible. That's the vulnerability that still needs addressing before 2028.
