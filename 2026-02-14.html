The Secret Weapon That Saved Georgia's Elections: How Specific Procedural Rules Made Subversion Harder

Epistemic status: High confidence on the documented facts about Georgia's election procedures and what happened in 2020. Medium confidence on the broader lessons about procedural design, since we're working from a small number of cases.

Today I want to talk about something that sounds incredibly boring but turns out to be critical: the specific procedural rules that govern how election results get certified.

Here's why this matters: When Trump tried to overturn Georgia's 2020 election results, he failed. But it wasn't just because Brad Raffensperger was brave (though he was). The system had specific procedural features that made it much harder to manipulate results—features that were absent in other states.

Understanding these features is directly relevant for 2026 and 2028, because right now, states are actively revising their election procedures. Some changes make manipulation harder. Others make it easier.

What Actually Happened in Georgia

Let's start with what we know. On January 2, 2021, Trump called Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger. The full transcript was leaked to the Washington Post the next day.

Trump asked Raffensperger to "find 11,780 votes"—the exact number needed to flip Georgia. He made various claims about fraud. He suggested Raffensperger could be criminally liable if he didn't act.

Raffensperger said no. But here's the question: Could Raffensperger have said yes? Did he have the legal authority to simply change the results?

The answer is no—and this is where procedural design matters.

Georgia's Specific Procedural Protections

Georgia had several features that made unilateral result-changing nearly impossible:

1. County certification came first, with distributed authority

Before the Secretary of State could certify anything, all 159 Georgia counties had to certify their results. According to Georgia election law, county boards had to submit certified results by a specific deadline.

This meant Raffensperger couldn't just unilaterally change the statewide total. He'd need cooperation from county officials across the state.

2. Recounts were automatic and hand-counted

Because the margin was under 0.5%, Georgia law triggered an automatic recount. But more importantly, Georgia conducted a full hand recount of all 5 million ballots—not just a machine recount.

This created a paper trail that would be extremely difficult to manipulate. You can't just change numbers in a database when thousands of election workers have physically handled and counted paper ballots.

3. The Governor couldn't override the Secretary of State

In Georgia, the Secretary of State has independent constitutional authority over elections. Governor Brian Kemp couldn't have ordered Raffensperger to change the results even if he'd wanted to.

This distributed authority meant Trump would have needed to pressure multiple officials—and as reporting from the Atlanta Journal-Constitution documented, both Raffensperger and Kemp received enormous pressure but held firm.

4. Results were public and verified before certification

Georgia published detailed results by county and precinct before certification. This created transparency that made it harder to change numbers without detection.

If Raffensperger had suddenly "found" 11,780 votes, everyone would have asked: which counties? Which precincts? The specificity of public data made invisible manipulation impossible.

Contrast This With Procedural Vulnerabilities Elsewhere

Now compare Georgia's setup with other states that had weaker procedural protections:

Arizona's county-level chaos: In 2022, Cochise County's board of supervisors initially refused to certify election results at all, delaying statewide certification. They only certified after the Secretary of State filed a lawsuit and a judge ordered certification.

Arizona's system gave individual counties more ability to gum up the works. Georgia's mandatory deadlines and clear hierarchical structure made this harder.

Michigan's decentralized chaos in 2020: Wayne County's board of canvassers (split evenly between parties) initially deadlocked 2-2 on certifying Detroit's results before reversing course after public pressure.

Michigan's system required bipartisan agreement at the county level—which sounds good but created a potential choke point for manipulation.

Pennsylvania's extended ballot receipt deadlines: In 2020, Pennsylvania allowed ballots received up to three days after Election Day to be counted if postmarked by Election Day. This became a major source of contested litigation and claims of illegitimacy.

Not because anything was wrong with the ballots, but because the extended timeline created ambiguity. Georgia's stricter deadlines meant less room for procedural challenges.

The Boring Power of Good Procedural Design

Here's the general principle: Good procedural design makes manipulation require more people and create more evidence.

Georgia's system had several features that accomplished this:

  • Distributed authority: Multiple officials at multiple levels had to agree
  • Paper trails: Physical ballots that thousands of people handled
  • Public transparency: Results published before certification
  • Clear hierarchies: Unambiguous rules about who could do what
  • Mandatory deadlines: Limited discretion to delay or change processes

    • None of these features require heroic officials. They work automatically, regardless of who's in office.

    Compare this to systems that concentrate power in single officials, rely on purely digital records, keep results opaque until certification, or give officials broad discretion. These features make manipulation easier and harder to detect.

    Why This Matters for 2026 and 2028

    Right now, states are revising their election procedures. Some changes strengthen procedural protections. Others weaken them.

    Concerning trends:

    According to research from the Brennan Center, between 2021 and 2023, at least 27 states considered or passed laws that changed certification procedures. Some changes include:

  • Giving individual officials more discretion to reject results
  • Removing automatic triggers for recounts
  • Reducing transparency in vote counting
  • Centralizing authority in partisan officials

    • These changes make systems more vulnerable to the kind of pressure Trump applied in 2020.

    Positive developments:

    Other states have moved in the opposite direction. For example, Michigan's 2023 reforms expanded early voting and strengthened election official protections, making the system more resilient.

    The Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022 also fixed some federal-level vulnerabilities (though not state-level certification procedures).

    The Research on Procedural Protections

    Political scientists have studied what makes election systems resilient to manipulation. The findings align with Georgia's experience:

    Distributed authority helps: Research by political scientists Pippa Norris and Alessandro Nai finds that election systems with distributed authority and multiple checkpoints are more resistant to manipulation than centralized systems.

    The logic is simple: it's easier to pressure one official than to coordinate pressure across dozens of officials at different levels.

    Transparency matters: A 2019 study by Susan Hyde on international election monitoring found that public observation and transparent counting reduce fraud because manipulation becomes visible to more actors.

    Georgia's public results and hand recount created this transparency.

    Paper trails are critical: The PNAS paper by Lawrence Norden et al. on election security emphasizes that voter-verified paper ballots create an auditable record that's much harder to manipulate than purely electronic systems.

    Bottom Line: Design Systems That Work Regardless of Who's Running Them

    The key lesson from Georgia isn't that Brad Raffensperger was brave. It's that Georgia's procedural design made it very difficult for even a weak-willed official to manipulate results without detection.

  • Education and monitoring: Help voters and local officials understand what good procedural design looks like. Many procedural changes sound technical but have major implications.
