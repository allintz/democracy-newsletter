The Democracy Fund: What Happens When Philanthropy Tries to Save Elections

Epistemic status: High confidence on basic facts about the Democracy Fund's structure and grants (based on public 990 filings and their website). Medium confidence on effectiveness assessments—these are inherently subjective and based on limited public information about outcomes.

There's a weird feature of the US democracy protection ecosystem that I keep running into: most of the major organizations defending election integrity get their funding from a surprisingly small number of sources. Today I want to profile one of the biggest—the Democracy Fund—because understanding how pro-democracy work gets funded tells you a lot about what work actually gets done and what gaps remain.

What Is The Democracy Fund?

The Democracy Fund is a bipartisan foundation established in 2014 by eBay founder Pierre Omidyar with an initial $100 million endowment. According to their 2023 annual report, they've now distributed over $250 million in grants to strengthen US democratic institutions.

Their theory of change is straightforward: American democracy is facing serious structural challenges, and strategic philanthropy can help address them by funding organizations working on election administration, voting access, civic engagement, and accountability.

Here's what makes them interesting. Unlike most foundations that just write checks, the Democracy Fund tries to build entire fields. They identify gaps in the democracy protection ecosystem, find or create organizations to fill those gaps, provide multi-year funding, and help grantees coordinate with each other.

What They Actually Fund

Looking at their grants database, the Democracy Fund's portfolio breaks down into several categories:

Election administration support represents their largest bucket. They've been major funders of the Center for Tech and Civic Life (over $20 million since 2015), which provides technology and training to local election officials. They funded States United Democracy Center with approximately $5 million in 2021-2022 to protect election officials from intimidation and prevent certification obstruction—work that proved crucial during the 2022 midterms when certification refusals occurred in multiple states, as documented by ProPublica.

Voting rights litigation gets substantial support. According to their public filings, they've provided $12 million to the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law since 2016. They funded Campaign Legal Center with approximately $8 million over the same period for litigation defending voting access.

Research infrastructure includes funding for the MIT Election Lab, Stanford-MIT Healthy Elections Project, and the Brennan Center's election-related research programs. These grants typically run $500,000 to $2 million per year.

Field-building work means funding coordination organizations and networks. They provided seed funding for the Election Official Legal Defense Network (approximately $2 million in 2022-2023) after recognizing that intimidated election officials had nowhere to turn for legal support.

The Actual Track Record

Let me be specific about what Democracy Fund grants have accomplished, because "strengthening democracy" is pretty vague.

Their funding helped establish the infrastructure that kept 2020 and 2022 elections functioning under unprecedented pressure. The Center for Tech and Civic Life grants they funded helped over 2,500 local election offices improve their capacity, according to research by Stanford's Healthy Elections Project. When you read about election officials successfully defending certification processes in 2022, many had received training or legal support from Democracy Fund grantees.

Their litigation funding produced concrete wins. The Lawyers' Committee—a major Democracy Fund grantee—successfully blocked Virginia's last-minute voter purge in October 2024, as reported by NPR, preventing approximately 1,600 citizens from being wrongly removed from rolls just before the election.

But there are also clear limitations. Despite $250 million spent over nine years, key vulnerabilities remain unaddressed. There's still no systematic monitoring of county canvassing board appointments across swing states—a gap that became obvious during the 2022 certification crises. The Democracy Fund funds research on election subversion but not operational programs that would actually track or prevent it at the county level.

What They Don't Fund (And Why That Matters)

Here's where it gets interesting. Looking at what the Democracy Fund doesn't fund reveals strategic choices and philosophical constraints.

They don't fund directly partisan work. Their bipartisan commitment means they won't support organizations that explicitly oppose Republican election subversion efforts, even when those efforts are well-documented. This creates a gap: who funds work specifically focused on countering coordinated GOP certification obstruction?

They don't fund rapid-response operational work. The Democracy Fund makes grants in six-month to multi-year cycles. But many 2022 certification crises required same-week emergency responses—legal filings, emergency court orders, public pressure campaigns. According to States United's 2022 report, they responded to certification threats in real-time, but the Democracy Fund's grant structure makes it hard to fund that kind of work.

They don't fund state-level political organizing. The Democracy Fund supports nonpartisan civic engagement, but not efforts to defeat election-denying candidates or pass democracy reforms through ballot initiatives. That's someone else's job—but whose?

They largely don't fund technology development. Despite being founded by a tech entrepreneur, the Democracy Fund hasn't funded development of tools like AI-generated content detection systems, voter registration anomaly detection, or synthetic candidate verification platforms. They fund organizations that use technology, but not organizations that build it.

The Coordination Problem

Here's something I found surprising when researching this: the Democracy Fund runs a "Democracy Fund Network" that tries to coordinate their grantees, hosting regular convenings and sharing information between organizations working on elections.

This sounds bureaucratic, but it actually matters. In 2022, when Otero County, New Mexico refused to certify its primary results, multiple Democracy Fund grantees coordinated their response within 48 hours: States United provided legal analysis, the Brennan Center provided research on certification law, and local civic engagement groups mobilized public pressure. The county commissioners eventually certified after the New Mexico Supreme Court intervened.

Without that coordination infrastructure, each organization would have responded independently, probably less effectively. The Democracy Fund essentially built a rapid-response network by funding organizations that trust each other and can move quickly together.

But there's a darker side to this coordination model. When one funder supports most of the major players in a field, that funder's priorities and constraints become the field's priorities and constraints. The Democracy Fund's bipartisan commitment means most major election protection organizations are similarly constrained from calling out Republican-specific threats, even when evidence is overwhelming.

The Funding Sustainability Problem

The Democracy Fund operates primarily off its original $100 million endowment, which has grown through investment returns but also shrunk through grants. According to their IRS Form 990 for 2023, they had approximately $140 million in assets and distributed $28 million in grants that year.

At current spending levels, they can continue indefinitely. But if threats escalate—imagine needing to defend election certification in 50 counties simultaneously in 2028—the Democracy Fund doesn't have resources to dramatically scale up quickly.

More concerning: they're one of only three major funders in this space. The others are the Carnegie Corporation and the Ford Foundation. If any of these three funders shift priorities, huge parts of the democracy protection infrastructure lose support.

Compare this to the opposition landscape. Conservative legal organizations opposing voting access reforms have diversified funding from hundreds of donors, as documented by the Center for Media and Democracy. They can lose individual funders without institutional collapse.

What This Means for Someone Starting Projects

If you're thinking about starting a democracy protection project, here's what the Democracy Fund's existence means for you:

Don't duplicate existing grantee work. The Democracy Fund already funds election official support networks, voting rights litigation, and academic research. They're good at these things. Starting a competitor probably doesn't help.

Focus on gaps they can't or won't fill. Rapid operational response to local certification crises. State-level ballot initiative campaigns for democracy reforms. Technology development for election security. Partisan-but-evidence-based work calling out specific subversion attempts. The Democracy Fund's structure and philosophy prevent them from funding these areas effectively.

Think about funding sustainability differently. The Democracy Fund model—large endowment making grants to nonprofits—works for steady-state democracy protection. It doesn't work for rapidly scaling responses to acute threats. Consider models that can attract crisis funding: litigation funds that could grow quickly if certification fails in multiple states, mutual aid networks for threatened election officials, or technology platforms with revenue models.

Build relationships with existing grantees. The Democracy Fund's coordination network is valuable. If you're doing complementary work, connecting with their grantees gives you access to shared knowledge, established relationships with election officials, and coordinated response capabilities during crises.

The Bigger Question

Here's what really concerns me about the Democracy Fund model: it reveals how thin the actual funding base for democracy protection is.

One foundation, originally $100 million, is a major funder for protecting election integrity across the entire United States. That's not enough. Compare it to estimated spending by conservative legal organizations opposing voting access—the Heritage Foundation alone has a yearly budget over $100 million, much of which goes to election-related work.

The Democracy Fund has done important work and made strategic grants that built useful infrastructure. But philanthropy alone can't sustain democracy protection at the scale the threat requires. We need funding models that can scale rapidly, diversify beyond three major foundations, and support work those foundations won't touch.

Bottom Line & Project Ideas

The key takeaway: The Democracy Fund has successfully built coordination infrastructure and funded important election protection work, but its bipartisan constraints, slow grant cycles, and limited resources create systematic gaps in defending against partisan election subversion, rapid-response crises, and technological threats.

For project incubators: The highest-value projects are probably those the Democracy Fund structurally can't support—rapid-response legal funds for emergency certification crises, technology platforms for detecting AI-generated synthetic candidates or registration manipulation, and state-level ballot initiative campaigns for democracy reforms in swing states before 2028.

Existing organization worth supporting: States United Democracy Center is probably the Democracy Fund's most effective grantee for election subversion defense, but they're capacity-constrained and focused primarily on legal response rather than prevention. There's room for a complementary organization focused on proactive monitoring and early intervention before certification crises occur.

Funding gap to watch: Nobody is systematically funding technology development for election security. The Democracy Fund funds organizations that use technology, but not organizations that build verification systems, detection tools, or security infrastructure. This is probably the single biggest gap in the democracy protection funding landscape, and it's only getting more important as AI threats escalate.
